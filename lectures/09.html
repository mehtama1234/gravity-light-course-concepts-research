Lecture 09: Lecture 9: Newtonian spacetime is curved!

How does this lecture use Curvature, Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change, Ricci Curvature As Volume Change to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Turn falling into geometry: The course must explain free motion without saying that every path is judged against an invisible flat background. It needs its own rule for straightness and its own test for real gravitational curvature.

First-Principles Role

This lecture shows that the geometric reading of gravity is not just a later Einsteinian invention. By focusing on relative acceleration and curvature-like bookkeeping, it makes tidal behavior the durable fact behind older force language.

Starting Problem

This lecture presses on a surprising claim: even Newtonian gravity can be read as curvature when it is written with the right spacetime bookkeeping. The point is not to pretend Newton's theory is already general relativity. The point is to show that gravity is not captured by the force felt by one body alone. The more stable fact is how neighboring freely falling paths accelerate relative to each other. That relative motion is the bridge from an old gravity picture to the geometric one.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to read gravitational content through deviation of nearby free paths and through the curvature terms that control that deviation. A single falling observer can remove much of the local pull by choosing a falling frame. A family of nearby observers cannot remove the way their separations change. Ricci-type information then records part of that focusing or spreading. This prepares the reader to see Einstein gravity as a law about tidal geometry rather than a replacement word for force.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a small cloud of nearby freely falling test bodies.
  2. Ignore the motion of one body by itself and compare separations inside the cloud.
  3. Read relative acceleration as the gravitational fact that survives a falling frame.
  4. Connect that relative acceleration to curvature, especially the Ricci information used later.

Objects To Track

  • Curvature is the tensorial failure of covariant derivatives to commute, equivalently the failure of parallel transport around small loops to return a vector unchanged.
  • The Riemann curvature tensor records the failure of two covariant derivatives, taken in different orders, to give the same result.
  • Ricci curvature is a contraction of the full curvature tensor that captures focusing or spreading of nearby geodesic families.

Linked Concepts

Curvature

Two astronauts in free fall near each other will drift together or apart. Not because they push or pull, but because spacetime itself is shaped. You need a local way to measure that drifting—to say 'here, nearby motion diverges from what flat space would predict.'

Object: Curvature is the mismatch that arises when you try to compare directions at different places. Specifically, when two covariant derivatives (a tool for comparing neighboring directions) are applied in different orders, they give different results. That failure-to-commute is curvature, encoded in a Riemann tensor: a quantity that takes four direction-indices and produces a number saying how much the comparison went wrong.

Operation: You perform the curvature test by taking two covariant derivatives in opposite order and subtracting them—like moving east then north, then north then east, and checking if you end up in the same place. For gravity, you alternatively watch two nearby geodesics (free-fall paths): if they accelerate relative to each other, that relative acceleration directly measures the curvature.

Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change

Picture two friends standing close together on Earth's surface, both facing north. If they each walk 100 meters north, then 100 meters east, they end up at slightly different spots — the Earth's curvature got in the way. A mathematician needs a precise object that says: 'Taking steps in different orders leaves a mismatch.' That object is Riemann curvature.

Object: The Riemann curvature tensor is a machine built from the connection, a tool that tells you how to compare directions between nearby points. It measures: if you move a vector along a small closed loop, how much does it rotate by the time it gets back? More precisely, it captures the failure of two derivatives (covariant derivatives, a corrected way to differentiate respecting the curved geometry) taken in opposite orders to produce the same result.

Operation: You give the Riemann tensor three pieces of information: two different directions in which you want to move (call them X and Y), and a vector to carry along. The Riemann tensor then outputs: the extra twist or shift that arises from commuting those two moves. Algebraically, you compute the derivative along X of the derivative along Y, then subtract the derivative along Y of the derivative along X — the remainder is Riemann acting on your vector.

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Imagine a sphere: if you send nearby particles all in straight paths outward from the center, do they draw together or spread apart? The same question applies to spacetime itself. You need to measure whether paths that feel straight (geodesics—roads objects naturally follow when nothing pushes them) bunch closer or fan wider. That bundling or spreading is what Ricci curvature tracks.

Object: Ricci curvature is a simplified version of the full curvature object (the Riemann tensor—a list of numbers encoding how a tiny loop of displacement produces a net twist in any direction). To build Ricci, you take that full curvature tensor and perform a contraction: a summation that pairs up components in a specific way. The result is a smaller object—a rank-2 tensor, like a matrix—that captures only the part describing volume change of geodesic families.

Operation: To compute Ricci curvature, start with the full Riemann tensor (the complete twist-record). Pick one direction in spacetime, and sum up (contract) only the component pairs that measure how that direction changes. Do this for every possible direction. The result: a single number (or tensor component) for each spot on the manifold, telling you the net focusing or spreading.
