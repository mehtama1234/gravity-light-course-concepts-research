Lecture 28: Lecture 28: How to detect gravitational waves

How does this lecture use Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves, Strain As Relative Length Change, Interferometer Light As A Ruler to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Extract small signals: Exact solutions are rare, but real detectors measure small changes in realistic systems. The course needs controlled approximations that separate physical effects from coordinate changes and connect source motion to measurable detector strain.

First-Principles Role

This lecture closes the course by connecting metric disturbance to detector strain. The abstract measuring rule becomes a tiny relative length change read by light.

Starting Problem

The final detector lecture has to say what a gravitational-wave observation actually measures. It is not a photograph of spacetime rippling and not a force shoved onto detector parts. The measurable quantity is strain: a tiny relative change in length. An interferometer reads that change by comparing light travel in two arms. This closes the course loop because the same metric that defined light cones and proper time now becomes a small measured length change.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to connect metric perturbation to relative separation and then to optical phase comparison. A passing wave changes the proper distance relation between freely suspended test masses by a tiny fraction. Light in the interferometer arms compares those changes with extreme precision. The detector does not need an outside ruler untouched by gravity; it uses light and geometry together to read differential strain.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a small physical metric disturbance arriving at the detector.
  2. Translate that disturbance into relative length change between test masses.
  3. Measure the fractional change as strain, not as an absolute shove.
  4. Use light phase differences in the interferometer arms to read the strain.

Objects To Track

  • A perturbation is a controlled small change of the metric or matter fields around a background solution.
  • Strain is fractional length change: change in separation divided by the original separation.
  • An interferometer uses split laser light traveling along different arms as an extremely sensitive ruler.

Linked Concepts

Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves

Two stars orbit each other and gradually spiral inward. As they do, they send out ripples in spacetime itself—disturbances in the shape and curvature of space and time—and these ripples carry away energy. We need a way to study those ripples on their own, measure what energy they carry, and predict how instruments on Earth (like laser interferometers that measure tiny length changes) will detect them.

Object: A perturbation is a small correction to the metric—the rule that tells you distances and times. You write the metric as a background part (which you already know, like flat spacetime or the region around a single black hole) plus a tiny add-on term. This add-on must stay small everywhere, and its rates of change must also stay small.

Operation: You take Einstein's equations (the rules that connect spacetime curvature to matter and energy) and split them into two pieces: one piece describes the background spacetime, the other describes how the small correction evolves on top of that background. You then solve just the equation for the small correction, and you ignore any term where the correction multiplies by itself (because such products are tiny compared to linear terms).

Strain As Relative Length Change

Two long metal tubes sit in space, standing perpendicular to each other, with mirrors at their ends. A gravitational wave ripples through — spacetime itself flexes. The mirrors move closer together in one direction and farther apart in the other. You want to measure this motion, but the distances shift by amounts smaller than an atom. How do you notice something so impossibly small?

Object: Strain is a pure ratio — no units, just a number. It's the change in distance between two nearby points, divided by their starting distance. Example: two mirrors separated by 1000 meters. A wave shifts them to 1001 meters apart. The change is 1 meter. Divide by the original 1000 meters and get 0.001. That's the strain. It tells you the fractional change — how much the separation grew or shrank as a percentage of what it was.

Operation: Pick two reference points — say, the mirrors at each end of a tube. Measure how far apart they are right now. Call this L_old. Wait a moment. Measure the distance again, call it L_new. Calculate (L_new minus L_old) divided by L_old. That result is strain. It's the fractional shift in the separation.

Interferometer Light As A Ruler

A gravitational wave squeezes and stretches space itself—the actual shape of the space-and-time mesh. You need a way to measure how much that squeezing and stretching happens. A normal ruler won't work because the ruler is made of space too. When space stretches, the ruler stretches along with it. You end up trying to measure change using a tool that changes in exactly the same way.

Object: An interferometer uses light as a measuring stick. A laser fires toward a splitter that divides the beam into two identical copies. Each copy travels down one arm (each arm the same length), bounces off a mirror at the far end, and comes back. The two returning light waves carry information about how long each path was. If one arm grows longer, the light traveling there takes more time to make the round trip.

Operation: When the two light waves recombine, compare where they are in their cycles. If both paths are equal, the wave peaks arrive at the same moment—the waves reinforce each other, producing bright spots. If one path is longer, the waves' peaks and troughs no longer line up. They clash—some spots stay bright, others go dark. Count how many of these bright-dark bands (fringes) shift across your viewing screen. That count is your direct measure of the path-length difference.
