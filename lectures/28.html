Lecture 28: Lecture 28: How to detect gravitational waves

How does this lecture use Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves, Strain As Relative Length Change, Interferometer Light As A Ruler to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Extract small signals: Exact solutions are rare, but real detectors measure small changes in realistic systems. The course needs controlled approximations that separate physical effects from coordinate changes and connect source motion to measurable detector strain.

First-Principles Role

This lecture closes the course by connecting metric disturbance to detector strain. The abstract measuring rule becomes a tiny relative length change read by light.

Starting Problem

The final detector lecture has to say what a gravitational-wave observation actually measures. It is not a photograph of spacetime rippling and not a force shoved onto detector parts. The measurable quantity is strain: a tiny relative change in length. An interferometer reads that change by comparing light travel in two arms. This closes the course loop because the same metric that defined light cones and proper time now becomes a small measured length change.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to connect metric perturbation to relative separation and then to optical phase comparison. A passing wave changes the proper distance relation between freely suspended test masses by a tiny fraction. Light in the interferometer arms compares those changes with extreme precision. The detector does not need an outside ruler untouched by gravity; it uses light and geometry together to read differential strain.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a small physical metric disturbance arriving at the detector.
  2. Translate that disturbance into relative length change between test masses.
  3. Measure the fractional change as strain, not as an absolute shove.
  4. Use light phase differences in the interferometer arms to read the strain.

Objects To Track

  • A perturbation is a controlled small change of the metric or matter fields around a background solution.
  • Strain is fractional length change: change in separation divided by the original separation.
  • An interferometer uses split laser light traveling along different arms as an extremely sensitive ruler.

Linked Concepts

Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves

Spacetime around a binary star is nearly flat except for a tiny ripple carrying energy away. We need a way to study that small ripple separately from the flat background, measure what it carries, and predict how instruments on Earth will see it.

Object: A perturbation is a metric — the rule for distances — written as a background part (flat or a known solution) plus a small correction term. The correction is controlled: it stays small everywhere, and its derivatives are also small.

Operation: You split the Einstein equations into two parts: one telling you about the background, one telling you how the small correction evolves. Then you solve the small-correction equation alone, ignoring terms where the correction multiplies itself.

Strain As Relative Length Change

Imagine two long, straight metal tubes held in space very carefully, perpendicular to each other, with mirrors at the ends. When a gravitational wave passes through, the distances between the mirrors shift — one gets slightly longer, one slightly shorter. How do you measure something so tiny that the change is far smaller than an atom?

Object: Strain is a dimensionless ratio: the change in separation between two points, divided by the original separation. If two mirrors were 1000 meters apart and the separation shifts by 1 meter, the strain is 1 divided by 1000, or 0.001. It's a pure number with no units, telling you the fractional change.

Operation: Take two reference points (the mirrors). Measure their distance apart at one moment in time. Measure the distance again a short time later. Subtract: new distance minus old distance. Divide by the original distance. The result is strain. It compares the two measurements to yield a pure ratio.

Interferometer Light As A Ruler

A gravitational wave squeezes and stretches space itself. We need a way to measure how much—to turn the invisible wiggle in the metric into a number an experiment can record. A mechanical ruler won't work: the stretching is too small and happens to the space the ruler sits in, not the ruler separately.

Object: An interferometer is a light-based ruler. A laser beam splits into two paths of equal length, travels down each arm, reflects, and recombines. The two light waves that return carry a record of how long each path took. If one arm stretches, the light traveling there arrives out of step with the light from the other arm—they no longer match crest-to-crest.

Operation: Measure the phase difference between the two recombined light waves. A phase difference means the waves do not line up perfectly; they interfere—some spots bright, some dark. Count the fringes or measure the shift in the fringe pattern. That shift directly tells you how much one arm grew relative to the other.
