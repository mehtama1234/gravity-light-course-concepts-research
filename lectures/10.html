Lecture 10: Lecture 10: Metric Manifolds

How does this lecture use Metric Manifolds, Metric Signature And Light Cones, Proper Time As Clock Reading to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Turn falling into geometry: The course must explain free motion without saying that every path is judged against an invisible flat background. It needs its own rule for straightness and its own test for real gravitational curvature.

First-Principles Role

This lecture gives the course its measuring field. The metric turns tangent directions into intervals, light cones, and proper time, so geometry becomes something clocks, rulers, and light signals can read.

Starting Problem

The course has built topology, charts, tangent spaces, and comparison rules, but none of that yet says what a clock reads or which directions light can take. This lecture introduces measurement as part of the geometry. The metric is not a background ruler placed on spacetime from outside. It is the local rule that turns tangent directions into intervals, separates time-like from space-like from light-like, and lets proper time become a path-dependent clock reading.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to make measurement a field. At each point, the metric takes tangent directions as inputs and returns interval information. Because the metric has Lorentzian signature in spacetime, it does more than measure length: it sorts directions into causal types. A zero interval direction is not a zero direction; it is a light direction. A time-like path can be integrated to get proper time. This turns abstract tangent geometry into physical readings.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Begin with tangent directions that have not yet been measured.
  2. Apply the metric to a direction and itself to get its interval type.
  3. Use the sign and zero cases to draw the local light cone.
  4. Add metric clock readings along a time-like path to get proper time.

Objects To Track

  • A metric is a smoothly varying inner product on tangent spaces, with Lorentzian signature in spacetime so that time and space enter differently.
  • Lorentzian signature is the metric's time-space sign pattern, and light cones are the resulting boundaries of causal influence.
  • Proper time is the time measured along a time-like path by integrating the metric interval along that path.

Linked Concepts

Metric Manifolds

Imagine laying a path on a curved hillside. You can point to which spots are next to each other, but you cannot yet answer: how far apart? How fast does something move between them? Which paths can light take? To answer these, you need a rule—a measurement device that works at every tiny piece of the surface and tells you the distance and time interval for any small step.

Object: A metric is a machine at every point in space and time that takes two infinitesimal displacements—two tiny arrows showing direction and magnitude—and returns a single number: their inner product, the generalization of a dot product. On a sphere the metric is Euclidean (flat geometry rules): if you give it two unit-length perpendicular arrows, it returns 1. In spacetime the metric is Lorentzian (relativity rules): a timelike and a spacelike arrow can return zero, meaning they are perpendicular in the relativistic sense.

Operation: To use a metric: pick a point and choose two infinitesimal displacements—directions and magnitudes—at that point. Put them into the metric machine. It outputs a number. That number is the inner product; it is also the square of the distance between those two displacements (in spacetime, this number can be negative, zero, or positive, and the sign tells you whether the displacements point in a timelike, lightlike, or spacelike direction).

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

Object: A Lorentzian metric is a measurement table that lives at each point of spacetime. It takes any tiny displacement (a shift in time and space) and computes an interval—a signed number, not always positive. Unlike a ruler in flat space (where distances are always positive), this metric has one time direction that counts as negative and three space directions that count as positive. That pattern of signs is called the signature, and it is what makes light and causality work.

Operation: Take two nearby events and write their separation as a vector: (change in time, change in x, change in y, change in z). The metric is a table of numbers (a matrix). Multiply the vector by the matrix, following the rules of that operation. The result is the squared interval—a single signed number. If it is negative, the separation is timelike, meaning one event could have caused the other. If it is positive, the separation is spacelike, meaning they are far apart and cannot influence each other. If it is exactly zero, the separation is null, and a light beam travels from one event to the other.

Proper Time As Clock Reading

Two people set off on journeys through space and time, each with a wristwatch. They meet years later. Their watches show different amounts of elapsed time. The question: how do we describe and measure what each clock actually counted?

Object: Proper time is a number attached to each path through spacetime. You compute it by taking the metric (the rule for measuring distance-time intervals) at each infinitesimal step of the path, applying it to the direction the path is heading, and adding all those tiny contributions from start to finish. The Lorentzian metric weights space and time directions separately to account for gravity.

Operation: To find proper time along a path: at each tiny step forward, use the metric to measure the infinitesimal distance-time interval; multiply that interval by the direction the path is moving; then add up all these products from beginning to end. Like counting heartbeats as you walk—each step contributes a small amount to the total.
