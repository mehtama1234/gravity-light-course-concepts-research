Lecture 10: Lecture 10: Metric Manifolds

How does this lecture use Metric Manifolds, Metric Signature And Light Cones, Proper Time As Clock Reading to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Turn falling into geometry: The course must explain free motion without saying that every path is judged against an invisible flat background. It needs its own rule for straightness and its own test for real gravitational curvature.

First-Principles Role

This lecture gives the course its measuring field. The metric turns tangent directions into intervals, light cones, and proper time, so geometry becomes something clocks, rulers, and light signals can read.

Starting Problem

The course has built topology, charts, tangent spaces, and comparison rules, but none of that yet says what a clock reads or which directions light can take. This lecture introduces measurement as part of the geometry. The metric is not a background ruler placed on spacetime from outside. It is the local rule that turns tangent directions into intervals, separates time-like from space-like from light-like, and lets proper time become a path-dependent clock reading.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to make measurement a field. At each point, the metric takes tangent directions as inputs and returns interval information. Because the metric has Lorentzian signature in spacetime, it does more than measure length: it sorts directions into causal types. A zero interval direction is not a zero direction; it is a light direction. A time-like path can be integrated to get proper time. This turns abstract tangent geometry into physical readings.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Begin with tangent directions that have not yet been measured.
  2. Apply the metric to a direction and itself to get its interval type.
  3. Use the sign and zero cases to draw the local light cone.
  4. Add metric clock readings along a time-like path to get proper time.

Objects To Track

  • A metric is a smoothly varying inner product on tangent spaces, with Lorentzian signature in spacetime so that time and space enter differently.
  • Lorentzian signature is the metric's time-space sign pattern, and light cones are the resulting boundaries of causal influence.
  • Proper time is the time measured along a time-like path by integrating the metric interval along that path.

Linked Concepts

Metric Manifolds

Imagine laying a path on a curved hillside. You can describe which spots are near each other, but you cannot yet say how far apart they are, how fast something travels between them, or which paths a light ray can take. You need a rule that assigns a measurement—a notion of distance and time interval—to every tiny step on the surface.

Object: A metric is a machine (mathematicians call it a tensor field, a smoothly varying function) that takes any two tiny displacements at a point and returns a number—their interval, or dot product. On a sphere it is Euclidean: two orthogonal vectors of lengths 1 have interval 1. In spacetime the metric is Lorentzian: a time-like and space-like vector can have zero interval, meaning they are perpendicular in a relativistic sense.

Operation: To use a metric: choose a point and two tangent vectors (infinitesimal displacements) at that point. Feed them into the metric. Out comes a number. That number is the dot product, or equivalently, the square of the distance between those two displacements (in spacetime, it can be negative, zero, or positive, and that sign tells you whether they are time-like, light-like, or space-like).

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

Object: A Lorentzian metric is a measurement tool that assigns a signed interval to any infinitesimal displacement. Unlike a Euclidean metric (where the square of a distance is always positive), a Lorentzian metric has one negative direction (time) and three positive directions (space). The signature—the count of signs—is what makes spacetime causal and light possible.

Operation: To use the metric: write down two nearby events' separation as a vector in spacetime. Apply the metric (a matrix of numbers) to that vector. The result—the squared interval—tells you the separation's type. If the squared interval is negative, the separation is timelike (one event might cause the other). If positive, it's spacelike (they're far apart in space, causally disconnected). If zero, it's null or lightlike (light travels this path exactly).

Proper Time As Clock Reading

Two people set off on a journey through space and time, each wearing a wristwatch. They meet again years later. Their watches show different elapsed times. How do we describe what each clock actually counted?

Object: Proper time is a quantity attached to a path through spacetime: you sum up the infinitesimal metric intervals (the tiny spacetime distances) all along that path using a Lorentzian metric, which accounts for both space and time directions weighted by the gravitational field.

Operation: To find proper time on a path, you take the metric at each tiny step forward on that path, apply it to the path's direction vector, collect those results, and add them up from start to finish—like counting heartbeats as you walk.
