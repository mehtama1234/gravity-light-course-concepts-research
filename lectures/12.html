Lecture 12: Lecture 12: Integration on manifolds

How does this lecture use Integration on Manifolds, Stokes' Theorem As Boundary-Bulk Accounting to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Make laws coordinate-free: Once geometry exists, the course needs laws that can be stated without picking a favorite observer or map. It also needs a precise object for what matter contributes to gravity.

First-Principles Role

This lecture turns integration into chart-independent accounting. It explains how local quantities can be added over curved regions and how boundary terms are tied to bulk behavior.

Starting Problem

The course needs to add local facts over curved regions without letting the answer depend on the chart used for the addition. Ordinary integration can hide its dependence on x-axes and coordinate cells. On a manifold, a region may need many charts, and a chart change can stretch or flip the coordinate grid. This lecture makes integration into a coordinate-independence problem: the thing being integrated must carry the correction needed for all charts to agree.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to use forms and orientation-aware volume elements. When coordinates change, the size of a coordinate cell changes by a determinant factor. The form transforms with the compensating factor, so the final total belongs to the region and the form, not to the grid. Stokes' theorem then gives the same accounting in another direction: what happens across the boundary is tied to what happens in the bulk.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a local quantity that must be added over a curved region.
  2. Change charts and track how the coordinate cell size changes.
  3. Use a form whose transformation cancels that chart-change factor.
  4. Relate boundary accounting to bulk accounting through Stokes' theorem for the same region.

Objects To Track

  • Differential forms and volume elements package what can be integrated in a coordinate-independent way.
  • Stokes' theorem says the integral of a derivative-like form over a region equals the integral of the original form over the boundary.

Linked Concepts

Integration on Manifolds

You want to add up quantities spread across a curved surface—rainfall over a country, the charge in a region of space, the mass in a galaxy—without your answer depending on which coordinate system you picked. The earth is curved, but you need one reliable total.

Object: A differential form is a recipe that tells you, at every point on your manifold, how to multiply infinitesimal displacements together to get a number. A volume form is the special differential form that, when integrated, gives you the volume of any region—and it is the same for all observers.

Operation: To integrate a differential form over a curved region, you pull the form back into a coordinate chart (your local flat picture), perform an ordinary integral there, and glue the pieces together using the fact that differential forms automatically correct for chart overlaps.

Stokes' Theorem As Boundary-Bulk Accounting

You have a region of space—say, a ball of gas, or a patch of spacetime. Something is flowing through it: energy, particles, momentum. You want to know the total amount in the region. One way: look inside, measure everything. Another way: stand at the surface and count what crosses the boundary. These two must agree if the accounting is honest.

Object: A differential form (a machine that takes vectors at each point and produces numbers) is the object Stokes' theorem describes. When you take a derivative of such a machine (the exterior derivative, a rule for how it changes as you move), you get a new form. Stokes says: integrating that new form over a region equals integrating the original form over the region's boundary surface.

Operation: You start with a form (like a density or a flux). You compute its exterior derivative (how it varies). Instead of integrating the derivative throughout the bulk, you integrate the original form only on the boundary. The two integrals are equal—this is the swap.
