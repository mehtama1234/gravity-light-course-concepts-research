Lecture 19: Lecture 19: Canonical Formulation of GR II

How does this lecture use Canonical Formulation and Constraints, Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Solve evolution and global shape: The course then asks how whole spacetimes behave: how valid initial data evolves, how the universe expands, and how causal boundaries such as horizons are understood.

First-Principles Role

This lecture deepens the canonical view by separating real evolution from choices of slicing and description. It keeps coordinate freedom visible inside the initial-value formulation.

Starting Problem

The second canonical lecture deepens the same issue: evolution in general relativity is not just time marching on a fixed stage. The split into slices introduces choices about lapse, shift, and coordinates, and those choices can move the description without changing the physical spacetime. The reader has to distinguish genuine evolution of geometry from a changed slicing or labeling of the same geometry, while keeping the constraints satisfied throughout the motion.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to treat constraints and gauge freedom as part of the dynamics. The canonical variables cannot be chosen independently, and their evolution must preserve the constraints. This is why the Hamiltonian-looking formulation of general relativity is not ordinary mechanics with more variables. Some apparent motion is the freedom to describe spacetime with different slices, and the equations must respect that freedom.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Begin with slice geometry and momentum-like data that already satisfy the constraints.
  2. Choose lapse and shift as ways of describing how slices are related.
  3. Evolve the data while checking that the constraints remain satisfied.
  4. Separate physical change in geometry from a changed slicing of the same spacetime.

Objects To Track

  • The canonical formulation rewrites spacetime geometry as slice geometry plus momentum-like data, constrained by the same physics in a different language.
  • Initial data consists of spatial geometry plus momentum-like change data that obeys Hamiltonian and momentum constraints.

Linked Concepts

Canonical Formulation and Constraints

Imagine filming a soap bubble as it swells. Each frame captures the bubble's shape at one moment, but the film alone does not tell you what the bubble will do next. You need two pieces of information: what it looks like right now, and how fast it is changing. General relativity runs into the same puzzle with spacetime. Spacetime has no universal clock ticking everywhere at the same rate, so we cannot simply say "here is the geometry now, here is how fast it is changing—go compute the future." We must build a language that does not assume a natural time-flow.

Object: The canonical formulation rewrites the Einstein field equations into a form suited to evolution. It breaks the four-dimensional spacetime metric into pieces: a three-dimensional metric h that lives on a spatial slice (it measures distances within that slice), a lapse function α (a number at each point that controls how fast time passes at that location), and a shift vector β (a direction that shows how the spatial grid tilts or slides as you step forward in time). This repackaging turns the problem into one that looks like a dynamical system: it has degrees of freedom (h and a momentum-like field conjugate to h) and algebraic constraints these degrees of freedom must satisfy at each instant.

Operation: To use this formulation, start by proposing a three-dimensional metric h on your spatial slice and a momentum field. Then check whether they obey two constraint equations (the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint). These constraints are not differential equations—they are algebraic relations that must hold at each moment. If your h and momentum satisfy both constraints, the evolution equations (which are differential equations) tell you exactly how h and momentum change as you advance to the next slice. The lapse α and shift β are not determined by the equations; you are free to choose them. This freedom is precisely the gauge freedom—the freedom to pick your coordinates.

Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints

You want to predict how spacetime will change going forward. But here is the catch: not every picture of space you can draw right now is one that could actually exist at the start of a real spacetime. Before you let evolution equations work on a snapshot, something has to check whether that snapshot is allowed by Einstein's rules.

Object: Initial data is two pieces stuck together: first, the shape of one thin slice of space at one moment (a spatial metric—the measuring rule for distances on that slice), and second, the squeeze-and-stretch of that space as it begins to change (extrinsic curvature—how much space is bending as time moves forward). These two pieces must pass a two-part check: the Hamiltonian constraint (linking space's bend to the energy sitting in it) and the momentum constraint (linking space's bend to the momentum sitting in it).

Operation: Before you feed a snapshot into the evolution equations, you run it through two tests. Does the shape of space and its rate of change satisfy the Hamiltonian constraint everywhere on that slice? Does it satisfy the momentum constraint everywhere? If both pass, the data is lawful and you may evolve. If either fails anywhere, that snapshot cannot be the start of any real spacetime.
