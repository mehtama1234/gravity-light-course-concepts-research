Lecture 17: Lecture 17: Optical Geometry II

How does this lecture use Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics, Gravitational Lensing And Focusing to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Read spacetime with light: The course needs a bridge from equations to observation. Light is the bridge because it follows the causal structure set by the metric and carries information to detectors.

First-Principles Role

This lecture extends optical geometry from one light path to families of paths. It shows how focusing, lensing, and image distortion turn spacetime curvature into received astronomical evidence.

Starting Problem

The second optical-geometry lecture asks what happens when light is not treated one ray at a time. Real observation depends on families of light paths: neighboring rays can focus, spread, arrive late, or make several images of the same source. The everyday picture of gravity tugging on photons misses the important structure. The metric shapes the whole bundle of null geodesics, and the pattern seen by an observer is a record of that geometry.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to compare neighboring null geodesics. A single light path tells where one signal can travel; a family of paths tells how images, brightness, and arrival times change. Curvature enters through focusing and deviation, while the metric keeps the paths null. This is why lensing is not an observational side story. It is a direct way spacetime geometry changes the map between a source and what a telescope receives.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with several nearby light rays leaving the same source region.
  2. Keep each ray null and geodesic as it crosses the curved spacetime.
  3. Compare how the rays focus, spread, delay, or cross before reaching an observer.
  4. Read the changed image pattern as evidence for the geometry between source and observer.

Objects To Track

  • Null geodesics are curves whose tangent has zero metric length and is parallel transported along itself.
  • Lensing is the bending and focusing of families of null geodesics by spacetime curvature.

Linked Concepts

Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics

Watch starlight bend as it passes the Sun, or see light frozen just outside a black hole. That bending is not gravity yanking the light sideways like a magnet pulls iron. Instead, the light is following a path that the curved shape of space and time itself creates. We need words to describe 'which paths does light actually travel through a warped world?'

Object: A null geodesic is a curve snaking through spacetime where two things happen together. First: the tangent vector—an arrow showing the direction you're moving and how fast—has zero length when you measure it using the metric. (That zero-length property is called 'null' and marks light's path, not any other type of motion.) Second: as you move along the curve, your direction stays constant according to the space's curvature rules—a property called parallel transport. These two rules lock down exactly which curves light can take.

Operation: To find where a light ray goes, you write down the geodesic equation—the mathematical rule for finding the 'straightest' paths in a curved space. Then you add the null condition: the metric-measured length of the path must equal zero. Solve both equations together and you get the curve. Once you know the curve, you can compare two nearby light rays and see whether they come together (focusing, like a lens) or spread apart (defocusing).

Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

Look at a distant galaxy through a telescope. Between you and it sits another galaxy so massive that the space and time around it are bent. When light from the far galaxy travels toward you, it passes through this warped region. The bent space steers the light off its straight path. You see the distant galaxy in the wrong spot in the sky. Sometimes it looks magnified—brighter and larger. Sometimes it splits into two or three images of the same galaxy. We need a way to understand this bending without pretending gravity is a force pulling on light.

Object: A bundle of light rays traveling close together and nearly parallel is a single object called a congruence of null geodesics. In everyday language: a bundle (meaning many rays packed tight), of null (meaning light-speed paths), geodesics (meaning the straightest paths available). At any moment in space and time, you take a small patch of rays all pointing nearly the same way. That patch is one congruence. The congruence is the full rule: it tells you at every nearby spot in space-time, which direction a light ray points. Curved space and time act on the entire bundle at once. The bundle can get squeezed, stretched, tilted, or twisted by the curvature.

Operation: Take your bundle of light rays and imagine moving perpendicular to them by a tiny distance. Watch three things: Does the cross-sectional area shrink or grow? Do the rays' directions shift relative to each other? Are the rays spinning as a group? You are not watching single rays. You are watching how the whole bundle transforms. These three changes have names: expansion (area change), shear (directional shifts), and twist (group rotation).
