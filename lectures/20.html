Lecture 20: Lecture 20: Cosmology - The early epoch

How does this lecture use Cosmology and Expansion, Scale Factor As Expansion History to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Solve evolution and global shape: The course then asks how whole spacetimes behave: how valid initial data evolves, how the universe expands, and how causal boundaries such as horizons are understood.

First-Principles Role

This lecture applies Einstein gravity to the early universe by using large-scale symmetry. The scale factor becomes the readable measuring rule for early expansion.

Starting Problem

Early-universe cosmology asks how Einstein's equation behaves when the universe is hot, dense, and simplified by large-scale sameness. The point is not to track every galaxy or local lump. The point is to use symmetry to reduce the enormous field equation to a few quantities that describe the whole model. The scale factor becomes the main measuring rule: it says how distances between comoving points change with cosmic time.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to impose homogeneity and isotropy, then read the field equation through the scale factor and matter content. Symmetry removes many possible details because the model declares no preferred large-scale place or direction. What remains is a time-dependent geometry whose expansion is tied to density, pressure, and curvature assumptions. The early epoch is then studied by asking how those ingredients drive the scale factor backward or forward.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start by assuming the large-scale universe has no preferred place or direction.
  2. Reduce the metric to a form controlled mainly by the scale factor.
  3. Use the reduced field equation to connect expansion with matter content.
  4. Read the early epoch through how the scale factor behaves at high density.

Objects To Track

  • A cosmological model uses symmetry assumptions plus a scale factor to reduce Einstein's equation to equations for large-scale evolution.
  • The scale factor is the time-dependent multiplier that converts fixed comoving separations into physical separations.

Linked Concepts

Cosmology and Expansion

When you write down Einstein's equation for gravity, it describes how curved space-and-time changes in response to matter and energy. To use it for the whole universe, you would need to know the exact arrangement of every galaxy, star, and atom — an infinite amount of detail. Physicists needed a shortcut: a simpler rule that captures the universe's large-scale behavior without tracking everything.

Object: The scale factor is a single number written a(t) that depends only on time. Imagine it as a stretching factor: multiply it by every distance in space at each moment. When a(t) grows, all distances grow by the same fraction. When it shrinks, they shrink together. The full Einstein equation — which in principle tracks the shape of space at every point and how it bends — becomes a single rule: how does a(t) change with time? That is a radical simplification.

Operation: Start by assuming two things: the universe looks the same in every direction (if you are at any point and look around, there is no special direction), and it looks the same everywhere you go (no location is special compared to another). These assumptions are roughly true when you step back far enough. Under these conditions, Einstein's full equation simplifies to an equation for a(t) alone. Solve that equation, and you have the cosmic expansion history encoded in one curve.

Scale Factor As Expansion History

Imagine you scatter thousands of dots across a piece of rubber, each dot a galaxy. As the rubber stretches, every dot moves away from every other dot. You want one number—call it a—that changes with time and tells you how much that stretching has happened at any moment. That single number should capture the whole story of how distances in the universe change.

Object: The scale factor a(t) is a single number that changes with time. It measures how much every distance in space has been stretched since some starting moment. Imagine two galaxies locked in place on a cosmic grid—like latitude and longitude, but for spacetime. Their raw grid separation never changes. But the true distance between them—measured in actual miles or kilometers—is that grid separation multiplied by a(t). When a(t) = 1, the grid distance equals the true distance. When a(t) = 2, the true distance is twice the grid separation. As a(t) grows, the same grid positions mean objects are farther apart.

Operation: To use the scale factor, you feed it into Einstein's equations—the rules that link how spacetime curves to how much matter and energy live inside. These equations become recipes for how a(t) changes over time. You tell the equations what matter fills the universe (how dense it is, how much pressure it exerts), and the equations spit out a solution: a(t) for every moment in history. That solution is cosmology—the full story of how the universe expanded or contracted.
