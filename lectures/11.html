Lecture 11: Lecture 11: Symmetry

How does this lecture use Symmetry and Conservation, Killing Fields As Symmetry Directions to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Make laws coordinate-free: Once geometry exists, the course needs laws that can be stated without picking a favorite observer or map. It also needs a precise object for what matter contributes to gravity.

First-Principles Role

This lecture makes symmetry operational. It teaches that conserved quantities come from preserved geometric structure, not from a visual impression that a diagram looks unchanged.

Starting Problem

Symmetry matters only when it preserves the structure the theory actually cares about. A picture may look balanced, but that does not guarantee a conserved quantity or a useful simplification. This lecture asks how to test sameness in spacetime. The relevant question is whether a flow or transformation changes the metric and other geometric data. If the structure is truly preserved, the geometry has a direction of sameness that later can support conserved quantities.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to replace visual symmetry with an invariance test. Move points along a candidate flow and compare the geometric data before and after that move. If the metric stays unchanged, the flow is a Killing symmetry. This is why conservation is not assumed globally in every spacetime. Energy-like or angular-momentum-like quantities need the geometry to provide the right kind of preserved direction first.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a proposed motion through the spacetime, such as time shift or rotation.
  2. Move the metric along that flow and compare it with the original metric.
  3. Call the flow a symmetry only when the metric is unchanged.
  4. Use that preserved structure to justify the conserved quantity associated with the motion.

Objects To Track

  • A symmetry is a structure-preserving map or flow, often expressed by a vector field whose motion leaves the metric unchanged.
  • A Killing field is a vector field whose flow leaves the metric unchanged.

Linked Concepts

Symmetry and Conservation

When you study any physical system, you search for quantities that never change—conserved ones. A sphere stays the same shape when you rotate it. In spacetime near a black hole, if the geometry looks the same in all directions, what stays unchanged when you apply that sameness as a transformation?

Object: A symmetry is a vector field—an arrow assigned smoothly to every point in spacetime—whose flow (motion along those arrows) keeps the metric (the measuring rule) unchanged. Picture a smooth current flowing through spacetime that never warps the distances or time intervals you would measure.

Operation: To check if a vector field is a symmetry, you compute its Lie derivative acting on the metric: you watch how the metric shifts as you flow along the field. Zero shift means the field is a symmetry; the measuring rule stays put.

Killing Fields As Symmetry Directions

Imagine a spinning merry-go-round where every seat looks identical as you turn it. Or a straight train track that looks the same no matter where you stand on it. You want to say: 'This geometry has a symmetry.' But how do you write that down as a direction in space, so the math can grab it and use it?

Object: A Killing field is a direction of flow in spacetime (imagine an arrow pointing from each location, showing a direction and speed) with one defining property: if you move along it in infinitesimal steps, the metric—the rule measuring distances and time intervals—does not change. It's a path direction that leaves all geometric measurements untouched.

Operation: To check if a direction (a vector field) is a Killing field, you compute how the metric changes as you step along that direction. If the change is zero everywhere, you found a Killing field. The recipe is: write the metric down, take its rate of change along the direction, and check that the result is zero.
