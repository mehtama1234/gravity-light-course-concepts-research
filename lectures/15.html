Lecture 15: Lecture 15: Einstein Gravity

How does this lecture use Matter and Stress-Energy, Einstein Equation, Ricci Curvature As Volume Change, Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry, Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Make laws coordinate-free: Once geometry exists, the course needs laws that can be stated without picking a favorite observer or map. It also needs a precise object for what matter contributes to gravity.

First-Principles Role

This lecture joins the course's two main accounts: curvature-based geometry and stress-energy matter. The Einstein equation appears as a constrained tensor balance, not a slogan.

Starting Problem

Einstein gravity is the course's central balance, and it has to be sharper than the slogan that matter curves spacetime. The geometry side must be a tensor built from curvature and the metric. The matter side must be a tensor that records local energy and momentum accounting. The two sides must also match in conservation behavior. This lecture is where the earlier machinery is forced to fit: curvature, metric measurement, stress-energy, and covariant bookkeeping become one law.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to build the Einstein tensor from Ricci curvature, scalar curvature, and the metric so that its covariant divergence vanishes. That built-in conservation property is why it can be equated to stress-energy. The equation is difficult because the metric being solved for is also used to build the curvature on the left side. The cosmological constant then appears as an allowed uniform term in the same balance, not as an unrelated add-on.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with stress-energy as the local matter accounting object.
  2. Compress curvature into the Einstein tensor on the geometry side.
  3. Use the shared covariant conservation behavior to justify the balance.
  4. Recognize that solving the equation means solving for the metric that also builds the left side.

Objects To Track

  • The stress-energy tensor is the object that measures energy density, momentum flow, pressure, and stress through chosen directions or surfaces.
  • The Einstein tensor is built from curvature and the metric so that its covariant divergence vanishes, matching local conservation of stress-energy.
  • Ricci curvature is a contraction of the full curvature tensor that captures focusing or spreading of nearby geodesic families.
  • The Einstein tensor is the curvature combination whose covariant divergence vanishes automatically.
  • The cosmological constant is a constant term in Einstein's equation, often read as a vacuum energy contribution.

Linked Concepts

Matter and Stress-Energy

Gravity must account for what makes matter curve spacetime: not just mass, but energy, momentum, pressure, and internal stresses all push back on geometry. Newton thought mass was enough, but relativity reveals energy and momentum are faces of one unified object.

Object: The stress-energy tensor is a 4×4 table where each entry records a flow: energy flowing in time (top-left), momentum flowing through space (top-right block), pressure and stress between spatial directions (bottom-right 3×3 block). Rows ask 'what kind of stuff,' columns ask 'flowing which direction.'

Operation: Multiply the tensor entries by direction vectors representing an observer or surface normal. The result tells you the energy density that observer measures, the momentum flux through a surface, or the internal stress between regions.

Einstein Equation

We can see that gravity bends the paths of planets and light, and we suspect that this bending is really the shape of space and time. But seeing is not a law. We need a precise rule that says exactly how much the presence of matter and energy warps the geometry around it, and how that warping in turn steers everything moving through it.

Object: The central object is the Einstein tensor, written G. It is a specific recipe built out of the curvature of spacetime that measures, at each point, how the volume of a small cloud of free-falling particles starts to shrink. It is carefully assembled so that a particular running-total of it never changes as you move around, which is the geometric echo of a physical fact: energy and momentum are never created or destroyed, only moved.

Operation: You place the matter and energy of a region into a bookkeeping object called the stress-energy tensor, T, which lists the energy, the momentum, and the pressures present. You set the geometry side, G, equal to a fixed constant times T. Then you solve for the metric, the object that encodes all distances and times, that makes both sides agree everywhere at once.

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Imagine a sphere: if you send nearby particles all in straight paths outward from the center, do they draw together or spread apart? The same question applies to spacetime itself. You need to measure whether paths that feel straight (geodesics—roads objects naturally follow when nothing pushes them) bunch closer or fan wider. That bundling or spreading is what Ricci curvature tracks.

Object: Ricci curvature is a simplified version of the full curvature object (the Riemann tensor—a list of numbers encoding how a tiny loop of displacement produces a net twist in any direction). To build Ricci, you take that full curvature tensor and perform a contraction: a summation that pairs up components in a specific way. The result is a smaller object—a rank-2 tensor, like a matrix—that captures only the part describing volume change of geodesic families.

Operation: To compute Ricci curvature, start with the full Riemann tensor (the complete twist-record). Pick one direction in spacetime, and sum up (contract) only the component pairs that measure how that direction changes. Do this for every possible direction. The result: a single number (or tensor component) for each spot on the manifold, telling you the net focusing or spreading.

Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry

Imagine a rubber sheet with weights on it; the weights push down and the rubber curves. If you write an equation balancing the two sides—weights on the right, curvature on the left—both must follow the same accounting rules. Matter doesn't vanish or appear; it's conserved locally. Geometry must obey the same bookkeeping, or the equation is nonsense.

Object: The Einstein tensor is built by taking the Ricci tensor (which measures local bending), subtracting half the scalar curvature (average bend) times the metric (a measure of distances), and combining them so their divergence vanishes identically. It's a recipe that guarantees zero leak.

Operation: To compute it: measure the Ricci curvature at a point. Multiply the scalar curvature by the metric, then halve it. Subtract that from Ricci. The result is the Einstein tensor. Taking its divergence—summing changes in each direction—always gives zero, no matter what spacetime you're in.

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Imagine weighing all the matter in the universe—galaxies, gas, everything we can see. When physicists do this and ask whether the universe should be expanding faster or slower, the numbers don't match what telescopes show. Something else is pushing the expansion. That something is invisible and doesn't clump into galaxies like ordinary matter does.

Object: The cosmological constant is a single number, often written as the Greek letter Lambda (Λ). Unlike matter, which clumps and changes from place to place, this constant is the same everywhere and at every time. In the field equation, it acts as a built-in contribution to spacetime's curvature, as if spacetime itself carries a tiny, uniform energy.

Operation: You add the term into Einstein's field equation: on the left side (the geometry side), you append a term proportional to Λ. Then you solve the modified equation to see how the universe expands. The constant sits there, unchanging, pulling the expansion apart uniformly in all directions.
