Lecture 15: Lecture 15: Einstein Gravity

How does this lecture use Matter and Stress-Energy, Einstein Equation, Ricci Curvature As Volume Change, Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry, Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Make laws coordinate-free: Once geometry exists, the course needs laws that can be stated without picking a favorite observer or map. It also needs a precise object for what matter contributes to gravity.

First-Principles Role

This lecture joins the course's two main accounts: curvature-based geometry and stress-energy matter. The Einstein equation appears as a constrained tensor balance, not a slogan.

Starting Problem

Einstein gravity is the course's central balance, and it has to be sharper than the slogan that matter curves spacetime. The geometry side must be a tensor built from curvature and the metric. The matter side must be a tensor that records local energy and momentum accounting. The two sides must also match in conservation behavior. This lecture is where the earlier machinery is forced to fit: curvature, metric measurement, stress-energy, and covariant bookkeeping become one law.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to build the Einstein tensor from Ricci curvature, scalar curvature, and the metric so that its covariant divergence vanishes. That built-in conservation property is why it can be equated to stress-energy. The equation is difficult because the metric being solved for is also used to build the curvature on the left side. The cosmological constant then appears as an allowed uniform term in the same balance, not as an unrelated add-on.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with stress-energy as the local matter accounting object.
  2. Compress curvature into the Einstein tensor on the geometry side.
  3. Use the shared covariant conservation behavior to justify the balance.
  4. Recognize that solving the equation means solving for the metric that also builds the left side.

Objects To Track

  • The stress-energy tensor is the object that measures energy density, momentum flow, pressure, and stress through chosen directions or surfaces.
  • The Einstein tensor is built from curvature and the metric so that its covariant divergence vanishes, matching local conservation of stress-energy.
  • Ricci curvature is a contraction of the full curvature tensor that captures focusing or spreading of nearby geodesic families.
  • The Einstein tensor is the curvature combination whose covariant divergence vanishes automatically.
  • The cosmological constant is a constant term in Einstein's equation, often read as a vacuum energy contribution.

Linked Concepts

Matter and Stress-Energy

Gravity bends space and time. But what causes the bending? Newton said: mass. Einstein's answer is richer. Mass does bend spacetime—but so does energy, momentum (motion), pressure (crowding), and internal stress. Relativity treats all of these as one unified object, because energy and momentum are really the same thing seen from different angles. Gravity must respond to all of them together.

Object: A stress-energy tensor is a 4×4 bookkeeping table (like a spreadsheet with 4 rows and 4 columns). Each cell holds one number that describes a 'flow': a kind of stuff moving in a direction. Top-left corner: energy density (how much energy per unit volume, measured by someone at rest). Top-right block (three entries): momentum flowing—the push and tug through space. Bottom-right 3×3 block: pressure and internal stress—how much matter inside one region pushes on neighboring regions. The row tells you 'what stuff are we counting' (energy or momentum component). The column tells you 'in which direction is it flowing.' Multiply the numbers by a direction vector (an arrow showing which way you are looking) and you get a measurable quantity in that direction.

Operation: Take the tensor (the 4×4 table). Point an arrow in some direction—say, the direction an observer is moving, or the outward normal to a surface. Multiply each row by components of that arrow and sum. The result: the energy density that observer measures, or the momentum flux passing through that surface, or stress between regions. Different arrows give different answers, but all observers agree on the physical truth underneath.

Einstein Equation

We can see that gravity bends the paths of planets and light, and we suspect that this bending is really the shape of space and time. But seeing is not a law. We need a precise rule that says exactly how much the presence of matter and energy warps the geometry around it, and how that warping in turn steers everything moving through it.

Object: The central object is the Einstein tensor, written G. It is a specific recipe built out of the curvature of spacetime that measures, at each point, how the volume of a small cloud of free-falling particles starts to shrink. It is carefully assembled so that a particular running-total of it never changes as you move around, which is the geometric echo of a physical fact: energy and momentum are never created or destroyed, only moved.

Operation: You place the matter and energy of a region into a bookkeeping object called the stress-energy tensor, T, which lists the energy, the momentum, and the pressures present. You set the geometry side, G, equal to a fixed constant times T. Then you solve for the metric, the object that encodes all distances and times, that makes both sides agree everywhere at once.

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Picture a sphere. Stand at the north pole and send a bunch of particles outward in straight lines, like longitude lines on a globe. Watch what happens: as they travel, they get closer together—they converge. Now imagine a saddle (the curved surface of a horse saddle). Do the same thing—send particles in straight lines outward from a point. This time they spread apart. This bundling or spreading is the question: when objects follow their natural straight paths through curved space, do those paths squeeze together or fan wider? Ricci curvature measures exactly that.

Object: Curvature can be packaged in different ways. The most complete package is the Riemann tensor: a list of numbers that tells you, for every tiny loop you can draw in spacetime, how much twist or warp that loop produces. That is a lot of information—too much for the field equation. Ricci curvature is a reduced version. To build it, you take that full Riemann tensor and perform a contraction: a systematic pairing and summing of its components. What you get is a smaller object—one with fewer slots—that captures only the spreading or bunching of paths, throwing away other kinds of twist.

Operation: To calculate Ricci curvature at a point: start with the Riemann tensor, which records all forms of twist. Select a direction in spacetime. Sum up (contract) all the Riemann components that describe how that direction twists upon itself. Repeat for every possible direction. The final result is a single number at that point (or a component of the Ricci tensor), telling you whether geodesic families bunch or spread when they move in that direction.

Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry

Picture a rubber sheet. Place a weight on it and it sags. Now write an equation: weight on one side, how much the sheet curves on the other. Both sides have to follow the same rule—nothing appears or disappears without reason. The weight doesn't vanish, and the sheet's curvature doesn't either. If your equation doesn't respect this, it's broken.

Object: The Einstein tensor is constructed by taking the Ricci tensor—which measures local bending—and mixing it with the scalar curvature (the average of that bending) weighted by the metric (the rule that measures distances in your space). Specifically: Ricci minus half of (scalar curvature times metric). This particular recipe is the only one that makes the divergence zero identically, with no leak.

Operation: To compute the Einstein tensor, start with the Ricci tensor at a point. Compute the scalar curvature. Multiply the scalar curvature by the metric tensor. Divide by two. Subtract this whole thing from the Ricci tensor. What you have left is the Einstein tensor. When you take its divergence—the sum of how it changes in each direction—you always get zero, regardless of the spacetime around it.

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Astronomers measure how fast galaxies move apart—the universe is expanding. If you add up all the matter they can see (gas, dust, stars, and the invisible dark matter holding galaxies together), gravity's pull should slow that expansion down. But the actual measurements show something strange: the expansion is speeding up, not slowing. Something invisible is pushing outward, and it doesn't gather into clumps like matter does.

Object: The cosmological constant is a single unchanging number, usually written as the Greek letter Lambda (Λ). Unlike matter, which collects in dense clumps around galaxies and stars, this number is identical everywhere in the universe and at all times. In Einstein's equation, it acts as a permanent, built-in source of spacetime curvature, as if space and time themselves carry a tiny uniform energy.

Operation: You add a term into Einstein's field equation proportional to Λ on the geometry side. When you then solve this modified equation to predict how the universe evolves, the constant stays in place, uniform and unchanging, and pushes the expansion outward in all directions at once.
