Lecture 16: Lecture 16: Optical Geometry I

How does this lecture use Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics, Geodesics As Free Motion, Metric Signature And Light Cones, Redshift As Stretched Light, Gravitational Lensing And Focusing to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Read spacetime with light: The course needs a bridge from equations to observation. Light is the bridge because it follows the causal structure set by the metric and carries information to detectors.

First-Principles Role

This lecture turns observation into geometry. Light follows null geodesics set by the metric, so lensing, redshift, and signal paths become readings of spacetime structure.

Starting Problem

Optical geometry begins from the fact that most evidence about gravity reaches us as light. The lecture has to stop the reader from imagining light as a small ball being pushed sideways by a force. In relativity, light follows the causal structure supplied by the metric. A light path is a null geodesic: its tangent has zero interval and transports itself along the path. This makes observation a geometric question about allowed signal paths.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to combine the null condition with the geodesic condition. The metric first identifies which directions are light-like. The connection then supplies the straightest-path rule. Solving both together gives null geodesics. Neighboring null geodesics can focus, shear, delay, or bend, which is why lensing and redshift are not separate observational tricks. They are ways the metric writes itself into the light that arrives.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with the metric's light cone at an event where a signal could pass.
  2. Choose directions whose interval is zero, so they are candidates for light.
  3. Impose the geodesic rule so the tangent carries itself along the path.
  4. Compare neighboring light paths to read bending, focusing, delay, or redshift.

Objects To Track

  • Null geodesics are curves whose tangent has zero metric length and is parallel transported along itself.
  • A geodesic is a curve whose tangent direction is transported along itself according to the connection.
  • Lorentzian signature is the metric's time-space sign pattern, and light cones are the resulting boundaries of causal influence.
  • Redshift is the change in measured light frequency between emission and observation along a null path.
  • Lensing is the bending and focusing of families of null geodesics by spacetime curvature.

Linked Concepts

Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics

Watch starlight bend as it passes the Sun, or see light frozen just outside a black hole. That bending is not gravity yanking the light sideways like a magnet pulls iron. Instead, the light is following a path that the curved shape of space and time itself creates. We need words to describe 'which paths does light actually travel through a warped world?'

Object: A null geodesic is a curve snaking through spacetime where two things happen together. First: the tangent vector—an arrow showing the direction you're moving and how fast—has zero length when you measure it using the metric. (That zero-length property is called 'null' and marks light's path, not any other type of motion.) Second: as you move along the curve, your direction stays constant according to the space's curvature rules—a property called parallel transport. These two rules lock down exactly which curves light can take.

Operation: To find where a light ray goes, you write down the geodesic equation—the mathematical rule for finding the 'straightest' paths in a curved space. Then you add the null condition: the metric-measured length of the path must equal zero. Solve both equations together and you get the curve. Once you know the curve, you can compare two nearby light rays and see whether they come together (focusing, like a lens) or spread apart (defocusing).

Geodesics As Free Motion

A stone drifts through empty space, far from stars, gravity, and obstacles. It moves in a straight line forever. But what is straight? On a flat piece of paper the answer is trivial—draw a ruler. Yet near a black hole or inside the curved spacetime around a massive star, there is no background flat space to measure against. You cannot use a ruler because spacetime itself is the only thing that exists. The course needs a way to say: "this particle is moving with no forces pushing it" using only the shape of spacetime, nothing outside it.

Object: A geodesic is a path traced through spacetime with a special property: as you move along the path, the direction you are headed does not change according to the local rule for comparing directions at nearby points—a rule called the connection. Think of walking on the curved surface of Earth. The connection is the instruction for what "same direction" means when you shift from one spot to the next without leaving the surface. A geodesic is a path where, by that local rule, your heading never turns.

Operation: To test if a path is a geodesic: at each point along the path, take the direction you are headed (the tangent vector). Move that direction to the next point using the connection rule. Ask: did the direction change? If the answer is always no (the covariant derivative is zero), the path is a geodesic. This turns a feeling—"I am not turning"—into a precise, checkable recipe.

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

Object: A Lorentzian metric is a measurement table that lives at each point of spacetime. It takes any tiny displacement (a shift in time and space) and computes an interval—a signed number, not always positive. Unlike a ruler in flat space (where distances are always positive), this metric has one time direction that counts as negative and three space directions that count as positive. That pattern of signs is called the signature, and it is what makes light and causality work.

Operation: Take two nearby events and write their separation as a vector: (change in time, change in x, change in y, change in z). The metric is a table of numbers (a matrix). Multiply the vector by the matrix, following the rules of that operation. The result is the squared interval—a single signed number. If it is negative, the separation is timelike, meaning one event could have caused the other. If it is positive, the separation is spacelike, meaning they are far apart and cannot influence each other. If it is exactly zero, the separation is null, and a light beam travels from one event to the other.

Redshift As Stretched Light

When you look at starlight through a telescope, you measure the distance between repeating peaks in the waves—the wavelength. For distant stars in an expanding universe, something strange happens: the peaks arrive farther apart than they left the star. The waves are stretched. They shift toward the red end of the light spectrum, where the wavelengths are longer and the jiggles slower. The question is: why? Did the star move away from you (like a siren's wail stretching as an ambulance recedes)? Did the space between you and the star get bigger (stretching the peaks apart just from the distance growing)? Or is the star buried in strong gravity where time itself runs slowly? All three look identical when you only look at the light.

Object: Redshift is a single pure number you extract from light by comparing it at two moments: when it left its source and when it hit your detector. You take the wavelength (or the frequency—the wiggle speed—of the light) at each moment and form a ratio. More formally, light travels as a wave in spacetime, carrying a property called the wave vector (a direction and frequency all bundled together). If you call this k at the source and k at the observer, the redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured using the local time of that location. Redshift is not a thing that sits in one place; it is a relationship stretching across spacetime from source to detector.

Operation: To compute redshift, you follow the light path backward from your detector to its source, tracking it through the spacetime geometry. At the moment and location of emission, you write down the wave vector—the light's direction and frequency—using the emitter's local concept of time. At the moment and location of detection, you do the same using your local concept of time. Then you divide: the frequency you detected divided by the frequency that left the source. That ratio (1+z) is pure numbers; it encodes everything the spacetime geometry did to the light between those two events. You are not changing anything, not moving the light, not rewriting spacetime—you are just reading what the metric says the frequencies were at each end.

Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

Look at a distant galaxy through a telescope. Between you and it sits another galaxy so massive that the space and time around it are bent. When light from the far galaxy travels toward you, it passes through this warped region. The bent space steers the light off its straight path. You see the distant galaxy in the wrong spot in the sky. Sometimes it looks magnified—brighter and larger. Sometimes it splits into two or three images of the same galaxy. We need a way to understand this bending without pretending gravity is a force pulling on light.

Object: A bundle of light rays traveling close together and nearly parallel is a single object called a congruence of null geodesics. In everyday language: a bundle (meaning many rays packed tight), of null (meaning light-speed paths), geodesics (meaning the straightest paths available). At any moment in space and time, you take a small patch of rays all pointing nearly the same way. That patch is one congruence. The congruence is the full rule: it tells you at every nearby spot in space-time, which direction a light ray points. Curved space and time act on the entire bundle at once. The bundle can get squeezed, stretched, tilted, or twisted by the curvature.

Operation: Take your bundle of light rays and imagine moving perpendicular to them by a tiny distance. Watch three things: Does the cross-sectional area shrink or grow? Do the rays' directions shift relative to each other? Are the rays spinning as a group? You are not watching single rays. You are watching how the whole bundle transforms. These three changes have names: expansion (area change), shear (directional shifts), and twist (group rotation).
