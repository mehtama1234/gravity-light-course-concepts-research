Lecture 16: Lecture 16: Optical Geometry I

How does this lecture use Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics, Geodesics As Free Motion, Metric Signature And Light Cones, Redshift As Stretched Light, Gravitational Lensing And Focusing to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Read spacetime with light: The course needs a bridge from equations to observation. Light is the bridge because it follows the causal structure set by the metric and carries information to detectors.

First-Principles Role

This lecture turns observation into geometry. Light follows null geodesics set by the metric, so lensing, redshift, and signal paths become readings of spacetime structure.

Starting Problem

Optical geometry begins from the fact that most evidence about gravity reaches us as light. The lecture has to stop the reader from imagining light as a small ball being pushed sideways by a force. In relativity, light follows the causal structure supplied by the metric. A light path is a null geodesic: its tangent has zero interval and transports itself along the path. This makes observation a geometric question about allowed signal paths.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to combine the null condition with the geodesic condition. The metric first identifies which directions are light-like. The connection then supplies the straightest-path rule. Solving both together gives null geodesics. Neighboring null geodesics can focus, shear, delay, or bend, which is why lensing and redshift are not separate observational tricks. They are ways the metric writes itself into the light that arrives.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with the metric's light cone at an event where a signal could pass.
  2. Choose directions whose interval is zero, so they are candidates for light.
  3. Impose the geodesic rule so the tangent carries itself along the path.
  4. Compare neighboring light paths to read bending, focusing, delay, or redshift.

Objects To Track

  • Null geodesics are curves whose tangent has zero metric length and is parallel transported along itself.
  • A geodesic is a curve whose tangent direction is transported along itself according to the connection.
  • Lorentzian signature is the metric's time-space sign pattern, and light cones are the resulting boundaries of causal influence.
  • Redshift is the change in measured light frequency between emission and observation along a null path.
  • Lensing is the bending and focusing of families of null geodesics by spacetime curvature.

Linked Concepts

Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics

You watch starlight bend around the Sun, or light hover at a black hole's edge. The light isn't being pulled by a force—it's following a path the curved spacetime itself prescribes. We need a language for 'paths light naturally takes through warped geometry.'

Object: A null geodesic is a curve in spacetime whose tangent vector (the direction you're moving) has zero length according to the metric. The curve also satisfies the geodesic condition: as you move along it, your direction stays constant (parallel transported) with respect to the space's bending.

Operation: To find a light ray: start with the geodesic equation (the rule for 'straightest' paths in curved space), impose the null condition (metric length = 0), and solve for the curve. You can then compare two nearby light rays to see if they converge (focusing) or diverge (defocusing).

Geodesics As Free Motion

Imagine a stone floating in space, far from all gravity and obstacles. It travels in a straight line. But what does "straight" mean when space itself is curved? In flat space the answer is obvious, but near a black hole or inside the warped spacetime around a star, there is no global flat background to measure against. The course needs a way to say "this particle is moving freely" using only the shape of spacetime itself.

Object: A geodesic is a curve (a path traced out in spacetime) with a special property: at every point along the curve, the tangent vector to the curve does not change direction according to the local measuring rule called the connection. The connection is the instruction for how to compare directions at nearby points without leaving the surface. A geodesic is a curve where that comparison says "the tangent stays the same."

Operation: To check whether a curve is a geodesic: take the tangent vector at each point on the curve, transport it to the next point using the connection rule, and ask if it changed. If it did not change (its covariant derivative is zero), the curve is a geodesic. This operation turns an intuitive idea—"the direction doesn't bend"—into a checkable mathematical condition.

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

Object: A Lorentzian metric is a measurement tool that assigns a signed interval to any infinitesimal displacement. Unlike a Euclidean metric (where the square of a distance is always positive), a Lorentzian metric has one negative direction (time) and three positive directions (space). The signature—the count of signs—is what makes spacetime causal and light possible.

Operation: To use the metric: write down two nearby events' separation as a vector in spacetime. Apply the metric (a matrix of numbers) to that vector. The result—the squared interval—tells you the separation's type. If the squared interval is negative, the separation is timelike (one event might cause the other). If positive, it's spacelike (they're far apart in space, causally disconnected). If zero, it's null or lightlike (light travels this path exactly).

Redshift As Stretched Light

You measure starlight in a telescope and count the wave peaks in time. When the star is far away in an expanding universe, the waves arrive stretched. The wavelength is longer, the frequency lower. The light is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. You need to know: is this redshift because the star is moving away, or because space itself is expanding between you and the star, or because the star is in strong gravity? These look identical in the data.

Object: The redshift is a pure number you compute by comparing light at two moments: when it left the emitter and when it arrived at the observer. You take the wavelength (or frequency) at those two events, write them as components of a vector that points along the light ray in spacetime, and compute their ratio. Formally, if the light ray carries a wave vector k, redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured in the local frame of time at that event. It does not live in one place; it is a relation across spacetime.

Operation: Write down the light ray as a path through spacetime from emitter to observer. At emission, write the wave vector as a quantity aligned with the emitter's notion of time. At observation, do the same in the observer's frame. Now compute the ratio: k_at_observer / k_at_emitter. That ratio—call it (1+z)—encodes everything the geometry did to the light between the two events. The operation is pure measurement: you do not move the light or change the spacetime. You just read off what the metric says the frequencies were.

Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

You see a distant galaxy, but its light has passed near a massive galaxy between you and it. The massive galaxy's gravity warps spacetime, bending the light path. Now the distant galaxy appears in the wrong place in the sky, magnified, or even split into multiple images. We need to understand how gravity bends light rays without pretending it pulls on light like a breeze pushes dust.

Object: A bundle of light rays close together is called a congruence of null geodesics (null means the paths are light paths; congruence means a family filling a region). At each moment in spacetime, you have a small patch of rays pointing in nearly the same direction. The congruence is the rule that tells you, at every nearby point in spacetime, which direction a light ray points. Spacetime curvature acts on this whole bundle: it can squeeze it, stretch it, tilt it, or twist it.

Operation: Take a congruence of light rays and move a small distance perpendicular to them. Measure how the cross-sectional area changes, how the rays' directions shift relative to each other, and whether the rays are rotating as a group. You are not measuring single rays; you are measuring how the bundle transforms. This is called the expansion, shear, and twist of the congruence.
