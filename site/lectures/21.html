Lecture 21: Lecture 21: Cosmology - The late epoch

How does this lecture use Cosmology and Expansion, Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term, Redshift As Stretched Light, Scale Factor As Expansion History, Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Solve evolution and global shape: The course then asks how whole spacetimes behave: how valid initial data evolves, how the universe expands, and how causal boundaries such as horizons are understood.

First-Principles Role

This lecture reads late-time cosmology through scale factor, Hubble rate, redshift, and possible vacuum energy. It treats expansion as changing geometry rather than motion from a center.

Starting Problem

Late-epoch cosmology asks why the universe's large-scale expansion does not behave like a simple coasting motion through empty space. Observations arrive through redshift and distance relations, while the model speaks through the scale factor, Hubble rate, matter content, curvature, and possible vacuum energy. The lecture has to connect these without falling back to the picture of galaxies flying away from a central explosion.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to treat redshift and Hubble rate as readings of the changing metric scale. The scale factor tells how comoving separations grow. Its fractional rate of change is the Hubble rate. Light wavelength stretches with the same large-scale geometry, so redshift becomes evidence for the expansion history. The cosmological constant then enters as a uniform term that changes the expansion without clumping like ordinary matter.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with comoving points whose reference separation stays fixed in the model.
  2. Use the scale factor to turn that reference separation into measured separation.
  3. Read the Hubble rate as the fractional change of that scale factor.
  4. Use redshift and possible vacuum terms to constrain the late-time expansion history.

Objects To Track

  • A cosmological model uses symmetry assumptions plus a scale factor to reduce Einstein's equation to equations for large-scale evolution.
  • The cosmological constant is a constant term in Einstein's equation, often read as a vacuum energy contribution.
  • Redshift is the change in measured light frequency between emission and observation along a null path.
  • The scale factor is the time-dependent multiplier that converts fixed comoving separations into physical separations.
  • The Hubble rate is the rate of change of the scale factor divided by the scale factor itself.

Linked Concepts

Cosmology and Expansion

When you try to apply gravity's equation to the whole universe, you face an impossible task: track every galaxy, every star, every atom. Physicists need a way to capture the universe's large-scale behavior without that hopeless detail work.

Object: A scale factor is a single number, usually written a(t), that multiplies all distances in space at each moment in time. When a(t) grows, all distances grow together; when it shrinks, they shrink. It reduces the full Einstein equation (which tracks every detail of spacetime geometry) to a much simpler rule: how does this one number evolve?

Operation: You start by assuming the universe looks the same in every direction (isotropy) and everywhere you go (homogeneity) — at large scales, roughly true. Under those assumptions, the Einstein equation simplifies to an equation for a(t) alone. You solve for a(t), and from that single curve, you can read off cosmic expansion history and how matter density changes.

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Imagine weighing all the matter in the universe—galaxies, gas, everything we can see. When physicists do this and ask whether the universe should be expanding faster or slower, the numbers don't match what telescopes show. Something else is pushing the expansion. That something is invisible and doesn't clump into galaxies like ordinary matter does.

Object: The cosmological constant is a single number, often written as the Greek letter Lambda (Λ). Unlike matter, which clumps and changes from place to place, this constant is the same everywhere and at every time. In the field equation, it acts as a built-in contribution to spacetime's curvature, as if spacetime itself carries a tiny, uniform energy.

Operation: You add the term into Einstein's field equation: on the left side (the geometry side), you append a term proportional to Λ. Then you solve the modified equation to see how the universe expands. The constant sits there, unchanging, pulling the expansion apart uniformly in all directions.

Redshift As Stretched Light

You measure starlight in a telescope and count the wave peaks in time. When the star is far away in an expanding universe, the waves arrive stretched. The wavelength is longer, the frequency lower. The light is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. You need to know: is this redshift because the star is moving away, or because space itself is expanding between you and the star, or because the star is in strong gravity? These look identical in the data.

Object: The redshift is a pure number you compute by comparing light at two moments: when it left the emitter and when it arrived at the observer. You take the wavelength (or frequency) at those two events, write them as components of a vector that points along the light ray in spacetime, and compute their ratio. Formally, if the light ray carries a wave vector k, redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured in the local frame of time at that event. It does not live in one place; it is a relation across spacetime.

Operation: Write down the light ray as a path through spacetime from emitter to observer. At emission, write the wave vector as a quantity aligned with the emitter's notion of time. At observation, do the same in the observer's frame. Now compute the ratio: k_at_observer / k_at_emitter. That ratio—call it (1+z)—encodes everything the geometry did to the light between the two events. The operation is pure measurement: you do not move the light or change the spacetime. You just read off what the metric says the frequencies were.

Scale Factor As Expansion History

A universe with millions of galaxies scattered throughout space doesn't stay still—distances between them change over time. You need one number, varying with time, that captures this change in a unified way for the whole spacetime.

Object: The scale factor is a single function of time, often written a(t), that measures how much physical distances scale up or down as the universe ages. It sits in the spacetime geometry itself. Suppose two galaxies are at fixed coordinates (like longitude and latitude on Earth, but for spacetime). Their true separation in meters is the coordinate distance multiplied by a(t). As a(t) grows, the same coordinate gap means a larger physical gap.

Operation: You use the scale factor by plugging it into the Einstein field equations—the laws linking spacetime curvature to matter and energy. These equations become ordinary time-dependent differential equations for how a(t) changes. You solve them once you specify how much matter and energy fill the universe, and the shape of spacetime curvature assumed. The solution tells you a(t) for the whole history.

Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and see that galaxies are getting farther apart over time. But just knowing the distance grows is not enough—you need a way to ask: *how fast* is space expanding right now? Is it doubling every billion years or every trillion years? That difference is everything for predicting the future of the universe.

Object: The Hubble rate is the fractional rate of change of the scale factor—a number that describes how much bigger the universe is now than at an earlier time. If the scale factor (call it $a(t)$) has grown from value $a_0$ to value $a_1$ over a time $\Delta t$, the Hubble rate is how fast $a$ is changing *relative to its current size*: $H = (1/a) \, da/dt$. It answers the question: per unit time, what fraction of the current cosmic size is being added?

Operation: To find the Hubble rate from observations: measure the redshift of a galaxy (how much light from it is stretched), convert that to a recession speed using the Doppler formula, then divide by the distance. The result is a number per unit time—often quoted in units like inverse megayears. That number tells you how fast the scale factor is changing relative to itself *right now*.
