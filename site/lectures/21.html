Lecture 21: Lecture 21: Cosmology - The late epoch

How does this lecture use Cosmology and Expansion, Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term, Redshift As Stretched Light, Scale Factor As Expansion History, Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Solve evolution and global shape: The course then asks how whole spacetimes behave: how valid initial data evolves, how the universe expands, and how causal boundaries such as horizons are understood.

First-Principles Role

This lecture reads late-time cosmology through scale factor, Hubble rate, redshift, and possible vacuum energy. It treats expansion as changing geometry rather than motion from a center.

Starting Problem

Late-epoch cosmology asks why the universe's large-scale expansion does not behave like a simple coasting motion through empty space. Observations arrive through redshift and distance relations, while the model speaks through the scale factor, Hubble rate, matter content, curvature, and possible vacuum energy. The lecture has to connect these without falling back to the picture of galaxies flying away from a central explosion.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to treat redshift and Hubble rate as readings of the changing metric scale. The scale factor tells how comoving separations grow. Its fractional rate of change is the Hubble rate. Light wavelength stretches with the same large-scale geometry, so redshift becomes evidence for the expansion history. The cosmological constant then enters as a uniform term that changes the expansion without clumping like ordinary matter.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with comoving points whose reference separation stays fixed in the model.
  2. Use the scale factor to turn that reference separation into measured separation.
  3. Read the Hubble rate as the fractional change of that scale factor.
  4. Use redshift and possible vacuum terms to constrain the late-time expansion history.

Objects To Track

  • A cosmological model uses symmetry assumptions plus a scale factor to reduce Einstein's equation to equations for large-scale evolution.
  • The cosmological constant is a constant term in Einstein's equation, often read as a vacuum energy contribution.
  • Redshift is the change in measured light frequency between emission and observation along a null path.
  • The scale factor is the time-dependent multiplier that converts fixed comoving separations into physical separations.
  • The Hubble rate is the rate of change of the scale factor divided by the scale factor itself.

Linked Concepts

Cosmology and Expansion

When you write down Einstein's equation for gravity, it describes how curved space-and-time changes in response to matter and energy. To use it for the whole universe, you would need to know the exact arrangement of every galaxy, star, and atom — an infinite amount of detail. Physicists needed a shortcut: a simpler rule that captures the universe's large-scale behavior without tracking everything.

Object: The scale factor is a single number written a(t) that depends only on time. Imagine it as a stretching factor: multiply it by every distance in space at each moment. When a(t) grows, all distances grow by the same fraction. When it shrinks, they shrink together. The full Einstein equation — which in principle tracks the shape of space at every point and how it bends — becomes a single rule: how does a(t) change with time? That is a radical simplification.

Operation: Start by assuming two things: the universe looks the same in every direction (if you are at any point and look around, there is no special direction), and it looks the same everywhere you go (no location is special compared to another). These assumptions are roughly true when you step back far enough. Under these conditions, Einstein's full equation simplifies to an equation for a(t) alone. Solve that equation, and you have the cosmic expansion history encoded in one curve.

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Astronomers measure how fast galaxies move apart—the universe is expanding. If you add up all the matter they can see (gas, dust, stars, and the invisible dark matter holding galaxies together), gravity's pull should slow that expansion down. But the actual measurements show something strange: the expansion is speeding up, not slowing. Something invisible is pushing outward, and it doesn't gather into clumps like matter does.

Object: The cosmological constant is a single unchanging number, usually written as the Greek letter Lambda (Λ). Unlike matter, which collects in dense clumps around galaxies and stars, this number is identical everywhere in the universe and at all times. In Einstein's equation, it acts as a permanent, built-in source of spacetime curvature, as if space and time themselves carry a tiny uniform energy.

Operation: You add a term into Einstein's field equation proportional to Λ on the geometry side. When you then solve this modified equation to predict how the universe evolves, the constant stays in place, uniform and unchanging, and pushes the expansion outward in all directions at once.

Redshift As Stretched Light

When you look at starlight through a telescope, you measure the distance between repeating peaks in the waves—the wavelength. For distant stars in an expanding universe, something strange happens: the peaks arrive farther apart than they left the star. The waves are stretched. They shift toward the red end of the light spectrum, where the wavelengths are longer and the jiggles slower. The question is: why? Did the star move away from you (like a siren's wail stretching as an ambulance recedes)? Did the space between you and the star get bigger (stretching the peaks apart just from the distance growing)? Or is the star buried in strong gravity where time itself runs slowly? All three look identical when you only look at the light.

Object: Redshift is a single pure number you extract from light by comparing it at two moments: when it left its source and when it hit your detector. You take the wavelength (or the frequency—the wiggle speed—of the light) at each moment and form a ratio. More formally, light travels as a wave in spacetime, carrying a property called the wave vector (a direction and frequency all bundled together). If you call this k at the source and k at the observer, the redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured using the local time of that location. Redshift is not a thing that sits in one place; it is a relationship stretching across spacetime from source to detector.

Operation: To compute redshift, you follow the light path backward from your detector to its source, tracking it through the spacetime geometry. At the moment and location of emission, you write down the wave vector—the light's direction and frequency—using the emitter's local concept of time. At the moment and location of detection, you do the same using your local concept of time. Then you divide: the frequency you detected divided by the frequency that left the source. That ratio (1+z) is pure numbers; it encodes everything the spacetime geometry did to the light between those two events. You are not changing anything, not moving the light, not rewriting spacetime—you are just reading what the metric says the frequencies were at each end.

Scale Factor As Expansion History

Imagine you scatter thousands of dots across a piece of rubber, each dot a galaxy. As the rubber stretches, every dot moves away from every other dot. You want one number—call it a—that changes with time and tells you how much that stretching has happened at any moment. That single number should capture the whole story of how distances in the universe change.

Object: The scale factor a(t) is a single number that changes with time. It measures how much every distance in space has been stretched since some starting moment. Imagine two galaxies locked in place on a cosmic grid—like latitude and longitude, but for spacetime. Their raw grid separation never changes. But the true distance between them—measured in actual miles or kilometers—is that grid separation multiplied by a(t). When a(t) = 1, the grid distance equals the true distance. When a(t) = 2, the true distance is twice the grid separation. As a(t) grows, the same grid positions mean objects are farther apart.

Operation: To use the scale factor, you feed it into Einstein's equations—the rules that link how spacetime curves to how much matter and energy live inside. These equations become recipes for how a(t) changes over time. You tell the equations what matter fills the universe (how dense it is, how much pressure it exerts), and the equations spit out a solution: a(t) for every moment in history. That solution is cosmology—the full story of how the universe expanded or contracted.

Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and notice that galaxies are pulling apart from each other—getting farther away over time. But knowing the distance grows is only half the picture. You need to know *how fast* this is happening right now, at this moment. Is space doubling in size every billion years, or every trillion years? That difference changes everything about what happens to the universe in the future.

Object: The Hubble rate is a single number that tells you the fractional expansion speed. Imagine the universe has a size that we can label with a scale factor—call it $a(t)$—which says how much bigger the universe is now than at some earlier time. If the scale factor goes from $a_0$ to $a_1$ over time $\Delta t$, the Hubble rate answers: per unit time, what *fraction* of the current size is being added? Written as a formula: $H = (1/a) \, da/dt$. This is the fractional rate of change.

Operation: Here is how astronomers find the Hubble rate: observe a galaxy and measure how much its light is redshifted—stretched to longer wavelengths. Use the Doppler formula to convert that into a recession speed. Measure the galaxy's distance using brightness or other methods. Then divide the speed by the distance. The result is a number per unit time—say, 0.1 per billion years. That is the Hubble rate: how much the scale factor grows as a fraction of itself per unit time, right now.
