Lecture 05: Lecture 5: Tangent Spaces

How does this lecture use Tangent Spaces and Fields, Dual Vectors Measure Directions, Tangent Vectors As Derivatives to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Build space before measuring it: A theory of gravity cannot begin by assuming a fixed grid, fixed rulers, or a surrounding room. These lectures build the minimum language needed to speak about places, change, and physical quantities before distance has been granted.

First-Principles Role

This lecture gives direction an internal meaning. A tangent vector is understood through rates of change of functions, which lets the course talk about velocity, light direction, and local motion without an outside drawing space.

Starting Problem

Tangent spaces are needed because a direction at a point cannot be defined by an arrow drawn in an outside room. Spacetime is not presented as a surface embedded in a larger flat container. The lecture therefore asks what a direction means from inside the manifold. The answer is operational: a direction is known by the rates of change it gives to functions at the point. That makes velocity and light direction local before any global picture is available.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to define tangent vectors by their action on test functions. Choose a function near the point and ask how fast it changes along a possible curve through the point. Curves that give the same rate for every test function define the same tangent vector. This shifts direction from picture to operation, which is why later vector fields, observer velocities, and null directions can be defined intrinsically.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Begin with a point where a direction is needed but no outside arrow is allowed.
  2. Choose local functions that can test movement through that point.
  3. Define the direction by the rate it assigns to each test function.
  4. Collect all such directions at the point into the tangent space.

Objects To Track

  • A tangent space is the vector space of directions available at one point; a field assigns a value, such as a vector or tensor, to each point.
  • A dual vector, also called a covector or one-form, is a linear measuring rule on vectors.
  • A tangent vector can be understood as a rule that tells how functions change at a point along a possible direction.

Linked Concepts

Tangent Spaces and Fields

At any moment and place in spacetime, a particle or light ray can move in many different directions. At the same moment, you need to measure things that vary from point to point—temperature, pressure, electric field strength. Without a way to say "these are the directions available here" and "this is how quantities change as you move," physics cannot describe what actually happens at each specific event.

Object: At any point p in spacetime, a tangent space is the complete set of all tiny, infinitesimal directions you could head. These directions are built from a precise rule: pick a smooth curve passing through point p; any quantity that varies smoothly (like temperature along the curve) changes at different rates depending on the curve's direction. This rate of change directly encodes the direction. A field assigns to every point in spacetime a specific value—a single number, a direction vector, or a more complex measurement rule—and all these values change smoothly from place to place.

Operation: Trace any curve through spacetime point by point. At each point, the curve has a tangent direction—the way it is heading at that instant. This direction is a tangent vector. Now pick any smoothly varying quantity (temperature, altitude, clock reading). The rate at which this quantity changes as you move along the curve, in the tangent direction, is found by differentiation. Repeat this operation at every point throughout spacetime: you now have a working field that tells you the rate of change in any direction at any location.

Dual Vectors Measure Directions

Physics tracks two types of quantities: arrows (velocity, force) show direction and speed of motion, while measuring rules (pressure gradient, light intensity) ask 'how much flows in this direction?' Treating them identically breaks the math because they transform opposite ways when you shift coordinates.

Object: A dual vector (also called a covector or one-form) is a measuring machine built for vectors. Feed it a vector: it produces one number. The machine respects addition and scaling—double the vector input, and the output doubles. When coordinates change, the machine's internal weights shift in a specific opposite way that keeps its output unchanged.

Operation: Apply a dual vector by feeding a vector into it. If the dual vector has components (a₁, a₂, a₃) and the vector is (v₁, v₂, v₃), compute a₁v₁ + a₂v₂ + a₃v₃. The result is one number that stays the same no matter which coordinate system you used to write down those components.

Tangent Vectors As Derivatives

You stand on a hillside and want to measure how temperature changes as you step in one direction versus another. But what exactly is 'a direction at a point'? In Einstein's picture, spacetime—the thing that makes up everything—isn't sitting inside some larger flat container. There is no external reference frame to draw arrows from. So you need a way to define what 'a direction' even means using only what is built into spacetime itself.

Object: A tangent vector is a rule—a machine—that takes any smooth function defined on the space and outputs a number: how fast that function is changing along a chosen direction at a specific point. The machine has no picture, no arrow. It is purely intrinsic to the space; you build it from the space's own structure using calculus. The rule encodes both 'which point' and 'which direction' through the way it measures change in functions.

Operation: To use a tangent vector, hand it a smooth function—say, temperature T defined everywhere on spacetime. The tangent vector spits out the rate of change of T along its direction at its point. You can verify what tangent vector you have by testing it on many different functions: the same point, the same direction, but different inputs. The tangent vector always follows two rules: adding two functions gives the sum of the outputs, and the product rule from calculus holds—exactly like taking a derivative.
