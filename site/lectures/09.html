Lecture 09: Lecture 9: Newtonian spacetime is curved!

How does this lecture use Curvature, Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change, Ricci Curvature As Volume Change to move the course from everyday geometric intuition toward a usable theory of gravity and light?

Family Role

Turn falling into geometry: The course must explain free motion without saying that every path is judged against an invisible flat background. It needs its own rule for straightness and its own test for real gravitational curvature.

First-Principles Role

This lecture shows that the geometric reading of gravity is not just a later Einsteinian invention. By focusing on relative acceleration and curvature-like bookkeeping, it makes tidal behavior the durable fact behind older force language.

Starting Problem

This lecture presses on a surprising claim: even Newtonian gravity can be read as curvature when it is written with the right spacetime bookkeeping. The point is not to pretend Newton's theory is already general relativity. The point is to show that gravity is not captured by the force felt by one body alone. The more stable fact is how neighboring freely falling paths accelerate relative to each other. That relative motion is the bridge from an old gravity picture to the geometric one.

Mathematical Turn

The mathematical turn is to read gravitational content through deviation of nearby free paths and through the curvature terms that control that deviation. A single falling observer can remove much of the local pull by choosing a falling frame. A family of nearby observers cannot remove the way their separations change. Ricci-type information then records part of that focusing or spreading. This prepares the reader to see Einstein gravity as a law about tidal geometry rather than a replacement word for force.

Worked Path Through The Lecture

  1. Start with a small cloud of nearby freely falling test bodies.
  2. Ignore the motion of one body by itself and compare separations inside the cloud.
  3. Read relative acceleration as the gravitational fact that survives a falling frame.
  4. Connect that relative acceleration to curvature, especially the Ricci information used later.

Objects To Track

  • Curvature is the tensorial failure of covariant derivatives to commute, equivalently the failure of parallel transport around small loops to return a vector unchanged.
  • The Riemann curvature tensor records the failure of two covariant derivatives, taken in different orders, to give the same result.
  • Ricci curvature is a contraction of the full curvature tensor that captures focusing or spreading of nearby geodesic families.

Linked Concepts

Curvature

Two astronauts floating freely next to each other will gradually move closer together or farther apart. Not because they push or pull, but because the space around them is bent. We need a way to measure this drift and compare it to what would happen in flat, unbent space.

Object: Curvature measures a failure to commute. Imagine two operations, Call them D-east and D-north, that shift a number or vector slightly east or north while accounting for the curved grid. If space were flat, doing D-east then D-north would give the same result as D-north then D-east. In curved space, they don't. That difference is the Riemann tensor—a bookkeeping object with four slots: two for the directions you compared, one for which component of the thing you moved changed, and one for which component of the result you read. The mismatch is curvature.

Operation: To test for curvature, take two derivatives in opposite orders and subtract them—like walking east then north, then north then east, and checking if you end up at the same place. For gravity, you instead watch two nearby free-falling objects: if they accelerate relative to each other (squeeze closer or peel apart), that relative acceleration directly tells you the curvature.

Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change

Imagine two friends standing very close together on Earth's curved surface, both facing north. They each walk 100 meters forward (north), then 100 meters to the right (east). If Earth were flat, they'd meet up at the same spot. But Earth is curved, so they land slightly apart — neither of them moved wrong, the space itself changed their directions as they walked. A mathematician needs a precise way to capture this: a statement that says 'the order in which you take steps matters on a curved surface.' That is what Riemann curvature measures.

Object: The Riemann curvature tensor is a machine — built from something called the connection, which is a rule that tells you how to transport (move) a direction arrow from one spot to a neighboring spot while respecting the curvature. Riemann measures: if you pick up an arrow and walk it in a small loop (north, then east, then south, then west, back to start), how much does that arrow rotate or twist by the time it returns home? More precisely, Riemann captures the failure of two corrected derivatives — covariant derivatives, which are operations that act like 'change' but stay honest to the curved geometry — to produce the same result when applied in opposite orders.

Operation: Feed Riemann curvature three ingredients: two different directions you want to move (call them X and Y), and a vector (an arrow showing a direction and speed) that you want to carry along. Riemann outputs: the extra twist, tilt, or offset that happens because these two moves don't commute (don't produce the same result in either order). In formulas, you compute the change rate along X of the change rate along Y, then subtract the change rate along Y of the change rate along X — whatever is left over is Riemann acting on your vector.

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Picture a sphere. Stand at the north pole and send a bunch of particles outward in straight lines, like longitude lines on a globe. Watch what happens: as they travel, they get closer together—they converge. Now imagine a saddle (the curved surface of a horse saddle). Do the same thing—send particles in straight lines outward from a point. This time they spread apart. This bundling or spreading is the question: when objects follow their natural straight paths through curved space, do those paths squeeze together or fan wider? Ricci curvature measures exactly that.

Object: Curvature can be packaged in different ways. The most complete package is the Riemann tensor: a list of numbers that tells you, for every tiny loop you can draw in spacetime, how much twist or warp that loop produces. That is a lot of information—too much for the field equation. Ricci curvature is a reduced version. To build it, you take that full Riemann tensor and perform a contraction: a systematic pairing and summing of its components. What you get is a smaller object—one with fewer slots—that captures only the spreading or bunching of paths, throwing away other kinds of twist.

Operation: To calculate Ricci curvature at a point: start with the Riemann tensor, which records all forms of twist. Select a direction in spacetime. Sum up (contract) all the Riemann components that describe how that direction twists upon itself. Repeat for every possible direction. The final result is a single number at that point (or a component of the Ricci tensor), telling you whether geodesic families bunch or spread when they move in that direction.
