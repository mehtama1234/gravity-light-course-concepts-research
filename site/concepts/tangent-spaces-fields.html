Tangent Spaces and Fields

At any moment and place in spacetime, a particle or light ray can move in many different directions. At the same moment, you need to measure things that vary from point to point—temperature, pressure, electric field strength. Without a way to say "these are the directions available here" and "this is how quantities change as you move," physics cannot describe what actually happens at each specific event.

Naive Picture

Picture spacetime as a curved rubber balloon or a wrinkled hill. At each spot on the surface, imagine tiny arrows sticking straight out—like grass blades or an ant's legs touching the ground. These arrows represent the directions a thing could move next. The set of all such arrows at one point is the tangent space.

Why It Fails

Spacetime is not sitting inside a larger, flat space where you can draw arrows on some invisible page around it. There is no outer container, no surrounding flat region. Arrows and directions must be defined purely from spacetime's own geometry and structure—not imported from an imaginary outside world.

Mathematical Object

At any point p in spacetime, a tangent space is the complete set of all tiny, infinitesimal directions you could head. These directions are built from a precise rule: pick a smooth curve passing through point p; any quantity that varies smoothly (like temperature along the curve) changes at different rates depending on the curve's direction. This rate of change directly encodes the direction. A field assigns to every point in spacetime a specific value—a single number, a direction vector, or a more complex measurement rule—and all these values change smoothly from place to place.

Operation

Trace any curve through spacetime point by point. At each point, the curve has a tangent direction—the way it is heading at that instant. This direction is a tangent vector. Now pick any smoothly varying quantity (temperature, altitude, clock reading). The rate at which this quantity changes as you move along the curve, in the tangent direction, is found by differentiation. Repeat this operation at every point throughout spacetime: you now have a working field that tells you the rate of change in any direction at any location.

Why It Matters

Particles move along curves in spacetime. At each point on that curve, the tangent vector shows the particle's direction and speed. Light moves along special tangent directions—null directions—where the spacetime distance (built from the metric's measurement rule) equals zero. Gravity operates by comparing tangent vectors at different locations using a connection (a rule for how to slide vectors from one point to another). All matter and forces are represented as tensor fields: at each spacetime event, they assign vectors, measurement rules, or higher mathematical objects that encode energy, momentum, and stress.

What Breaks Without It

Without tangent spaces, velocity loses all meaning—it hides behind your choice of coordinates instead of saying something coordinate-free about the motion. Local measurements like density, temperature, or field strength evaporate into subjective observer preferences. Physics becomes entangled: you cannot tell what spacetime's actual curvature does from what your arbitrary map or choice of coordinates merely imposed.

Mini Example

Stand at one point on a winding mountain trail. From where you are, the next steps form a complete landscape of directions: straight ahead along the path, uphill to the left, downhill to the right, and every angle between. These possible next-step directions are your tangent space at that point. At the same location, measure the local conditions: wind speed and direction, air temperature, barometric pressure, how steeply the ground slopes. Each measurement is a field value at that spot. Take one step forward and stand at a new point on the trail. The tangent space—the set of possible next steps—rotates and changes shape because the ground itself has tilted. The field values also shift: temperature drops at higher altitude, wind direction swings as you enter a different feature, pressure decreases. This continuous, location-dependent behavior holds everywhere on the mountain without ever having to flatten it into a plane or imagine it sitting inside a larger space.

Common Confusion

A field is not a force or influence pushing things around. It is any smoothly assigned quantity: a temperature field (assigning degrees to each location), a density field (assigning mass per volume), a wind field (assigning direction and speed), an electric field (assigning force per charge), a metric (assigning a measurement rule to find distances), or an abstract tensor field (assigning a multilinear object to bookkeep complex relationships).

First-Principles Walkthrough

Start with what you can know at your own location and along the path immediately ahead of you. You need two separate languages. First language: what motions are possible at this point, right now? Second language: how do physical measurements—height, temperature, time on a clock—change as you move across the world? A natural thought: these must live somewhere outside spacetime, in some invisible surrounding space where you can draw arrows. This is wrong. There is no surrounding space. Instead, tangent directions emerge from spacetime's most fundamental property: smooth quantities change along curves. You choose any path through spacetime. You pick any smoothly varying quantity. That quantity changes at a certain rate along your path. That rate of change directly defines and encodes a direction. By considering all possible infinitesimal directions, you build the entire tangent space at that point. A field then becomes the assignment of values—a number, a vector, a measurement rule—at every single location. Neither tangent spaces nor fields require an invisible container. Both come straight from spacetime's intrinsic structure.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The tangent space T_p at a point p is a vector space—a collection of directions—where each direction encodes a rate of change. Formally, directions are directional derivatives: you pick a direction, then measure how fast a smooth function increases in that direction. A scalar field is a rule that assigns a real number to each point; if you zoom in, nearby points have nearby numbers. A vector field assigns to each point p a direction vector living in that point's tangent space T_p; as you walk across spacetime, the vector rotates and changes. A tensor field assigns a multilinear bookkeeping object at each point—something that packages multiple related numbers in a coordinate-free way. All fields must be smooth: if you stand at one point and look at a nearby point, the field values must stay close to each other, with no sudden jumps. This smoothness property ensures physics is intrinsic and depends only on spacetime's geometry, never on your arbitrary choice of coordinate labels.

Where This Idea Itself Breaks Down

Tangent spaces assume the surface is smooth (infinitely differentiable) at the point in question. Near a curvature singularity—where the Riemann curvature tensor components diverge—the notion of a unique tangent space breaks down and the directional derivative becomes ill-defined. For extended bodies (not point particles), the assumption that vectors live in an infinitesimal tangent space fails when tidal forces become comparable to internal forces, so the body must be treated as an extended object with internal stress rather than a collection of freely floating points.

The smoothness requirement also fails at coordinate singularities if you choose coordinates that blow up there, though a different coordinate choice can restore the tangent space's existence. The true physical failure is at genuine singularities (like r=0 in Schwarzschild) where spacetime itself has a boundary or a discontinuity, and tangent vectors cannot be continuously extended through the singular point.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without tangent spaces and fields, you cannot state what a vector or measurement even means on curved spacetime. Every physical quantity that depends on direction—velocity showing how fast and which way, acceleration showing how velocity changes, force acting on an object, spin of a particle—becomes ambiguous and locked into coordinate choices. You lose the language needed to separate what the geometry itself demands from what your arbitrary map merely imposed. Gravity, light, matter, and waves cease to have intrinsic meaning at spacetime events; instead, they become shadows of coordinate artifacts. The entire foundation of relativity collapses: physics becomes merely a coordinate trick, not a statement about the real world. Without this language, you cannot even write the equations of motion or say why an object falls the way it does.

Where It Sits In The Course

Tangent spaces follow logically after learning multilinear algebra and the definition of smoothness. Tangent vectors as directional derivatives come next, followed by fields as coordinate-free assignments. Tensor components then explain why a single vector or tensor object can wear many different names depending on which coordinate system you choose. Connections will later introduce rules for comparing and sliding vectors between different points. The metric will add a measurement structure. Together, they form the complete language of curved spacetime.

Connective Thread

Tangent spaces arrive after multilinear algebra and smoothness are established. Next come tangent vectors as derivatives and fields as coordinate-free instructions at each point. The narrative thread is: objects that don't depend on your coordinate choice allow you to make statements about motion and change that are universally true. This coordinate-independent language is what makes general relativity coherent and lets us describe physics that depends only on spacetime itself, not on arbitrary labels we assign.

Tutorial Pressure Tests

Tutorial 5: Tangent Spaces

Pressure test: Use a tangent vector by letting it act on functions. The tutorial pressure is to stop relying on a drawn arrow outside the manifold and instead ask what directional rate the vector assigns to each local function.

Why it changes this concept: This makes tangent space internal. Motion, light direction, and observer velocity can now be explained without borrowing a surrounding flat space, because the direction is known by what it does at the point. That also prepares the later field picture: a field is built from these local actions point by point.

The selected span states that a tangent vector maps a function on the manifold to a real number. That sentence is the reason the page avoids the little-arrow picture as the main explanation. A direction is understood by the rates it gives to functions, which makes tangent vectors internal to the manifold rather than imported from an outside space.

Tutorial 6: Fields

Pressure test: Build a field through bundle language: base, total space, projection, fiber, and section. The tutorial pressure is to make a field choose one allowed object above each base point, then check that projecting it back returns the point it came from.

Why it changes this concept: This changes fields from a vague substance picture into pointwise assignment with bookkeeping. It explains why vector fields, tensor fields, matter fields, and observer fields all need a bundle-level home before they can be differentiated or compared.

The manual span is the definition check: base space, total space, projection, fiber, and section. That is why the page does not describe a field as something poured over space. It describes a field as a section, a rule choosing one allowed object upstairs over each point downstairs while the projection returns the point it came from.
