Tangent Spaces and Fields

You need to describe motion at each event in spacetime—what direction a particle could move next. You also need to assign local measurements like temperature or field strength to every point. Without a language for "possible next steps" and "point-by-point quantities," physics has no way to express local physics.

Naive Picture

Imagine spacetime as a curved surface, like a balloon or a hill. Tangent vectors are little arrows sticking out from the surface, the way an ant's legs touch the ground at each location.

Why It Fails

Spacetime is not embedded in a larger flat space. There is no outside page to draw arrows on. Tangent directions must be built from the world's own structure, not smuggled in from an invisible container.

Mathematical Object

A tangent space at one point is the set of all infinitesimal directions you can move, built from how functions change along possible curves. A field assigns a number, vector, or tensor to every point, smoothly so nearby values stay close to each other.

Operation

Follow a curve through spacetime. At each point, the tangent vector to that curve is its local direction. Differentiate any smooth function (temperature, height, pressure) along that tangent direction: you get the rate of change. Repeat at every point across spacetime and you have a working field operation.

Why It Matters

Particles move along tangent directions to their worldlines. Light travels along special null tangent directions where the spacetime interval—the measure built from the metric—vanishes. Gravity works by comparing vectors at different points using a connection. Matter and physical fields are tensors (fields of vectors and higher objects) at each event in spacetime.

What Breaks Without It

Velocity becomes meaningless without tangent spaces—it hides behind coordinate choices. Local measurements like density or temperature evaporate into observer preferences. Physics conflates what spacetime does with what your chosen map does.

Mini Example

On a curved hiking trail, consider one specific spot on the path. The possible next steps—uphill, along the contour, downhill, and all directions between—form a tangent space at that point. At the same spot, you measure wind speed and direction, air temperature, barometric pressure, the slope—each a field value assigned to that location. Walk to an adjacent spot on the trail. The tangent space rotates and tilts because the ground itself has changed direction. The field values also shift—the air is cooler at higher altitude, the wind has shifted. This behavior works at any point on any landscape without needing to flatten the mountain into a plane.

Common Confusion

A field is not inherently a force or influence. It is any smoothly assigned quantity: a scalar field (temperature or density), a vector field (wind or electric force), a metric (a rule for measuring distance), or an abstract tensor.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine knowing only what happens at your feet and along the path ahead. You need two languages: one for "what motions are possible here," another for "how physical quantities vary across the world." The tempting thought is that these live in some invisible surrounding space. Wrong. There is no surrounding space. Instead, tangent directions come from the most basic fact: smooth functions change along curves. Pick a curve; any smooth quantity (height, temperature, clock reading) changes. That infinitesimal change defines the tangent direction. All possible infinitesimal directions form the tangent space. A field then says: at each location, here is a value—a number, a vector, a rule for measurement. Neither tangent spaces nor fields need an outside container. Both come from spacetime's own structure.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The tangent space T_p at point p is the vector space of all directional derivatives—rates of change along possible directions. A scalar field assigns a real number to each point; a vector field assigns to each point p a vector in its tangent space T_p; a tensor field assigns a multilinear object at each point. All fields must be smooth: values at nearby points stay close. This smoothness ensures physics is intrinsic—depends only on spacetime structure, never on arbitrary coordinate choices.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without tangent spaces and fields, you cannot even ask what a vector means on curved spacetime. Every quantity that depends on direction—velocity, acceleration, force, and spin—becomes ambiguous and coordinate-dependent. You lose the language to distinguish what the geometry itself declares from what your arbitrary choice of map merely imposed. Gravity, light, matter, and waves lose their intrinsic physical meaning at spacetime events. The entire theoretical foundation crumbles: relativity becomes merely a coordinate trick, not a real statement about physics.

Where It Sits In The Course

Tangent spaces anchor intrinsic geometry; tensor-components-vs-object teaches that one vector has many names depending on coordinate choice. Fields extend tangent vectors to all of spacetime. Connections later introduce rules for comparing vectors across points; the metric adds measurement structure.

Connective Thread

Tangent spaces arrive after multilinear algebra and smoothness are solid. Next come tangent vectors as derivatives and fields as local instructions. The through-line: intrinsic objects enable coordinate-free statements about motion and change, which make general relativity coherent.

Tutorial Pressure Tests

Tutorial 5: Tangent Spaces

Pressure test: Use a tangent vector by letting it act on functions. The tutorial pressure is to stop relying on a drawn arrow outside the manifold and instead ask what directional rate the vector assigns to each local function.

Why it changes this concept: This makes tangent space internal. Motion, light direction, and observer velocity can now be explained without borrowing a surrounding flat space, because the direction is known by what it does at the point. That also prepares the later field picture: a field is built from these local actions point by point.

The selected span states that a tangent vector maps a function on the manifold to a real number. That sentence is the reason the page avoids the little-arrow picture as the main explanation. A direction is understood by the rates it gives to functions, which makes tangent vectors internal to the manifold rather than imported from an outside space.

Tutorial 6: Fields

Pressure test: Build a field through bundle language: base, total space, projection, fiber, and section. The tutorial pressure is to make a field choose one allowed object above each base point, then check that projecting it back returns the point it came from.

Why it changes this concept: This changes fields from a vague substance picture into pointwise assignment with bookkeeping. It explains why vector fields, tensor fields, matter fields, and observer fields all need a bundle-level home before they can be differentiated or compared.

The manual span is the definition check: base space, total space, projection, fiber, and section. That is why the page does not describe a field as something poured over space. It describes a field as a section, a rule choosing one allowed object upstairs over each point downstairs while the projection returns the point it came from.
