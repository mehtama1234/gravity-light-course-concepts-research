Redshift As Stretched Light

You measure starlight in a telescope and count the wave peaks in time. When the star is far away in an expanding universe, the waves arrive stretched. The wavelength is longer, the frequency lower. The light is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. You need to know: is this redshift because the star is moving away, or because space itself is expanding between you and the star, or because the star is in strong gravity? These look identical in the data.

Naive Picture

The natural first thought is to blame motion. A siren moving away makes sound waves stretch in the air because the source keeps running away as each wave cycle completes—the Doppler effect. If redshift is just light doing the same thing, the math works and you move on. Stretched waves mean the source recedes.

Why It Fails

Pure motion fails the moment you leave a small local region. In cosmology, galaxies are not flying through space at billions of miles per hour; rather, the metric itself—the ruler you use to measure distance—is expanding. The wavelength gets longer not because the source moved but because the distances between peaks grew while the light was traveling. In strong gravity near a black hole, a clock near the horizon ticks slowly compared to one far away. Light emitted from the slow clock looks redshifted to the distant observer even if nothing is moving at all.

Mathematical Object

The redshift is a pure number you compute by comparing light at two moments: when it left the emitter and when it arrived at the observer. You take the wavelength (or frequency) at those two events, write them as components of a vector that points along the light ray in spacetime, and compute their ratio. Formally, if the light ray carries a wave vector k, redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured in the local frame of time at that event. It does not live in one place; it is a relation across spacetime.

Operation

Write down the light ray as a path through spacetime from emitter to observer. At emission, write the wave vector as a quantity aligned with the emitter's notion of time. At observation, do the same in the observer's frame. Now compute the ratio: k_at_observer / k_at_emitter. That ratio—call it (1+z)—encodes everything the geometry did to the light between the two events. The operation is pure measurement: you do not move the light or change the spacetime. You just read off what the metric says the frequencies were.

Why It Matters

This is the bridge from abstract geometry to data. The metric tells you how time flows in each location. Different flow rates mean light frequencies shift. By computing redshift carefully—using the metric and the light ray path—you can decode what the metric was doing from what you measure in the telescope. This is how cosmology tests whether space is expanding. This is how you confirm that time runs slower in strong gravity. Redshift is the instrument that makes spacetime geometry observable.

What Breaks Without It

Without a disciplined definition of redshift rooted in the metric and the light path, you cannot tell motion from geometry. You misread cosmological observations: you might think distant galaxies are flying away at light speed when in fact the metric is expanding slowly. You cannot distinguish gravitational redshift (caused by geometry) from Doppler redshift (caused by motion). The whole experimental test of general relativity—comparing predictions to telescope data—falls apart because you have no precise way to say what the theory predicts you will measure.

Mini Example

Imagine a simple universe: a uniform grid of space expanding uniformly over time, like a rubber sheet being stretched. A light source at position x=0 emits a wave with wavelength λ₀ at time t=0. The light travels outward. At time t=T, the grid has doubled in size—every distance is twice what it was. The light has traveled distance d in the old units, but is now at position 2d in the new units. When it arrives, the wavelength has also doubled to λ=2λ₀. The observer measures redshift z=(λ-λ₀)/λ₀=1. Nothing moved; the space expanded, stretching the light.

Common Confusion

Many people think redshift has one cause—either motion, or expansion, or gravity—and you choose which one applies. In reality, all three are present in any real situation, and the redshift number measures their combined effect. A galaxy receding due to expansion, in its own local gravitational well, with peculiar motion relative to the cosmic flow, will show redshift from all three causes blended together. You cannot separate them without additional information (like knowing the galaxy's distance from other measurements). Redshift itself is not the cause; it is the faithful meter that reads what the metric actually does to light.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine looking through a telescope at a distant galaxy. The light that reaches you today left that galaxy billions of years ago. You measure the light's spectrum—its colors, or equivalently, the frequencies of its waves. You find the wavelength is longer than expected, shifted toward red. Your first impulse is to blame motion, like a siren wailing as it recedes. But this impulse hides a choice: it assumes space is fixed. In an expanding universe, space is not fixed; the metric is stretching. The distance between any two points grows over time. As light travels through this expanding space, the peaks of its waves get pulled farther apart, just as a tape measure stretched over a balloon would show marks farther and farther apart. This is not motion through space; it is space itself enlarging. Now, how do you measure this precisely? You need a definition that works in any spacetime, not just in the simple example. The metric—the local ruler that tells you how to measure distances and time intervals—is your tool. At the moment the star emitted the light, the star's local clock was ticking at a certain rate, encoding the star's concept of time. At the moment you detect the light, your clock ticks at its own rate. The light wave was stamped with the frequency of the emitter's clock and arrives marked with a different frequency because your clock ticks differently. That difference is encoded in the spacetime metric between the emitter and you. Computing redshift means comparing those two frequencies using the metric and the light path that connects them. This single number—1+z—tells you everything the geometry did: whether space expanded, whether you sat in stronger gravity, whether the source was moving, or some mixture of all three. Without this definition, you cannot extract geometry from observations.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Redshift is defined using the wave vector of light, which is a quantity that encodes both the direction light travels and the frequency of its oscillations. In spacetime, this wave vector is k = (ω/c, k⃗), where ω is the angular frequency and k⃗ is the spatial direction. What matters is that k is a tangent vector—a direction and magnitude living in spacetime at a point. The metric g tells you how to compute the length of tangent vectors and how to compare vectors at different points. The redshift formula is 1+z = (g(u_obs, k)) / (g(u_emit, k)), where u is the four-velocity of the observer and emitter respectively, and g(u,k) computes their inner product using the metric. This looks abstract, but it says something simple: you are comparing how much the emitter's notion of time aligns with the light wave at emission, versus how much the observer's notion of time aligns with the same wave at arrival. If the observers are moving at the same velocity and the metric is the same everywhere, this ratio is 1 and z=0 (no redshift). If the observer is moving away, z>0. If the observer is in stronger gravity where time runs slow, z>0. The formula does not separate these causes; it measures their combined effect.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Redshift is the foundation of observational relativity. General relativity makes predictions about the metric—how spacetime is curved by matter and energy. But you cannot see the metric directly. You only see light. To test the theory against experiments, you must connect predictions about the metric to predictions about redshift, and then compare to telescope observations. Without a clear definition of redshift rooted in the metric, the theory is a abstract game with no contact to the real world. With it, every observation becomes a test: is the universe really expanding as the theory predicts? does light slow down near massive objects as expected? The stakes are not just technical refinement; they are whether we can trust the theory at all.

Where It Sits In The Course

Redshift sits at the center of a web: geodesics describe how light and matter move through spacetime; the metric defines those geodesics and also defines how frequencies transform between observers; the Einstein equation relates the metric to the distribution of matter; observations of redshift in real galaxies and stars test whether the metric the Einstein equation predicted is actually the one we live in. It is the language that translates between abstract geometry and experimental data.

Connective Thread

The earlier concepts supply the ingredients: geodesics explain light paths in curved spacetime; the metric explains how to measure anything locally; the Einstein equation explains what metric to expect from matter. This concept packages those pieces into a single observable: redshift. The later concepts—cosmology, gravitational waves, black holes—all depend on redshift as the way to read off what the spacetime metric is actually doing. Without this concept, each later topic would need to invent its own idiosyncratic measurement recipe, and you would lose the unified view that the metric controls everything we observe.
