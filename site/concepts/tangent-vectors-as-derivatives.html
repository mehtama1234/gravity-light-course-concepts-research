Tangent Vectors As Derivatives

You stand on a hillside and want to measure how temperature changes as you step in one direction versus another. But what exactly is 'a direction at a point'? In Einstein's picture, spacetime—the thing that makes up everything—isn't sitting inside some larger flat container. There is no external reference frame to draw arrows from. So you need a way to define what 'a direction' even means using only what is built into spacetime itself.

Naive Picture

From high school, a direction looks simple: draw a tiny arrow pointing the way you want to go, like a compass needle touching a map. Measure the change in temperature along that arrow's direction. Arrows are concrete, easy to visualize, and feel like the right answer.

Why It Fails

Drawing an arrow works when your space is flat, like a sheet of paper or a flat field—there is room all around it to draw from. But spacetime curves everywhere, and there is no room outside it. An arrow drawn from outside the space tells you nothing about the space's true shape. You would be smuggling in extra structure—a flat external space—that does not actually exist. It breaks the whole point: Einstein's discovery was that gravity IS the shape of spacetime, not a force acting inside some preexisting flat stage.

Mathematical Object

A tangent vector is a rule—a machine—that takes any smooth function defined on the space and outputs a number: how fast that function is changing along a chosen direction at a specific point. The machine has no picture, no arrow. It is purely intrinsic to the space; you build it from the space's own structure using calculus. The rule encodes both 'which point' and 'which direction' through the way it measures change in functions.

Operation

To use a tangent vector, hand it a smooth function—say, temperature T defined everywhere on spacetime. The tangent vector spits out the rate of change of T along its direction at its point. You can verify what tangent vector you have by testing it on many different functions: the same point, the same direction, but different inputs. The tangent vector always follows two rules: adding two functions gives the sum of the outputs, and the product rule from calculus holds—exactly like taking a derivative.

Why It Matters

When you move, your velocity is a tangent vector—it tells you how your position coordinates change with passing time. Light rays travel along specific tangent vectors called null directions. Electric and magnetic field strengths point along tangent vectors. If you only had external arrows—not intrinsic directions living inside spacetime—you could not write any equation for motion or fields without assuming spacetime sits inside some external flat space. But it does not.

What Breaks Without It

If tangent vectors are just external arrows, you are forced to pretend spacetime sits in a larger flat environment, like spacetime is a curved surface floating in higher-dimensional flat space. Then the idea of intrinsic geometry—the true shape of spacetime itself—dissolves into nonsense. Curvature becomes just arithmetic about coordinates, and Einstein's equation has no stage to live on. The whole discovery—that gravity is curvature, not a force—falls apart.

Mini Example

Imagine a temperature field T spread across the curved surface of a sphere. Pick a spot (say, the North Pole) and a direction (say, downward toward the equator along a longitude line). The tangent vector in that direction works like a derivative machine: feed it T and it outputs a number—how many degrees hotter per mile as you walk that way. Now feed the same tangent vector a different function—air pressure P. It outputs a different number: pressure change per mile in the same direction. The tangent vector is one fixed direction and point, but it gives different output numbers for different functions. That is the whole point: the tangent vector *is* the rule, not the arrow.

Common Confusion

Many people think a tangent vector is a regular arrow with a size and direction—a vector like in high school physics. It is not. Arrows, magnitudes, and directions only appear later, once you add a metric—a ruler that defines distances and times in spacetime. The tangent vector *by itself* is just the derivative rule: what it does to functions. Two tangent vectors are identical if and only if they give the same rate-of-change number for every function, no matter what coordinate system you use or what ruler you choose.

First-Principles Walkthrough

You are standing on a hillside and notice temperature changes as you walk. Walk down, it gets warmer. Walk sideways, it stays about the same. Intuitively, a direction at a point is an arrow. But if spacetime curves everywhere and has nothing around it, where would you draw this arrow? The answer: stop thinking of arrows. Instead, a direction at a point is a rule—a measuring stick—that you apply to any function. Test it on temperature: it tells you the temperature slope that way. Test it on pressure: it tells you the pressure slope. Test it on height: it tells you the height slope. The rule always works the same way: it respects addition (the slope of a sum is the sum of slopes) and multiplication (product rule from calculus applies). This rule *is* the tangent vector. It is intrinsic—it lives entirely inside the space itself, asks no external reference frame, and captures exactly what 'a direction with a rate' means on a curved manifold. Now, inside spacetime, you have a way to talk about velocity, light rays, and fields without sneaking in a flat background.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A tangent vector v at a point p is a rule that eats smooth functions and outputs numbers. If f is any smooth function on the space, then v(f) is a real number computed as follows: the rule respects linearity—if you feed it a mixture a·f + b·g (where a and b are constants), the output is a·v(f) + b·v(g)—and it obeys the product rule—v(f·g) = v(f)·g(p) + f(p)·v(g). These two rules are the exact definition of a derivative operator, imported straight from calculus. When you use coordinates x¹, x², ... on the space and write the function as f(x¹, x², ...), and the tangent vector as v with components v^i, then v(f) = v^i times the partial derivative of f with respect to x^i—the directional derivative from calculus. But here is the crucial point: the tangent vector v itself does not depend on which coordinates you choose; the rule it encodes is the same no matter which coordinate system you use to label the points.

Where This Idea Itself Breaks Down

Tangent vectors as derivatives fail at singular points of the manifold where the smoothness assumption breaks down. At a cusp, a corner, or a pinch (like the apex of a cone), there is no unique tangent space. The tangent vector as a 'machine that eats smooth functions' presumes the manifold is smooth enough that you can define derivatives. If the manifold is not differentiable at a point, no tangent vectors exist there, and the intrinsic directional machinery fails.

More concretely: at a black-hole singularity or at the point where a wormhole throat pinches off, the manifold is no longer smooth. Attempting to define a tangent vector as a directional derivative of a function yields multiple incompatible results depending on how you approach the singularity, or the derivatives are undefined altogether. A physicist reaching this point would find that geodesics terminate, and smooth motion cannot be extended through the singular point.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

All of differential geometry rests on one ability: talking about how things change at a point without assuming the space sits in a flat external container. If you defined tangent vectors any other way—as arrows imported from outside, or as just a list of numbers without the rule—then parallel transport (moving vectors along curves while keeping them 'straight'), curvature (bending of space), and the Einstein equation (gravity as shape) would all crumble. They would become just coordinate illusions. This definition anchors everything: tangent vectors are local operators that live in the manifold itself, built from the space's own calculus. That is why Einstein's theory works.

Connective Thread

Earlier pages built up smooth manifolds and functions on them—the language for spacetime. This page answers a hard question: how do you define a direction, a slope, a rate of change inside a curved space with no external reference? The answer: through functions and their derivatives, using only tools that live inside the space. Everything that comes next—velocity and acceleration, parallel transport, curvature, the metric, the full Einstein equation—stands on this foundation. Without an intrinsic definition of tangent vector, you could never separate what is truly happening to spacetime from what is merely an artifact of your choice of coordinates. With it, relativity becomes a coherent story: gravity is the shape of spacetime itself, and that shape is written in the language of derivatives.
