Vector Fields As Local Instructions

Imagine a landscape where at every point—a mountain summit, a valley floor, anywhere—there is a direction you can move or a velocity you inherit. You need language to assign one direction per location and track how directions change as you move. That is the problem: how do you capture motion or instruction that varies smoothly from place to place?

Naive Picture

Picture a field as a color or fog diffused through space, like heat spreading from a fire. At each point the fog has a density; the field tells you how dense it is there. This is intuitive and useful for scalar fields like temperature.

Why It Fails

A fog is a physical substance; it has material properties and can flow. But a vector field that assigns motion or direction is not made of anything. It is a rule: at each point, you have one vector (a direction and magnitude). The field is the collection of all those vectors, not a stuff occupying space.

Mathematical Object

A vector field is a smooth assignment: to each point on a manifold (a curved surface or spacetime), it assigns exactly one tangent vector (a tiny arrow indicating direction and speed at that point). Smooth means nearby points get nearby vectors, with no sudden jumps. It is a function that lives on the manifold itself, with no reference to an embedding or background space.

Operation

To use a vector field, you follow the arrows. Start at a point, step along the direction the field points, arrive at a new point, read the field's arrow there, step again. This trace is called a flow line or integral curve. Alternatively, you can use the field as a machine: give it another quantity (like temperature), and it tells you the rate of change along the flow line.

Why It Matters

General relativity says gravity is not a force but curvature of spacetime itself. Observers and matter are not separate from spacetime—they move along curves determined by the geometry. All of this is encoded as vector fields: the field of velocities of observers, the field of matter flow, the field of symmetry directions that leave the geometry unchanged. Without vector fields as a language, you cannot describe how observers or matter vary across a region.

What Breaks Without It

If you remove vector fields, you lose the ability to talk about continuous families of observers, the flow of matter through a region, or directions of symmetry. You would be forced to describe only single points or discrete data, losing the local, differential character that makes the subject work. Curvature itself is defined through how vectors change along paths—impossible without this foundation.

Mini Example

Consider a spinning disk. At each radius r, the tangential velocity increases: v(r) = ωr, where ω is the spin rate. You can draw an arrow at every point showing the local velocity—tangent to the circle, longer farther from the center. This is a vector field. If you trace a flow line, you move in circles; the field tells you how fast. If you ask "how much does the velocity change if I move radially outward," the field gives you the rate: dv/dr = ω. The entire tangential velocity history is encoded in one field assignment.

Common Confusion

A vector field is often confused with a force field or an energy field. But a vector field is any smooth assignment of a vector to each point—it is a purely geometric language. It can represent a force (like gravity), a velocity (how particles move), a direction (which way is time), or any other instruction. The field itself says nothing about whether it represents a force, energy, or constraint; that meaning comes from the equations of motion or how the field couples to matter. Thinking "field means force" blocks understanding.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you are walking across a landscape where the terrain has a slope that varies from place to place. At the summit, the slope points downhill in one direction. In the valley, slopes point uphill toward the rim. You could carry a map with an arrow at every location showing which way the steepest descent goes. That map is a vector field—a smooth assignment of one direction per place. Now imagine you want to describe how air flows over that landscape, or how gravity bends a path. You need the same idea: at each point in space or spacetime, specify a velocity or a direction of motion, and do this smoothly so nearby points have nearby velocities. A tangent vector (a tiny arrow) at each point is the mathematical name for that direction. The field collects all these arrows into one object. You use it by following the arrows to trace how something moves, or by asking it questions like "at this point, what is the rate of change of a quantity if I flow along the field?" This operation is not just notation—it ensures you can describe motion and change without sneaking in hidden assumptions about which direction is absolute or preferred.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A vector field V on a manifold M assigns to each point p on M one tangent vector V(p). Tangency means the vector lives in the tangent space at p—the space of all directions you can move from that point. Smoothness is captured by saying: if you pick local coordinates (x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ) around a point, then V can be written as V = V¹(x₁,...,xₙ) ∂/∂x₁ + V²(x₁,...,xₙ) ∂/∂x₂ + .... Each coefficient Vⁱ is a smooth function (no jumps, derivatives exist). The symbol ∂/∂xᵢ is a basis direction in the tangent space—the direction you move if only xᵢ changes. The operation of following the field is solving the ordinary differential equation: dp/dt = V(p). If p(t) solves this, then p(t) is a flow line tracing the field's instruction.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Vector fields are the language in which local geometry and local physics are stated. Without them, you cannot write "at this point, matter has this velocity" or "spacetime curves in this direction" in a way that makes sense on a curved manifold. If you skip this foundation, every later concept—connections, curvature, the field equations, observers, geodesics—becomes either a formal exercise or a hidden coordinatization. The course insists on this foundation precisely so that later when you compute a quantity in one coordinate system and then transform to another, you can verify that the true physical content has not changed, only your description of it.

Tutorial Pressure Tests

Tutorial 6: Fields

Pressure test: Build a field through bundle language: base, total space, projection, fiber, and section. The tutorial pressure is to make a field choose one allowed object above each base point, then check that projecting it back returns the point it came from.

Why it changes this concept: This changes fields from a vague substance picture into pointwise assignment with bookkeeping. It explains why vector fields, tensor fields, matter fields, and observer fields all need a bundle-level home before they can be differentiated or compared.

The manual span is the definition check: base space, total space, projection, fiber, and section. That is why the page does not describe a field as something poured over space. It describes a field as a section, a rule choosing one allowed object upstairs over each point downstairs while the projection returns the point it came from.
