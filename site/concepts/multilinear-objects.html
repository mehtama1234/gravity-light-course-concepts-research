Multilinear Algebra

Imagine measuring the force on a stretched rubber sheet when you push in two directions at once. The force depends on both your push directions together, not one at a time. Physics constantly needs to describe how something responds to multiple inputs — directions, slopes, flows — and keep track of which inputs matter and how they combine.

Naive Picture

All quantities in physics are just vectors or matrices, with numbers filling slots. You add them, multiply them, decompose them into coordinates, and everything works the same way.

Why It Fails

Different quantities do fundamentally different jobs in spacetime. A velocity vector points in a direction. A gravitational field measures how much a path bends. A force-per-area combines two perpendicular directions — one for the surface orientation, one for the push direction. Treating them all as interchangeable hides these jobs and makes it impossible to ask whether two things are really equal.

Mathematical Object

A multilinear object is a rule that eats a specific list of vectors and covectors (covectors are measuring sticks that live in the dual space) and spits out a number or another object. The rule must be linear in each input separately: if you double one input, the output doubles; if you add two inputs, the output is the sum of two separate outputs. The shape and number of input slots are part of what defines the object.

Operation

You feed vectors and covectors into the slots in the right order, one at a time. At each slot, linearity makes the calculation predictable. You can also combine slots—when a vector slot and a covector slot both get filled, they can annihilate each other (called contraction), which reduces the number of empty slots. This turns one multilinear object into another with fewer or different slots.

Why It Matters

The Einstein equation equates two complicated objects: the metric (which measures distances) and the stress-energy tensor (which describes how matter and energy are distributed). Both must have the same input slots to make the equation meaningful. Without understanding multilinearity—what each object's slots do—you cannot see why these are the right things to equate, or what breaking the equation even means.

What Breaks Without It

If you treat all mathematical objects as interchangeable containers of numbers, you lose the ability to check whether a statement is coordinate-independent. A calculation in Cartesian coordinates might give a different-looking number than the same calculation in spherical coordinates, not because spacetime changed, but because you wrote down something that only makes sense in one coordinate system. Without multilinear discipline, you cannot tell whether that's a bug or a feature.

Mini Example

A parcel delivery costs depend on weight (in kilograms) and distance (in kilometers). If a package weighs 2 kg and travels 100 km, the cost is f(2, 100). If you double the weight to 4 kg, the cost becomes f(4, 100) = 2 × f(2, 100). If you double the distance to 200 km, the cost becomes f(2, 200) = 2 × f(2, 100). This is linearity in each slot. But the total cost for two separate packages is not f(2, 100) + f(3, 50)—that only makes sense if the shipping rule itself is multilinear in that way. When it is, you can predict the cost of any combination from the cost of base cases.

Common Confusion

Many people think a tensor IS its grid of numbers in a given coordinate system. It is not. The grid is just a coordinate picture, useful for calculation but not the object itself. The tensor is the multilinear rule: the machine that eats vectors and covectors, obeying linearity in each slot, and hands out numbers. A different coordinate system gives a different grid that represents the same tensor. The numbers change; the tensor does not.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Physics describes how things respond to multiple pushes and measurements at once. A rubber sheet pushed in two perpendicular directions gives a response that depends on both directions together. A curved surface has curvature, which is not a single number but a measuring device: you feed it two tangent vectors (directions at a point on the surface) and get back a number telling you how much the surface curves in that plane. These objects all share a critical property: they are linear in each input separately. Double one input, double the output; add two inputs, add two outputs from each. But different objects have different numbers of input slots, and that difference matters—it is not just bookkeeping but a statement about what the object measures. The metric has two slots (for two directions, to measure the angle and distance between them). Curvature has four slots (two directions defining a plane, two more for the tangent vectors that would be parallel-transported). A vector has one slot (it eats a measuring stick and tells you its component in that direction). Treating these as the same would be like confusing a question ('Is it raining?') with a two-part question ('Is it raining today and tomorrow?'). The language that captures this is multilinearity: the discipline of tracking exactly how many input slots each object has, what kind of object lives in each slot, and how the output behaves when you feed in linear combinations. Without this clarity, the equations of general relativity become unmoored—you can write down symbols that look right without knowing whether they are truly saying something about spacetime or just about your choice of coordinates.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A multilinear object T with, say, two vector slots and one covector slot can be written T(v₁, v₂, ω), where v₁ and v₂ are vectors, ω is a covector, and the output is a number. Multilinearity means T(av₁ + bv₁', v₂, ω) = aT(v₁, v₂, ω) + bT(v₁', v₂, ω) and so on for the other slots. In coordinates, if v₁ = vⁱ eᵢ and v₂ = vʲ eⱼ and ω = ωₖ dxᵏ (where eᵢ are basis vectors and dxᵏ are basis covectors), then T(v₁, v₂, ω) = vⁱ vʲ ωₖ T(eᵢ, eⱼ, dxᵏ) = vⁱ vʲ ωₖ Tⁱʲₖ. The components Tⁱʲₖ change in a predictable way when you change coordinates: if you apply a coordinate transformation with matrix A, the components transform as T'ᵃᵇ𝒄 = (A⁻¹)ᵃᵢ (A⁻¹)ᵇⱼ Aₖ𝒄 Tⁱʲₖ. This law—not the numbers themselves—is the invariant content. The metric gᵢⱼ and curvature Rᵢⱼₖₗ and stress-energy Tμν are all tensors obeying these transformation rules.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

The Einstein equation Gμν = (8πG/c⁴)Tμν says that the left side (curvature, packed into the Einstein tensor) equals the right side (matter and energy, packed into the stress-energy tensor). Both sides must be the same kind of object (same number of slots, same transformation law) for this to be a meaningful statement about spacetime rather than a coincidence of coordinates. If you do not understand multilinearity, you cannot explain why these two things belong on opposite sides of an equation. The whole edifice—how gravity is encoded, how matter sources curvature, how light bends—depends on this foundation.

Where It Sits In The Course

Charts and atlases taught you how to label points on a curved space with coordinates. Manifolds and local flatness taught you that a curved surface looks flat when you zoom in. Dual vectors taught you that covectors measure components of vectors—they live in a paired space. Multilinear objects are the natural language for describing anything that takes multiple measurements (vectors and covectors) as input and produces output in a consistent, coordinate-independent way. When you later meet the metric (measuring distances), curvature (measuring bending), and stress-energy (measuring matter), you will recognize them all as multilinear objects doing different jobs with different numbers of slots.

Connective Thread

You learned how to describe a single direction (a vector) and a single measuring stick (a covector). Those are objects with one slot each. Now you are learning the language for anything with multiple slots: how to combine them, how they transform between coordinates, what it means for them to be equal. The next step is to see tensors not just as abstract objects but as fields—a different multilinear object at each point on spacetime, varying smoothly as you move. That leads to derivatives of tensors, which build the covariant derivative and the curvature that will finally make Einstein's equation come alive.
