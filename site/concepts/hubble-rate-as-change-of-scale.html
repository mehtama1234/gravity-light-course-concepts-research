Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and see that galaxies are getting farther apart over time. But just knowing the distance grows is not enough—you need a way to ask: *how fast* is space expanding right now? Is it doubling every billion years or every trillion years? That difference is everything for predicting the future of the universe.

Naive Picture

The natural first thought is that the Hubble rate is simply how fast galaxies are flying away through empty space, like a speed you could measure with a radar gun. The farther a galaxy sits, the faster it zooms backward. Space itself is flat and unchanging; only the galaxies move through it.

Why It Fails

This picture fails because we are not measuring one galaxy's velocity in a fixed background. Galaxies are *not* moving through space—space itself is expanding, stretching the distance between them like a rubber sheet being pulled. The quantity we actually measure from redshifts is how the *scale* of the universe as a whole is changing, not the velocity of any single object through a container.

Mathematical Object

The Hubble rate is the fractional rate of change of the scale factor—a number that describes how much bigger the universe is now than at an earlier time. If the scale factor (call it $a(t)$) has grown from value $a_0$ to value $a_1$ over a time $\Delta t$, the Hubble rate is how fast $a$ is changing *relative to its current size*: $H = (1/a) \, da/dt$. It answers the question: per unit time, what fraction of the current cosmic size is being added?

Operation

To find the Hubble rate from observations: measure the redshift of a galaxy (how much light from it is stretched), convert that to a recession speed using the Doppler formula, then divide by the distance. The result is a number per unit time—often quoted in units like inverse megayears. That number tells you how fast the scale factor is changing relative to itself *right now*.

Why It Matters

In general relativity, gravity is not a force pulling objects through space—it is the geometry of spacetime itself. The Hubble rate is the fundamental quantity that measures whether spacetime geometry is stretching or contracting on cosmic scales. It is the observable signature of Einstein's equation acting on a universe full of matter and energy. Knowing the Hubble rate lets you write down what the metric—the mathematical object that describes distances and times—looks like, and thus what gravity is doing.

What Breaks Without It

Without a way to measure fractional expansion, you cannot connect the matter and energy in the universe to how spacetime curves. You cannot predict whether expansion will speed up or slow down. You cannot test whether observations match the predictions of general relativity on the largest scales. The entire bridge between what we measure (redshifts and distances) and what the theory predicts (the geometry of spacetime) collapses.

Mini Example

Suppose at time $t=0$, the scale factor $a = 1$ and the Hubble rate is $H = 0.1$ per billion years. At time $t = 1$ billion years later, the scale factor is approximately $a \approx 1.1$. The fractional increase is $(1.1 - 1)/1 = 10\%$, which matches $H \times \Delta t = 0.1 \times 1 = 0.1 = 10\%$. A galaxy that was 1 billion light-years away is now roughly 1.1 billion light-years away. But the Hubble rate is the same ($H \approx 0.1$) because we measure fractional change, not absolute change. If the scale factor were twice as large (say $a=2$), the same $H=0.1$ would mean adding $0.2$ to the scale factor, corresponding to a 10% fractional growth—both are expansion at the *same rate*.

Common Confusion

Many people think the Hubble rate is the same as the speed at which a distant galaxy is receding. This conflates two different things. The Hubble rate is a *ratio* (speed divided by distance), expressed as a fraction per unit time. Two galaxies at different distances recede at different speeds, but they both reflect the same underlying Hubble rate at that moment. The Hubble rate is not a speed; it is a description of how the metric itself is evolving.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Here is the central insight: when you measure that galaxies are moving away from each other, you are not watching objects fly through a static universe. You are watching the universe's *geometry* expand—the very fabric of space is stretching. This is why the ordinary notion of velocity fails. There is no fixed space to measure velocity *through*. Instead, what is expanding is the *scale* of the universe as a whole, captured by a single function $a(t)$ that tells you how much bigger distances have become. The Hubble rate measures how quickly this scale is growing *relative to how big it already is*—a fractional rate of change. If the scale factor grows by a billion light-years, that means something different depending on whether the universe is 10 billion or 1000 billion light-years across. The Hubble rate corrects for this: it reports growth as a fraction. Observationally, you measure recession speeds and distances, compute the ratio speed/distance, and that number *is* the Hubble rate—the fractional expansion speed right now. This single number, changing only with cosmic time, is the bridge between observations and the spacetime geometry that general relativity describes.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The definition is $H(t) = (1/a(t)) \, (da/dt)$. Read this carefully: $da/dt$ is the absolute rate of change of the scale factor in length units. Dividing by $a(t)$ makes it dimensionless (a pure fraction per unit time). If $a = 10$ billion light-years and $da/dt = 1$ billion light-years per billion years, then $H = (1/10) \times 1 = 0.1$ per billion years. This means the scale factor is growing at 10% per billion years. The Hubble rate is *not* the same everywhere in space (it is the same at all positions at a given moment because the universe is homogeneous), but it *changes* with time as the universe evolves. In a matter-dominated universe, $H$ decreases slowly over time; in a dark-energy-dominated universe, $H$ approaches a constant value. These different behaviors are directly tied to the stress-energy in Einstein's equation, making the Hubble rate the observable window into gravitational dynamics.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without the Hubble rate, you cannot distinguish between what the metric itself is doing and what your choice of coordinates makes it look like you're doing. Relativity demands that real physics be observer-independent. If you lose the Hubble rate, you lose the anchor that ties observations—redshift, distance, recession velocity—back to actual spacetime curvature. The entire theory becomes a coordinate game with no ground truth. Expansion as a physical fact collapses into pure illusion, indistinguishable from a gauge choice.

Where It Sits In The Course

The Hubble rate appears wherever the universe's large-scale structure is the subject: in cosmological models that solve Einstein's equation for matter and energy, in the notion of a cosmic horizon beyond which causal signals cannot reach, in the redshift-distance relations that astronomers use to map the universe. It is the single parameter that encodes the geometry of spacetime in a homogeneous and isotropic cosmos.

Connective Thread

This concept requires understanding the scale factor—how the universe's size is encoded in a single function of time—which earlier pages establish. It then serves as the foundation for predicting cosmic evolution: given the Hubble rate and the matter content, you can solve for the universe's past and future. Later concepts like horizons and Penrose diagrams rely on the Hubble rate to describe causal boundaries and the global structure of spacetime.
