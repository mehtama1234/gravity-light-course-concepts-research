Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints

You want to predict the future of spacetime itself. But not every snapshot of space you imagine can actually occur at the start of a real spacetime. Something has to check whether a snapshot is lawful before you evolve it forward.

Naive Picture

In ordinary mechanics, you pick positions and velocities freely at one moment, then the equations push them forward. The physicist is free: invent a starting state, then run the machine. Why not do the same in relativity?

Why It Fails

In relativity, the geometry and its change are not independent. Four of Einstein's ten equations lock the geometry and its time-rate-of-change to each other at every moment. If you invent a spatial shape and its rate of change without obeying those locks, you have something no real spacetime could ever contain.

Mathematical Object

Initial data is a pair of objects: the shape of one slice of space (a spatial metric—a rule for measuring distances on that slice) and a spatial momentum density (extrinsic curvature—a measure of how space is bending as it evolves). Together they must satisfy two constraint equations: the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint.

Operation

Before using a snapshot as a starting point, you plug the spatial geometry and momentum density into the two constraint equations. If the equations hold everywhere on that slice, the data is lawful. If they fail anywhere, the snapshot cannot be the start of any spacetime.

Why It Matters

General relativity tells us gravity is the shape of spacetime, and that shape evolves according to Einstein's equation. But Einstein's equation has a hidden structure: four pieces are not evolution rules—they are rules that link the shape at one moment to the shape's rate of change. Checking those links before evolution prevents inventing a starting state that contradicts Einstein's own theory.

What Breaks Without It

If you skip the constraint check and evolve any initial data forward using only the six evolution equations, you will compute a solution to half of Einstein's equations. The four constraint equations will not hold along your evolution. Your numerical solution describes fake physics that does not satisfy the full Einstein theory.

Mini Example

Imagine a small sphere of rubber at one moment in spacetime. You measure its shape: it bulges slightly. You measure its rate of deformation: it is expanding. But before you claim this snapshot can evolve into real spacetime, you must check: does this shape and this expansion rate satisfy the constraint equations? If the surface bulges too much for its expansion rate, or expands too fast for its curvature, the constraint fails. The snapshot is forbidden. No spacetime could start this way. If the constraints hold, you may evolve it forward, and Einstein's six evolution equations will obey the four constraints at every later time.

Common Confusion

Many people think constraints are extra equations you use if you are doing advanced numerics, or optional mathematical elegance. In fact, constraints are the reason Einstein's ten equations are self-consistent. Four of them are not about the future. They are about the present. They are already built into Einstein's equation. You cannot ignore them without breaking the theory.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Here is the puzzle: in everyday mechanics you sketch a position and a velocity, and the laws of motion do the rest. In relativity, something is different. The shape of space at one moment and the speed at which that shape changes are not free choices you can make independently. They are locked together by Einstein's own equations—specifically by four of the ten equations that nobody told you were constraints rather than evolution rules. What you really need to start a spacetime prediction is initial data: a spatial geometry (imagine the curved shape of one thin slice) and a spatial momentum density (imagine the squeeze-and-stretch of that shape as it begins to evolve). But not any pair works. Before you hand them to the evolution equations, you must check two constraint equations: the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint. These are the locks. They ask: does this spatial shape, combined with this rate of change, actually satisfy four of Einstein's equations? If yes, you may evolve. If no, the snapshot cannot be real. The reason this matters is subtle but deep: Einstein's equation has more symmetry than a naive list of ten equations suggests. Four of its pieces are not saying what happens next. They are saying what has to be true right now. Ignoring them leads to contradiction. Checking them before evolution is not optional—it is a requirement built into the physics itself.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Einstein's equation is ten tensor equations (tensors are multilinear objects—tools for comparing shapes under rotations and coordinate changes). When you decompose spacetime into slices of space plus time, using what is called the 3+1 split, four of those ten equations contain only spatial derivatives, no time derivatives. These four are the Hamiltonian constraint (relating spatial curvature to energy density) and the momentum constraint (relating how space bends to momentum density). The other six equations are true evolution equations: they tell you how the spatial metric changes in time, given initial data that satisfies the four constraints. In symbols, the constraints are ∇²h ≈ energy and ∂h/∂x ≈ momentum in various component forms, where h is extrinsic curvature, ∇ is a spatial derivative, and the proportionality involves the gravitational constant G and the speed of light c. Without these constraints holding on your initial slice, the evolution equations produce solutions to only six of Einstein's ten equations, not all ten.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

The course builds toward black holes, gravitational waves, and the expanding universe. Each of these is a prediction from Einstein's full equation. If you do not check constraints, your initial data might lead you to compute a solution that violates Einstein's theory at the foundation. In numerical relativity, which is how we predict black-hole mergers and detect gravitational waves, bad initial data without constraint checking creates ghost solutions that look physically reasonable but do not actually satisfy Einstein's equation. The constraints are not a side detail—they are the hidden structure that makes Einstein's equation self-consistent.

Where It Sits In The Course

This concept sits at the heart of how Einstein's equation splits into structure and evolution. It connects directly to the canonical formulation of general relativity (where Hamiltonian and momentum constraints are the pillars) and to numerical relativity (where you must enforce constraints to get physical solutions). It is also the key to understanding cosmology: the constraint equations link the expansion rate to the density of matter at each moment, forming the Friedmann equations that describe an expanding universe.

Connective Thread

To reach this page, you must know how spacetime can be sliced (the 3+1 split introduced in Canonical Formulation and Constraints), and you must see why geometry at one moment and geometry at another moment are not freely combinable (prepared by Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics). You also avoid the trap of later thinking that spacetime can be any smooth shape you invent—it must satisfy Einstein's equation, and that equation's self-consistency depends on constraints being true at every moment.

Pressure test: Put classical geometry and quantum matter on opposite sides of the same proposed equation and ask what kind of object each side is. The evening-lecture pressure is to notice that a definite spacetime source is no longer straightforward when matter is treated quantum mechanically.

Why it changes this concept: This makes quantum gravity a bookkeeping crisis, not a fashionable topic. The concept pages on constraints, source terms, and canonical formulation now point toward the place where classical spacetime stops being obviously self-contained. The reader sees why a source term is easy to write classically and hard to justify quantum mechanically.

That span exposes the central tension in ordinary terms: the left side wants a definite geometry, while the right side comes from quantum bookkeeping.
