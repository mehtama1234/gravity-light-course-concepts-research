Topology and Continuity

You need to describe curves that don't jump, and functions that keep nearby points nearby. But before you can say what 'nearby' means using measurements, you need to say which points count as neighbors to each other at all.

Naive Picture

Maybe two points are near if they fit inside an imaginary sphere of some fixed size—like how a city block marks a neighborhood. But that idea assumes you already know how to measure distance.

Why It Fails

In Einstein's relativity, distance is not fixed. Light cones—the shapes that describe which events can send signals to which other events—matter more than ordinary spheres. The theory must describe continuity (whether something jumps or flows smoothly) before it even decides what distance is. If you assume distance first, you sneak in hidden choices about what 'close' really means.

Mathematical Object

A topology is a carefully chosen collection of sets called 'open' sets. A set is open if whenever a point sits inside it, you can draw a neighborhood around that point (another open set) that stays completely inside. The collection itself must satisfy three rules: the whole space and the empty space are both in the collection; any union of open sets is also open; and the intersection of any *finite* number of open sets is also open. This structure lets you talk about which points are neighbors without any ruler or meter.

Operation

To check if a function is continuous, you ask: take any open set in where the function lands (the target space). Now look backward—which starting points map into that target set? If that backward set is also open in the starting space, then the function is continuous. Do this for *every* open set in the target. If it works for all of them, the function is continuous. This test needs no coordinates, no distances, no rulers—just the open sets.

Why It Matters

The gravitational field—the thing that curves space and tells objects how to move—must be continuous. The metric (the rule that measures distances in curved space) must be continuous. Light rays must be continuous curves through that curved space. Field equations—the rules that predict how the field changes—only make sense if the field is continuous. Before you can ask whether something wiggles smoothly or has a sharp jump, your space needs a topology to define that question.

What Breaks Without It

A formula written in one choice of coordinates (a label system) might look smooth. Rewrite the formula in a different coordinate system and it suddenly looks like it jumps. Without topology, you cannot tell whether the jump is real physics or just an artifact of changing your labels. This means field equations, energy conservation, and the definition of an observer all become ambiguous—you cannot separate true discontinuities from illusions of poor notation.

Mini Example

Imagine a weather map before you look at a thermometer. You can already ask: does the cold region border the warm region directly, or is there a barrier between them? You can trace a path from one zone to another and spot whether it has to jump discontinuously or can flow continuously. You can call some boundaries 'closed' (they trap their interior) and others 'open' (they include neighborhood space around them). None of this needs numbers—only knowledge of which points touch which. That is exactly what topology captures for space and time.

Common Confusion

Many people think topology is handwaving—'geometry without numbers.' Actually it is the opposite. Topology is the exact minimum structure you need to make continuity and limits mathematically precise. It refuses to commit to one specific distance rule. That refusal is strength: it lets Einstein's equations work on any curved space—whether embedded in a larger space or not, whether you have a metric or not—using only the open-set structure underneath.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Start with the core problem: you want to define curves that wiggle without jumps, and functions that keep neighbors as neighbors, but you have not yet chosen how to measure anything. Intuitively, you think you need a ruler first. But in Einstein's picture, the ruler is not fundamental—the gravitational field *creates* it. So the theory must have a language for continuity *before* it chooses a metric. That language is topology: a collection of open sets. Open sets do two jobs. First, they define neighborhoods—a neighborhood of a point is any open set holding that point. Second, they define limits—a sequence reaches its limit if all the tail terms fall into every open neighborhood of that limit. With neighborhoods and limits, continuity follows: a map is continuous if it sends the pre-image of every open target set back to an open source set. This uses only the collection of open sets, not distance or coordinates. That is why topology is the foundation for gravity. Fields must be continuous before they can be differentiated to give equations. Light paths must be continuous curves. Observers must vary continuously from place to place. All of that requires the theory to first have a topology—the structure underneath that makes the question 'is this continuous?' even meaningful.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A topology on a set X is a collection T of subsets of X with these three rules: X itself and the empty set both belong to T; if you take any bunch of sets from T and take their union, that union is also in T; if you take any *finite* list of sets from T and take their intersection, that intersection is also in T. Given this T, a function f : X → Y (where Y has its own topology T_Y) is continuous if and only if for every open set U in Y, the pre-image f⁻¹(U) = {x ∈ X : f(x) ∈ U} is open in X. Why does the inverse-image test work? Because 'a point is near the target' translates exactly to 'the starting point is near the source' when you use open sets on both sides. Why allow unions but only finite intersections? Because a point is considered 'near' a set if it sits in every open neighborhood around that set—that is an intersection. But you need the point in *finitely* many neighborhoods, not infinitely many. That captures the real notion of nearness. The three rules build the minimal structure to make limits and continuity rigorous without assuming any distance metric or coordinate system.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

If you have no topology, you cannot tell a real physical break from a fake one created by your choice of coordinates. A field might look continuous in one coordinate system and discontinuous in another—and you have no way to know which is the truth. The whole structure of general relativity—derivatives, field equations, conservation laws, the meaning of an observer—all rest on continuity. Continuity has no meaning without topology. Topology is the only solid ground.

Where It Sits In The Course

Continuous maps (checked by the pre-image test on open sets) and open sets (used to define neighborhoods) are the two pieces of topology you use directly in this course. Charts and atlases (the maps from curved-space patches to flat coordinate sheets) are defined using topology—they must be continuous, and their overlaps must be continuous. Manifolds are defined as topological spaces with charts. One-forms (dual vectors) are linear maps that must be continuous—and continuity has no definition without topology.

Connective Thread

After learning that continuous functions must preserve open sets, you reach this concept. This page makes that formal and builds the language. Next, charts will be smooth overlaps of topologically-open patches. Manifolds will sit on top of topological spaces. Every metric, every curvature, every component of Einstein's equation will assume continuity in the topological sense. The chain is: topology defines what continuity means; continuous charts and atlases build manifolds; manifolds carry metrics and curvature; curvature generates Einstein's law of gravity. Break the first link and the entire chain falls apart.
