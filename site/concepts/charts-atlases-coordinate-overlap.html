Charts, Atlases, and Overlap Rules

You are studying a sphere, a black hole, or the whole of spacetime. One coordinate system (a grid of numbers labeling points) works fine in some regions but fails in others—either the math breaks down or the physics doesn't make sense there. You need a practical way to cover the entire space with overlapping local grids, each working perfectly in its own zone, and then stitch them together so that any physical fact stays true no matter which grid you're using.

Naive Picture

A place 'is' its coordinates. If you change the number labels you assign to points, you've changed the place itself. So how can the same physical point have two different number names at all?

Why It Fails

Because coordinates are just human labels—names we assign to points. They are not built into the object itself. A drawn curve on a sphere curves exactly the same way whether you measure its path using latitude-longitude numbers or Cartesian (x, y, z) numbers—the actual shape of the curve does not change. You need a clear rule to translate from one numbering system to another without the space itself changing underneath.

Mathematical Object

A chart is a local coordinate system: a rule that assigns a sequence of numbers to each point in one patch of space. An atlas is a full collection of charts, each covering a different patch, and together they cover the entire space. The glue holding an atlas together is the overlap rule: where two charts cover the same region, there must be a smooth, reversible map that translates the numbers from one chart into the numbers of the other.

Operation

Take two charts that overlap. In the zone where they both apply, write down the formula that converts coordinates from the first chart into coordinates from the second chart. Check that this translation formula is smooth (no sudden jumps or tears). Check that it works both directions (you can go backward as easily as forward). If both checks pass, the two charts are compatible. Repeat this test for every pair of charts that share a region.

Why It Matters

In general relativity, the shape of spacetime is curved and no single coordinate grid can describe all of it smoothly. Schwarzschild coordinates (the standard labels near a black hole) break down at the event horizon; spherical coordinates (latitude-longitude style) break down at the north and south poles of a sphere. An atlas lets you blanket spacetime with multiple coordinate grids, each working where it is strongest, and smooth translation rules between them. This ensures that real physical properties—such as how curved spacetime actually is—survive and match regardless of which grid you switch to.

What Breaks Without It

Without overlap rules, you cannot tell whether a discontinuity or explosion in your calculations is a genuine physical feature or merely a failure of your choice of coordinate labels. A black hole horizon may look singular (infinite, broken) when described using one set of coordinates but smooth and ordinary when described using another. Overlap rules are how you recognize that the singularity is a coordinate accident, not real physics.

Mini Example

Consider a sphere. Use latitude-longitude numbers in the equatorial band (both work smoothly there). At the north pole, longitude numbers become useless—all the longitude lines meet. So instead, use Cartesian-like numbers (x, y, z) in a polar cap around the pole. The two systems overlap in the tropical band. The overlap rule states: for any point that appears in both systems, if it has latitude θ and longitude φ in the first system and numbers (x, y) in the second system, then x must equal sin(θ)cos(φ) and y must equal sin(θ)sin(φ). This translation is smooth and works both directions. That is the glue that holds the atlas together.

Common Confusion

Many people picture a chart as a physical map of territory, like a paper map of a city. In reality, a chart is a labeling or numbering system—it assigns numbers to points. The space itself exists independently of any chart and does not 'live inside' the charts. Overlapping charts do not mean the space exists twice or is duplicated; they mean the same set of points is being labeled using multiple different number systems. The overlap rule (the transition map) is the dictionary or Rosetta Stone that translates between the different labeling systems.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you are surveying a region. One coordinate grid works perfectly in the city—every street corner has a clear address. But beyond the city limits, the grid breaks; the rules for assigning addresses stop making sense. So you design a second grid for the countryside, with different rules suited to valleys and ridges. Both grids label the same ground, but with different address systems. Where the city grid and country grid overlap—perhaps a forest at the edge—both systems label the same trees and streams. The overlap rule is the translation book: if the city says a landmark is at position (10, 5) and the country system says it is at (8, 3), you need a consistent, smooth rule to convert one address into the other. That rule is the overlap map. In mathematics, a chart assigns numbers to points within a local patch. An atlas is a full set of charts tiling the space. The overlap rule—a smooth, reversible translation between charts wherever they meet—is what makes an atlas reliable: you can move from one chart to another without the underlying space jumping, tearing, or losing meaning. For gravity, this is critical because spacetime is curved and cannot be covered by one global grid. Near a black hole, one type of coordinate label fails; at the poles of a sphere, another fails. An atlas lets you use whatever coordinates work in each zone and translate smoothly between zones, so physical measurements remain consistent as you change your labeling convention.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A chart φ on a patch U of a space is a rule that assigns an ordered list of numbers (x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ) to each point p in U. The numbers are coordinates; they label the point. An atlas is a collection {φᵢ} of charts, each with its own patch {Uᵢ}, such that every point in the space lies in at least one patch. Where two patches Uᵢ and Uⱼ overlap, the transition map φⱼ ∘ φᵢ⁻¹ (read: 'φⱼ composed with the inverse of φᵢ') takes a number-tuple from chart i, reverses it back into a point in space, and then applies chart j to get a number-tuple in chart j's language. For two charts to be compatible, this transition map must satisfy two conditions: smoothness (the formula has no jumps or breaks, and all derivatives exist everywhere) and invertibility (you can apply the map in both directions equally well). Smoothness ensures no discontinuities appear as you cross from one chart's zone to another. Invertibility ensures the translation is unambiguous: one point in chart i corresponds to exactly one point in chart j. Together, these conditions guarantee that any quantity defined using one chart's coordinates can be reliably converted to another chart's coordinates without altering its actual meaning.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without overlap rules, you lose the ability to distinguish between a coordinate accident and a real physical event. A gravitational singularity might exist only because you chose bad coordinates, not because something genuinely singular is happening in spacetime. The foundation of general relativity—defining curvature, measuring distances and times, deriving field equations—rests on the principle that physical laws and predictions remain unchanged when you switch from one coordinate system to another. Overlap rules enforce this principle by guaranteeing that the same physics holds in the overlap zone regardless of which chart you are using. Remove the overlap rules and you lose that guarantee: arbitrary choice of coordinates can create fake features or erase real ones.

Where It Sits In The Course

Charts and atlases form the vocabulary for describing how any curved space can be locally labeled and navigated. They connect backward to continuity and open sets: a chart must assign numbers continuously (nearby points stay nearby in their numbers) and must be reversible, so the labeling faithfully reflects the space's structure. They connect forward to manifolds (the formal definition: a space that can be smoothly charted everywhere) and to tensors (quantities whose definitions are stable under coordinate change because the transition map guarantees their meaning translates correctly between charts).

Connective Thread

This concept arrives after the course has established what continuity and open sets mean (giving concrete sense to 'local') and has explained why a single global coordinate system is insufficient for curved spaces. It sets up the formal definition of a manifold as 'a space that admits an atlas of smooth, compatible charts' and enables the later definitions of tangent vectors, dual vectors, and tensor fields—all of which rely on coordinate transition maps to ensure their meaning is independent of which chart you happen to use.
