Charts, Atlases, and Overlap Rules

You're studying a sphere or a black hole or spacetime itself. One coordinate system works fine in some regions but breaks elsewhere—either mathematically or physically. You need a way to tile the whole space with overlapping local maps and stitch them together so the physics doesn't depend on which map you pick.

Naive Picture

A place 'is' its coordinates. Change the coordinate labels and you've changed the object itself. So how can the same physical point have different coordinate names?

Why It Fails

Because coordinates are human labels, not intrinsic to the object. A curve in space curves the same way whether you use latitude-longitude or Cartesian grid—the curve's shape doesn't depend on which label you chose. You need a way to translate between labels without the object itself changing.

Mathematical Object

A chart is a local coordinate system: a rule assigning numbers to points in one patch of space. An atlas is a collection of charts covering the whole space. The glue that holds an atlas together is the overlap rule: where two charts cover the same region, there must be a smooth, invertible map translating between their coordinate labels.

Operation

Pick two overlapping charts. In the overlap zone, write down how coordinates from one chart relate to coordinates from the other. Check that this transition map is smooth (no jumps or breaks). If it is, the two charts are compatible. Do this for every pair that overlaps.

Why It Matters

In general relativity, spacetime is curved and a single coordinate patch can't cover it all. Schwarzschild coordinates break at the event horizon; polar coordinates break at the north and south poles of a sphere. An atlas lets you cover spacetime with multiple coordinate patches and translate between them smoothly, so physical quantities (like the curvature of spacetime) survive the change.

What Breaks Without It

Without overlap rules, you can't tell whether a discontinuity in a calculation is a real physical feature or just an artifact of picking a bad coordinate system. A black hole horizon looks singular in some coordinates but is smooth in others—you need overlap rules to recognize that the singularity is coordinate artifact, not physics.

Mini Example

Consider a sphere. Use latitude-longitude in the equatorial region (both work smoothly). At the north pole, longitude becomes meaningless—all meridians meet. So switch to Cartesian-like coordinates (x, y, z) in a polar cap. In the overlap zone (tropics), both systems describe the same points. The overlap rule says: if a point has latitude θ and longitude φ in one chart and (x, y) in the other chart in the overlap, then x = sin(θ) cos(φ) and y = sin(θ) sin(φ) must hold. This map is smooth and invertible in the overlap. That's the glue.

Common Confusion

People think a chart is a map of the object, like a paper map of a territory. Actually, a chart is a labeling system—it assigns numbers to points. The object itself is abstract and doesn't 'live' in the charts. Overlapping charts don't mean the space is duplicated; they mean the same set of points is described using different number systems. The overlap rule (the transition map) is the Rosetta Stone between systems.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you're charting a world. One map works perfectly for a town square, but its grid breaks down in the hills beyond. So you draw a second map for the hills, with a different grid. Both maps are useful locally, but they use different labels for the same ground. The overlap zone—say, a forest edge—appears on both maps. Here's the key: the two grids must agree on what they mean by 'same point'. If the square's grid says a tree is at (10, 5) and the hill map says it's at (8, 3), there must be a consistent, smooth rule that translates one to the other. That translation rule is the overlap map. In mathematics, a chart assigns numbers to points in a local patch. An atlas is a full collection of charts. The overlap rule—a smooth, invertible translation between charts wherever they overlap—is what makes an atlas trustworthy: you can move from one chart to another without the underlying space jumping or tearing. In gravity, this matters because spacetime often can't be labeled by one global coordinate system. Near a black hole, horizon coordinates fail; at the poles of a sphere, spherical coordinates fail. An atlas lets you use what works locally and translate smoothly between patches, so physical measurements don't mysteriously change just because you switched your labeling convention.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A chart φ on a patch U of a space assigns a tuple of numbers (x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ) to each point p in U. The numbers are coordinates; they label the point. An atlas is a family {φᵢ} of charts with patches {Uᵢ} that cover the whole space: every point lies in at least one patch. Where two patches Uᵢ and Uⱼ overlap, the transition map φⱼ ∘ φᵢ⁻¹ takes coordinates from chart i, inverts them back to a point, then maps to chart j's coordinates. For the charts to be compatible, this transition map must be smooth (infinitely differentiable) and invertible. Smoothness means no sudden jumps; invertibility means the translation goes both ways. This ensures that any scalar quantity (like density) or vector field (like velocity) defined in chart i's coordinates can be translated to chart j's coordinates without losing meaning.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without overlap rules, you lose the distinction between coordinate artifact and physical reality. A gravitational singularity might be just a coordinate failure, not an actual physical divergence. The entire machinery of general relativity—defining curvature, measuring intervals, solving field equations—relies on the idea that when you change coordinates, the laws and predictions stay the same. Overlap rules guarantee this invariance. Skip them and coordinate choices start creating fake physics.

Where It Sits In The Course

Charts and atlases are the language for talking about local coordinate descriptions of any curved space. They connect back to continuity and open sets: a chart must be continuous and invertible so that 'nearby points in the space' stay 'nearby in coordinates'. They connect forward to manifolds (spaces that look locally flat in any coordinate chart) and to tensors (quantities that transform predictably under coordinate change via the transition map).

Connective Thread

This concept lives after topology and open sets have supplied the meaning of 'local' and 'continuous', and after the course has motivated why we need more than one coordinate view. It prepares the definition of a manifold as 'a space that can be charted smoothly' and it enables the later definitions of tangent vectors, covectors, and tensor fields, all of which rely on coordinate transition maps to ensure their definitions make sense regardless of which chart you use.

Tutorial 2: Topological Manifolds

Pressure test: Treat charts as local names, then test whether the names agree where patches overlap. The tutorial pressure is to keep the point and the coordinate label separate, especially when a statement appears true in one chart and must be carried through the atlas.

Why it changes this concept: This turns manifold language into a guard against false singularities. The concept is no longer just local flatness; it is the discipline of asking whether a claimed geometric fact survives a change in local naming system.

The chosen span is about continuity in a maximal atlas, which is the practical heart of the tutorial. It does not merely repeat the word manifold; it asks whether a property survives when read through chart descriptions. That is why the page centers overlap discipline rather than treating charts as harmless labels.

Tutorial 4: Differentiable Manifolds

Pressure test: Construct the transition map between overlapping charts and ask whether it is smooth enough for calculus to be legal. The tutorial pressure is to make derivatives pass through chart changes instead of trusting the first coordinate picture that looks differentiable.

Why it changes this concept: This turns smoothness into a permission rule. The concept page can now explain why later derivatives, connections, and curvature need chart compatibility before they can claim to describe spacetime rather than one drawing of spacetime.

The source span asks for the chart transition map. That is the tutorial's concrete test: if two coordinate descriptions overlap, the transition between them must be smooth enough for calculus to make the same kind of claim in both. The page therefore treats differentiability as a permission rule, not as visual smoothness.
