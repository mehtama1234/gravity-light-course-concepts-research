Continuous Maps Preserve Nearness

You measure the temperature of a room two ways: once with a Celsius thermometer, once with a Fahrenheit one. When the real air temperature changes by a tiny bit, do both thermometers show tiny changes, or can one jump wildly while the other creeps? How do you know that converting between temperature scales doesn't secretly hide jumps?

Naive Picture

A map is continuous if you can trace its path on graph paper without ever lifting your pencil—one smooth, unbroken line or curve from start to finish.

Why It Fails

Most spaces in this course cannot be drawn flat on a single piece of graph paper. A sphere's surface curves; you cannot flatten it without ripping or stretching it. You need a way to describe continuity that works even when there is no flat paper to draw on—a definition that lives in the spaces themselves.

Mathematical Object

A continuous map is a rule connecting two spaces—call them X and Y—with a special property: if you pick any open set (any "test region" of nearby points) in the target space Y, and trace backward through the map to find which points in X map into it, you always get an open set in X. The backward direction keeps openness intact.

Operation

To check if a map is continuous, do this: pick any open test-region in the space you are mapping to. Trace backward through the map to find all the starting points that land in that test-region. If that backward set is always open in the starting space, the map is continuous. Test with any open region; the rule must hold every time.

Why It Matters

Different observers see the same universe but describe it using different coordinate systems—different languages for labeling positions and times. A continuous map ensures that when two events sit close together in one observer's coordinate language, they remain close in every other observer's language too. No hidden tears or jumps appear when you switch from one description to another.

What Breaks Without It

If a map did not preserve openness, coordinate changes could create false discontinuities—regions that were smoothly connected could seem to split apart. A field describing a physical quantity could look smooth and ordinary in one coordinate system but appear to break or jump suddenly in another. Reality would depend on your choice of labels, which is nonsense.

Mini Example

A car's speedometer converts the engine's spinning speed, measured in revolutions per minute, into a needle angle on the dial, measured in degrees. When the true speed is near 3000 rpm, a small change in the actual speed produces a small change in the needle angle. The map from speed to needle position is continuous: if you pick any open range of needle positions—say, from 30° to 40°—then the speeds that produce needle angles in that range form an open interval of rpm values, like 2900 to 3100. There are no hidden jumps in the mapping; small changes in input always mean small changes in output.

Common Confusion

Continuity does not mean a map is the identity map or that nothing changes. A continuous map can twist, stretch, and bend wildly. It forbids only one thing: tearing the neighborhood structure—breaking the connection between points that start close and points that end close.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you measure the temperature in a room using two very different devices: an old mercury thermometer with a glass tube and a modern electronic sensor. When the air's real temperature shifts by a tiny amount, both devices should show a tiny shift in their readings. That's the intuition—nearby states of the world produce nearby readings on every measuring tool. The naive answer is simple: a map is continuous if you can draw its graph without lifting your pencil. But this course studies spaces that cannot lie flat on a single sheet of paper. A sphere's surface is curved all over; you cannot flatten it to a plane without tearing it apart. The mathematical surface describing spacetime in general relativity—called a manifold—is built by stitching together many overlapping flat patches, not from a single underlying sheet. So you need a way to define continuity that doesn't depend on external graph paper. The solution is clever: instead of asking whether the graph can be drawn without lifting your pencil, you work backward. A map is continuous if whenever you choose an open test-region (a neighborhood of nearby points) in the target space, the set of all starting points that map into that region always forms an open test-region in the starting space. This definition works for any kind of space—flat, curved, local neighborhoods, or global shapes—because it uses only the internal language of open sets. The practical power is enormous. In relativity, different observers use different coordinate systems to label the same events. Continuity guarantees that if one observer's coordinate system says two events are close, every other observer, no matter what coordinate system they choose, will also say those events are close. Without this protection, changing how you label positions and times could manufacture false discontinuities, making nearby events appear to tear apart. That would destroy the entire mathematical foundation of general relativity.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Let f represent a map that takes points from space X and sends them to space Y. Suppose U is an open set sitting inside Y. The preimage or inverse image of U, written f⁻¹(U), is the collection of all points in X whose images land inside U. The definition of continuity says: f is continuous if and only if for every open set U in Y, the set f⁻¹(U) is also open in X. Why does this rule preserve nearness? Open sets encode the idea of neighborhoods—sets of nearby points. If U is an open set in Y, then any point in U has a neighborhood that fits entirely within U. When you pull U backward through the map to get f⁻¹(U), you are asking: which starting points have all their images clustered together inside U? If f⁻¹(U) is open, then every point in f⁻¹(U) comes equipped with a neighborhood in X that also maps entirely into U. This is precisely the mathematical guarantee that points starting close together map to points ending close together.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without continuity, differential geometry—the mathematics of describing curved surfaces and spaces—falls apart. You cannot properly define a manifold, the curved mathematical surface that general relativity depends on. Calculus stops working: derivatives and integrals require you to zoom in on arbitrarily small regions and compare values at nearby points consistently. Every geometric tool—measuring how much a space bends, connecting direction arrows at different locations, writing equations for fields—collapses completely. The entire mathematical machinery that allows physicists to treat spacetime as a single, smooth, interconnected fabric shatters into isolated disconnected pieces.

Where It Sits In The Course

Open sets (from the previous page) are the core language; continuity is how maps must respect that language. The following pages—Topology and Continuity, Charts and Atlases—build on this idea to explain how different local views of the same curved space fit together smoothly.

Connective Thread

Continuity is the bridge between the abstract language of open sets and the real behavior of actual maps and functions. Every coordinate chart describing a patch of spacetime, every field equation written in physics, every transformation from one observer's coordinates to another relies on this single guarantee: points that are close in one mathematical description remain close in every other mathematical description—not by luck, but by the definition itself.
