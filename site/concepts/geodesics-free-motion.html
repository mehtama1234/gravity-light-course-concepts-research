Geodesics As Free Motion

A stone drifts through empty space, far from stars, gravity, and obstacles. It moves in a straight line forever. But what is straight? On a flat piece of paper the answer is trivial—draw a ruler. Yet near a black hole or inside the curved spacetime around a massive star, there is no background flat space to measure against. You cannot use a ruler because spacetime itself is the only thing that exists. The course needs a way to say: "this particle is moving with no forces pushing it" using only the shape of spacetime, nothing outside it.

Naive Picture

First thought: a freely moving object should trace the path that looks most straight when you imagine spacetime as a rubber sheet stretched in space. Imagine the curved surface, find the shortest curve between two points on that surface, and that curve is the path the particle takes.

Why It Fails

This trick hides a flat space outside. When you draw a curved rubber sheet on paper, you already have a flat piece of paper underneath. You are measuring against that flat background. But spacetime has no outside. Nothing exists beyond it to measure against. Any real definition of "free motion" must come entirely from the geometry of spacetime itself and cannot depend on imagining a flat space around it.

Mathematical Object

A geodesic is a path traced through spacetime with a special property: as you move along the path, the direction you are headed does not change according to the local rule for comparing directions at nearby points—a rule called the connection. Think of walking on the curved surface of Earth. The connection is the instruction for what "same direction" means when you shift from one spot to the next without leaving the surface. A geodesic is a path where, by that local rule, your heading never turns.

Operation

To test if a path is a geodesic: at each point along the path, take the direction you are headed (the tangent vector). Move that direction to the next point using the connection rule. Ask: did the direction change? If the answer is always no (the covariant derivative is zero), the path is a geodesic. This turns a feeling—"I am not turning"—into a precise, checkable recipe.

Why It Matters

Einstein's picture of gravity says: a particle with no forces acting on it follows a geodesic of spacetime. A light ray does the same, but along a special geodesic that travels at light speed. The metric—the rule for measuring distances and time intervals—determines which geodesics belong to particles and which to light. This is how gravity works: the curve of spacetime itself tells objects and light rays which paths to follow. No forces. No pulls. Just the shape of space and time.

What Breaks Without It

Take away the geodesic and you lose any way to express "free motion" or "no forces" in a curved universe. Equations might still spit out numbers, but those numbers would change depending on your coordinate choice, your private definitions, and your arbitrary setup. They would not represent something real about physics. The whole chain from geometry to observed motion snaps.

Mini Example

An airplane flies from New York to London. On a flat map, the shortest path curves up toward the north pole. But that curved line is the straightest path on the real curved Earth—a great circle. The pilot following this path does not try to turn left or right at any point. The pilot keeps the plane level, and Earth's curvature bends the path naturally. At no moment does the direction of flight change relative to the surface itself. The plane is a geodesic traveler.

Common Confusion

"Geodesics are always the shortest paths." Not true. A geodesic is locally straight by the connection rule—zero bend by the measure of parallel transport. But globally, a geodesic might be the longest path (the back way around a sphere's great circle), or there might be many geodesics between two spots. What matters: the geodesic is the path a free, unforced object actually takes, and that path is fixed by the connection, not by minimizing distance.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Start with the real problem: in spacetime bent by gravity, what does it mean for something to move with no forces? Not "move in a straight line"—spacetime has no global straight line, no universal reference. Not "move along the path that looks straight on a diagram"—that requires an imaginary flat space outside, which does not exist. We must define free motion using only the geometry inside spacetime. The tool is the connection: a local instruction for comparing directions at nearby points. A freely moving object is one whose heading does not change as we carry it from point to point along the object's own path, using that local rule. This is the precise, internal definition: a geodesic is a path whose heading, when moved along itself via the connection, never changes. No outside reference needed, no hidden flatness. The test is concrete: at each point on the path, does the connection rule say the heading changed? If not at any point, the object is in free motion.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Mark the path with a parameter λ (often time or distance). At each point the tangent vector (the direction the path points) is d/dλ. The covariant derivative ∇_(d/dλ) (d/dλ) measures how the tangent vector shifts when carried along the path using the connection ∇. For a geodesic, this derivative is zero: ∇_(d/dλ) (d/dλ) = 0. In coordinates, this becomes d²x^μ/dλ² + Γ^μ_ρσ (dx^ρ/dλ)(dx^σ/dλ) = 0. Here Γ^μ_ρσ are connection coefficients—numbers that encode the local comparison rule. The equation says: acceleration (second derivative of position) plus a correction built from how the coordinate grid itself is twisted equals zero. This is the geodesic equation.

Where This Idea Itself Breaks Down

The geodesic equation assumes the particle is a point mass with no internal structure, extension, or self-gravity. It fails for extended bodies when tidal forces (arising from spacetime curvature gradients) are strong enough to deform the body—the Weyl tensor component responsible for tidal stress becomes comparable to the body's internal stress. A macroscopic object experiences a fractional strain ~ (ΔL/L) ~ (R_curvature/L_body)^2, and when this exceeds the material's breaking strength, the body deforms and the point-particle trajectory no longer describes the motion.

The geodesic equation also assumes no dissipative forces (no friction, drag, radiation reaction). Near black holes or in radiative spacetimes, the emission of gravitational waves carries away energy and momentum, causing the actual trajectory to deviate from the geodesic—an effect measured by the Abraham-Lorentz-Dirac backreaction term, which becomes significant when gravitational wave luminosity L_gw ~ (Gm/c^3) a^2 is not negligible.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Every major idea ahead—curvature, moving vectors along surfaces, Einstein's equation, how matter bends spacetime, black holes, the expanding universe—is built on geodesics as the language for "free motion." Erase it and these ideas become symbol pushing with no meaning. Students would write equations without understanding what happens in the real universe. The geodesic transforms geometry from an abstract game into a full description of how things actually move through nature.

Where It Sits In The Course

Geodesics connect vector fields and parallel transport—how you move arrows on a surface—to the geometric ideas next: curvature, which measures how geodesics in different directions tilt toward or away from each other. Geodesics are the "straight" paths against which all bending is measured. When you reach curvature on the next page, you will see it defined precisely as the way two nearby geodesics fail to stay parallel as they are carried along.

Connective Thread

This concept rests on understanding vector fields, connections, parallel transport, and the covariant derivative from earlier pages. The geodesic weaves all those tools together in one idea: a curve whose tangent vector does not change under covariant derivative. Everything that follows—curvature, the Riemann tensor that measures spacetime's shape, Einstein's equation linking geometry to matter, the orbit of planets and the collision of black holes—depends on this definition of free motion as the foundation.
