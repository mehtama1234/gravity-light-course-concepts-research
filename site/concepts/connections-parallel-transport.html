Connections and Parallel Transport

Walk north on a globe, holding a compass pointing east. At the equator, your compass still points east. At the north pole, it has rotated ninety degrees. How do you measure whether the direction actually changed, or whether the surface itself twisted it?

Naive Picture

You might think: two arrows at different points are parallel if they point the same way when you draw them both on a flat page. Just compare the pictures.

Why It Fails

A curved surface has no global flat page. The globe itself cannot be flattened without tearing. Each location on the globe has its own tangent space—its own space of possible directions—and those spaces are separate. There is no inherited notion of 'the same direction' across them.

Mathematical Object

A connection is a machine that takes a vector (a direction at one point) and a path and produces a new vector at a nearby point. It does this consistently: the result at each step depends only on the local geometry and the direction you are moving, never on arbitrary coordinates.

Operation

To transport a vector along a curve, you break the curve into tiny steps. At each step, the connection tells you how much to adjust the vector to keep it 'pointing the same way' according to the local geometry. Sum up all those small adjustments and you have the transported vector.

Why It Matters

Gravity is not a force tugging objects; it is geometry. An object in free fall moves in a straight line through curved spacetime. The 'straightness' is defined by parallel transport: the object's velocity stays parallel to itself along its path. Light follows the same rule. Without a connection, you cannot even say what straight motion means on a curved spacetime.

What Breaks Without It

Without a connection, you cannot define acceleration as a geometric property. Every calculation of whether an object speeds up or slows down depends on picking coordinates—and different observers picking different coordinates get different answers. The theory collapses into coordinate choices, not physics.

Mini Example

Imagine a sphere, and a vector (direction) pointing north at the equator. Walk the vector north one degree along a meridian. The connection tells you: adjust the vector by a tiny amount so it still points 'north' relative to the meridian. Walk another degree, adjust again. After ninety degrees, you have reached the north pole and your vector now points in a definite direction (say, along the Greenwich meridian). Now walk west around the pole for ninety degrees, adjusting the vector at each step to stay parallel relative to your new direction of travel. When you return to the equator, your vector points east—rotated ninety degrees from the beginning. This rotation is not magic; it is the sum of all the tiny adjustments, and it measures the sphere's curvature.

Common Confusion

People often think a connection is a force that pulls or distorts vectors as they move. It is not. A connection is the bookkeeping rule that tells you what counts as moving in a straight line on a curved space. It is part of the definition of 'straight,' not a separate physical influence.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine holding a pencil and walking in a loop on a globe: north to the pole, then west around it, then back south. You try to keep the pencil pointing 'the same way' as you walk. But 'the same way' is tricky on a curved surface. At each point, there is a space of possible directions—the tangent space. Your pencil lives in that space. When you take a step, the tangent space shifts and tilts. How do you know if your pencil rotated, or if the space rotated? You need a rule. A connection is exactly that rule: it tells you, for each tiny step in each direction, how much to adjust your pencil to keep it 'parallel to itself' according to the local geometry. When you apply this rule around the full loop, the pencil has rotated. That rotation is not arbitrary—it measures the intrinsic curvature of the surface. For gravity, this is the essential insight: an object in free fall is one whose velocity vector stays parallel to itself along its worldline through spacetime. Light and falling bodies follow the same rule. If you had no connection, you could not even define what 'parallel' means on a curved space, and gravity would dissolve into coordinate bookkeeping.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A connection assigns to each direction (tangent vector) u at a point p and each vector field V a new vector, written ∇_u V, that measures how V changes in the u direction after subtracting the change that comes from merely shifting location. Formally: ∇_u V lives in the tangent space at p and is linear in u (doubling u doubles the change) and linear in V. For parallel transport, if V is a vector and u(t) is a velocity along a curve, the transported vector satisfies ∇_{u(t)} V = 0—it does not change in the u(t) direction. This is a differential equation that you integrate along the curve to find V at the end. The connection determines a unique parallel transport for every path, and different connections give different answers. In the context of gravity, we choose the Levi-Civita connection, which respects the metric (distances do not change under parallel transport) and is torsion-free (loops that close give the same result regardless of the path taken, up to curvature).

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without a connection, you cannot move vectors from one point to another on a curved surface without breaking their meaning. Any physical law written as an equation—saying how a field or object changes from place to place—depends on comparing values at adjacent points. If that comparison rule collapses, derivatives vanish and so do Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations, and Einstein's equations. The entire mathematical language for describing how things evolve in spacetime ceases to exist. Gravity itself becomes mute.

Where It Sits In The Course

The connection ties together the earlier language of tangent spaces and vector fields by defining how to compare and measure change across the manifold. It is the tool that makes those abstract objects concrete and operational. Later, curvature measures the failure of parallel transport around loops, and geodesics use the connection to define paths where the velocity is parallel to itself.
