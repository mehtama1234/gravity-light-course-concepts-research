Geodesics — why free fall is actually straight-line motion

An apple falls, a planet orbits, a light ray bends near the Sun. Newton saw gravity as a force pulling on every object. But Einstein said something stranger: there is no force. Instead, space and time are curved, and every free object simply follows the straightest available path through that curved geometry — a geodesic. Here is the idea, the measured route, why this reframing works, where it breaks, and why it is the core of general relativity.

1 · The problem — why do the paths curve?

A planet orbits the Sun instead of sailing off in a straight line. An apple falls instead of floating still. A beam of starlight warps as it passes the Sun. Newton gave the same answer to all three: gravity is a force pulling inward, bending the paths. The force is proportional to mass, and it acts on everything equally — cannonballs and feathers fall at the same rate.

This works beautifully for prediction. But it leaves a puzzle unsolved: What exactly is this force? Newton himself didn't know — he called gravity 'action at a distance' and admitted it was mysterious. Centuries later, Einstein asked: maybe this is not a force at all. Maybe there is a simpler picture hiding underneath.

2 · The reframe — forget the force; follow the geometry

Einstein's answer flips the picture upside down. Space and time around a mass are curved — the way a heavy ball warps the surface of a trampoline. In curved geometry, there is a notion of the straightest possible path on the curved surface, called a geodesic. An object with no forces acting on it moves along a geodesic, always heading straight ahead. But on a curved surface, heading straight ahead traces a path that looks bent from far away.

The twist: time is also curved, not just space. The 'straightest path' through curved spacetime is not the same as a straight line drawn on a flat map. When you compute the path that goes 'straight ahead at every step' through a geometry where both space and time are warped, you get the orbits of planets, the fall of apples, and the bending of light — not from a force, but from following the simplest, most direct route available.

The hidden quantity is the path that makes proper time extremal — the straightest possible worldline, which is exactly what a freely falling object follows.

On the globe itself, you are moving straight ahead the whole way — you never turn. The path curves only because the surface beneath you curves. This is exactly what happens in spacetime: a freely falling body is moving straight ahead (no forces), but the paths converge, diverge, or spiral because the geometry of spacetime around the mass is curved.

4 · Why 'straight through curved space' feels like a force

Consider you are in an elevator in free fall. You feel weightless — no force pushing on you. Yet from the perspective of someone on the ground, you are accelerating downward under gravity. Both views are true. You are simply following a geodesic through spacetime — the straightest possible path in curved geometry. Because spacetime is warped by Earth's mass, the geodesic happens to approach the Earth's surface. There is no force; you are moving straight, and the curvature brings you closer.

This is the core flip: in Newton's view, a force bends the path. In Einstein's view, the path is already straight (in the curved geometry), and the force is an illusion born from trying to describe straight motion in curved space using flat-space intuition.

5 · The one real limit — defining 'straight' near extreme curvature

The idea of 'the straightest path' is clear on a sphere or a saddle — shapes where curvature is gentle and uniform. But near a black hole, or where spacetime is wildly warped by colliding gravitational waves, curvature becomes extreme. In these regimes, geodesics can behave counterintuitively: two initially-parallel paths might focus and collide, or they might spiral into the singularity. The notion of 'straightest' itself — which requires a rule for comparing directions at different points — becomes subtle and must be defined carefully using differential geometry. And there is no guarantee that a geodesic stays nice forever; some paths that start straight may end in a place where the geometry is singular and the description breaks.

6 · The surprise — geodesics are the foundation of everything

Geodesics are not an isolated idea; they are the bedrock of general relativity. The Einstein equations say: matter and energy tell spacetime how to curve; spacetime tells matter how to move. That second part is the geodesic principle — everything on a geodesic is in free motion, with no applied force. Planets, stars, light rays, falling apples, and black holes — all follow geodesics.

From this single idea flow the orbits of Mercury (which deviate from Newton's prediction), the bending of starlight by the Sun (which Arthur Eddington measured in 1919 and made Einstein famous), the merger of neutron stars (which sends gravitational waves across the universe), and the edge of black holes (where geodesics end). The whole picture of gravity as geometry, with free motion as geodesic flow, has reshaped physics.

7 · Where it is used in practice — Straight lines in curved space: the path gravity takes

Satellites orbit Earth along geodesics, not because Earth's gravity pulls them, but because they follow the straightest possible path through the spacetime curved by Earth's mass. A spacecraft in free fall toward Mars traces a geodesic through the Sun's curved spacetime; mission planners compute these paths to minimize fuel. Light rays bend around massive objects (like the Sun or a galaxy cluster) because they follow geodesics through the warped geometry, not because gravity pulls on them. Every free object—from a falling apple to a photon grazing the Sun to a neutron star—moves along a geodesic.

The metric describes the geometry of spacetime and defines what 'straight ahead' means at each point. A geodesic is the longest path between two events (or shortest, depending on whether time or space dominates), and an object with no applied force moves along it naturally. The geodesic equation, built from the metric, contains all of gravity without invoking any force at all—curvature substitutes for acceleration.

Gravity ceases to be a mysterious force and becomes a purely geometric concept: curved spacetime makes free objects follow curved paths.
