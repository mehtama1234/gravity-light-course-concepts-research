Canonical Formulation and Constraints

Imagine filming a swelling soap bubble. Each frame shows the bubble's shape, but the film itself doesn't tell you what happens next: you need to know both how it looks now and how fast it's changing. General relativity faces the same problem—spacetime has no preferred "flow of time", so we must start from a frozen snapshot of space and ask what rules govern its evolution to the next snapshot.

Naive Picture

One might guess: pick a grid in space, measure distances and rates of change on it, and let time tick at the same rate everywhere. Feed these numbers into the theory and get the future. Like solving ordinary physics with initial position and velocity.

Why It Fails

General relativity has no universal clock. The metric—the thing that measures distances—does not come with a built-in, god-given flow of time. If you choose a grid and a time-direction arbitrarily, you may create a dataset that looks like initial conditions but violates the geometric constraints the theory demands. The theory is picky: not every choice of initial space and motion is allowed.

Mathematical Object

The canonical formulation rewrites the Einstein field equations into a language suited to evolution: it decomposes the full spacetime metric into a three-dimensional metric h living on a spatial slice, plus two more pieces—the lapse α and the shift β—that encode how the slice tips and moves through spacetime. The result looks like a dynamical system with degrees of freedom (h and related momentum-like variables) and constraint equations they must obey.

Operation

To use this formulation: write down a three-dimensional metric h on your spatial slice and propose a momentum field. Check that they satisfy two constraint equations (called the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints). If they do, the evolution equations tell you how h and momentum change as you move to the next slice—without you having to guess the lapse and shift freely.

Why It Matters

This viewpoint is essential for understanding how gravity actually changes spacetime over time. It lets us separate what is determined by the physics (h and its time-rate) from what is a choice of coordinate system (α and β). This clarity is needed to track gravitational waves, to set up initial conditions correctly, and to understand how measurements propagate forward in time without smuggling in hidden assumptions.

What Breaks Without It

If you ignore the constraints and just try to evolve any initial data you like, you end up with solutions that do not represent physical spacetime—they describe artifacts of your coordinate choice, not real gravitational effects. You cannot tell which features of your solution are geometry and which are illusion. The subsequent theory wobbles because it has no stable ground.

Mini Example

Consider two observers on a sphere, separated by a small distance. At one instant, you measure the distance between them and the rate at which it is changing (they are moving apart). But "moving apart" relative to what? Relative to a grid fixed in space? Relative to a grid that itself is shrinking? The canonical formulation forces you to state which: α and β are that answer. Once they are fixed, the constraint equations tell you whether your distance and rate-of-change data are consistent with any valid geometry. If they are, the equations predict uniquely how the distance evolves. If not, you chose an impossible initial snapshot.

Common Confusion

A common mistake is to think the canonical formulation is an approximation or a step down from the full four-dimensional Einstein equations. It is not. It is exactly the same theory, just rewritten to expose the structure suited to time-evolution. Some learners also think that α and β are determined by physics, when in fact they are free choices—leftovers of gauge freedom. The constraint equations do not determine them; they only constrain the data h and momentum so that any choice of α and β will give a valid evolution.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you have a snapshot of space right now. You can measure the shape of space—distances, angles, curvatures—and encode this in a metric h. You can also measure how fast things are moving and deforming. But here is the puzzle: space itself has no natural way to tick forward in time. There is no "next moment" written into space alone. General relativity does not give you a universal clock. So you must make a choice: how do you attach the next snapshot to this one? Do you move along a direction perpendicular to your current slice, or tilted, or stretched? The lapse α controls how far you move; the shift β controls which direction. These are coordinate choices, not physical facts. But here is the catch: if you choose α and β poorly, you might create initial data that is internally inconsistent. The theory demands that certain geometric relationships hold between the metric and its rate of change. These are the constraint equations. They are not new physics; they are the Einstein field equations reorganized. By writing them as constraints on initial data, you can check: does my snapshot fit the theory? If yes, evolution is deterministic: the constraint equations plus the evolution equations tell you exactly how the metric h changes on the next slice, and the lapse and shift carry it forward. This split—between the data determined by physics (h and its momentum) and the choices left to you (α and β)—is what the canonical formulation gives you.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Write the spacetime metric as a sum of pieces: the four-dimensional metric splits into a three-dimensional metric h on a spatial slice, plus a lapse function α that measures time passage (α = 1 means normal time-flow; α ≠ 1 means time passes at different rates at different places), plus a shift vector β that tilts the slice (β tells you how a coordinate grid in space "slides" as you step forward in time). The Hamiltonian constraint relates h and its momentum to the matter present; the momentum constraint relates the momentum to the momentum density. These two constraints are algebraic (they do not involve time-derivatives) and must hold on each slice. The evolution equations are then differential equations (involving time-derivatives) that tell you how h changes from slice to slice. Since α and β are not determined by the equations, they remain free: this freedom is exactly the gauge freedom—the freedom to choose coordinates. The power of the formulation is that it splits the problem: solve the constraints to get allowed initial data, then solve evolution equations to get the future.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without this split, general relativity is a maze. You write down Einstein's field equations as ten coupled nonlinear partial differential equations in four dimensions, and you cannot see which pieces describe real physics and which describe your coordinate choice. The canonical formulation strips away the choice: it defines a phase space of all possible spatial geometries and momenta, shows which subspace (defined by the constraints) is physical, and makes evolution transparent. Without this framework, numerical simulations of gravity, searches for gravitational waves, and attempts to quantize gravity all founder. With it, the theory becomes a lever: you can pry apart the physical content.

Where It Sits In The Course

The canonical formulation sits at the heart of the course's turn from geometry to dynamics. It connects backward to the metric and curvature (which define h and its behavior) and forward to gravitational waves (which are ripples in h that propagate forward according to the evolution equations), to cosmology (where α and β encode the expansion and the shape of the universe's foliation), and to black holes (where the constraints are satisfied even at the horizon, but α vanishes there—a signal that the horizon is null).

Connective Thread

The canonical formulation opens the bridge from describing spacetime as a static object (topics like manifolds, geodesics, and the metric) to describing it as a dynamical system (evolution of slices, waves, the causal structure). It answers the question: if spacetime is not a fixed stage but a evolving entity, what rules tell it how to evolve? It does this by reorganizing the Einstein field equations into initial-value form, respecting the coordinate freedom that general relativity guarantees. Later topics (gravitational waves, black hole dynamics, cosmological solutions) all rely on this initial-value perspective: they describe how specific geometries evolve, not how static universes are built.

Pressure test: Put classical geometry and quantum matter on opposite sides of the same proposed equation and ask what kind of object each side is. The evening-lecture pressure is to notice that a definite spacetime source is no longer straightforward when matter is treated quantum mechanically.

Why it changes this concept: This makes quantum gravity a bookkeeping crisis, not a fashionable topic. The concept pages on constraints, source terms, and canonical formulation now point toward the place where classical spacetime stops being obviously self-contained. The reader sees why a source term is easy to write classically and hard to justify quantum mechanically.
