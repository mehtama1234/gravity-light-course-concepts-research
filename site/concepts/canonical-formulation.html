Canonical Formulation and Constraints

Imagine filming a soap bubble as it swells. Each frame captures the bubble's shape at one moment, but the film alone does not tell you what the bubble will do next. You need two pieces of information: what it looks like right now, and how fast it is changing. General relativity runs into the same puzzle with spacetime. Spacetime has no universal clock ticking everywhere at the same rate, so we cannot simply say "here is the geometry now, here is how fast it is changing—go compute the future." We must build a language that does not assume a natural time-flow.

Naive Picture

One might guess this approach: mark out a grid on space, measure distances and velocities at each grid point, then let time advance uniformly everywhere. Plug these numbers into the theory and calculate the future state. This mirrors how you would solve everyday physics problems: given initial position and velocity, compute what happens next. It feels natural because in Newtonian mechanics, this is exactly how you proceed.

Why It Fails

General relativity has no universal clock—no single ticking rate that all observers share. The metric (the rule that measures distances and times) does not come pre-installed with a built-in "flow of time." If you pick a spatial grid and a time-direction on a whim, you might construct a dataset that appears to be valid initial conditions but actually violates the geometric rules the theory requires. The theory is strict: not every snapshot of space and not every choice of how to label time-flow yields a solution. Some choices are internally inconsistent with the equations themselves.

Mathematical Object

The canonical formulation rewrites the Einstein field equations into a form suited to evolution. It breaks the four-dimensional spacetime metric into pieces: a three-dimensional metric h that lives on a spatial slice (it measures distances within that slice), a lapse function α (a number at each point that controls how fast time passes at that location), and a shift vector β (a direction that shows how the spatial grid tilts or slides as you step forward in time). This repackaging turns the problem into one that looks like a dynamical system: it has degrees of freedom (h and a momentum-like field conjugate to h) and algebraic constraints these degrees of freedom must satisfy at each instant.

Operation

To use this formulation, start by proposing a three-dimensional metric h on your spatial slice and a momentum field. Then check whether they obey two constraint equations (the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint). These constraints are not differential equations—they are algebraic relations that must hold at each moment. If your h and momentum satisfy both constraints, the evolution equations (which are differential equations) tell you exactly how h and momentum change as you advance to the next slice. The lapse α and shift β are not determined by the equations; you are free to choose them. This freedom is precisely the gauge freedom—the freedom to pick your coordinates.

Why It Matters

This viewpoint is essential for understanding how gravity actually remakes spacetime over time. It separates what is determined by physics (the metric h and how its shape evolves) from what is a choice of coordinates (the lapse α and shift β). This clarity is crucial for tracking gravitational waves (which are real motions of h), for setting up initial conditions without smuggling in hidden assumptions, and for understanding how information and causality propagate forward without ambiguity about what is physical and what is merely a choice of labels.

What Breaks Without It

If you ignore the constraints and try to evolve any initial data you conjure without checking them, you produce solutions that do not represent physical spacetime. Instead, they represent artifacts of your coordinate choice—illusions created by how you labeled space and time, not real gravitational phenomena. You lose the ability to tell which features of your solution are genuine geometry and which are coordinate mirages. The subsequent calculations become unreliable because they lack a solid foundation in validated initial conditions.

Mini Example

Consider two observers standing on a sphere, separated by a small distance. At one instant, you measure the distance between them and measure the rate at which this distance is changing (they are moving apart). But "moving apart" relative to what frame? Relative to a grid anchored to the sphere's surface? Relative to a grid that is itself shrinking? The canonical formulation forces you to state exactly which: the lapse α and shift β encode that answer. Once α and β are specified, the constraint equations tell you whether your measured distance and rate-of-change form a valid snapshot of geometry. If they do, the evolution equations predict uniquely how the distance will evolve in the next instant. If they do not, you have chosen an impossible initial snapshot—one that contradicts the theory.

Common Confusion

A frequent misunderstanding is to treat the canonical formulation as an approximation or a simplified version of the full four-dimensional Einstein equations. This is backward—it is the same theory exactly, just restructured to expose the features that matter for time-evolution. Another common mix-up: learners sometimes think that α and β are determined by the physics and the equations. They are not. The lapse and shift are free choices, leftover gauge freedom. The constraint equations do not solve for α and β; they only constrain h and momentum so that any choice of α and β you make will produce a valid evolution.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine you have a snapshot of space at one instant. You can measure the shape of that space—how far apart points are, what angles are formed, how the space curves—and encode all this in a three-dimensional metric h. You can also measure how fast structures are moving and deforming. But here is the core puzzle: space by itself has no natural way to advance to the next moment. There is no "next instant" written into a spatial slice alone. General relativity does not hand you a universal clock. So you must make a decision: when you step forward in time, how do you connect the next spatial slice to this one? Do you move perpendicular to the current slice, or at an angle, or stretched differently at different places? The lapse α is your answer to how far you move forward in time; the shift β is your answer to which direction. Both are choices you make—they are coordinate decisions, not facts about physics. But here is the constraint: if you choose α and β poorly, the initial data you construct might be internally contradictory. The Einstein equations, when reorganized, demand that certain geometric relationships hold between h and how fast it is changing. These are the constraint equations—they are not new physics, just the Einstein equations translated into a form that checks whether a snapshot is valid. By writing them as algebraic constraints, you can ask: does my proposed snapshot obey the theory? If yes, then evolution is deterministic. The constraint equations plus the evolution equations tell you exactly how h transforms on the next slice, and the lapse and shift carry that slice forward. This separation—between what physics determines (h and its momentum) and what you choose (α and β)—is the gift the canonical formulation gives you.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Start by writing the spacetime metric as a collection of pieces. The full four-dimensional metric gets decomposed into a three-dimensional metric h that sits on a spatial slice (h measures distances within that slice), a lapse function α at each point (α = 1 means time flows at the standard rate; α = 2 means time flows twice as fast at that location; α = 1/2 means it flows half as fast), and a shift vector β (a direction at each point that shows how a spatial coordinate grid "slides" sideways as you step forward in time). The Hamiltonian constraint is an algebraic equation relating h, its momentum field, and the matter content (mass and stress present at that location). The momentum constraint is another algebraic equation relating the momentum and the momentum density. These constraints must be satisfied at each instant on the slice; they do not involve time-derivatives, so they are not evolution equations. The actual evolution equations are differential equations (they involve how quantities change with time) that determine how h and its momentum transform from one slice to the next. Since α and β are not restricted by any equation, they remain free parameters: this freedom is exactly the gauge freedom—your right to pick coordinates however you wish. The power of this repackaging is that it splits the problem neatly: first solve the constraints to identify which initial configurations are allowed, then solve the evolution equations to compute the future.

Where This Idea Itself Breaks Down

The canonical formulation assumes the spatial slices can be parametrized smoothly throughout time without developing caustics (focal points where infinitesimally separated geodesics cross). Near black-hole singularities or in the deep interior of collapsing matter, the spatial slicing degenerates: either the lapse α → 0 (coordinate time stops), or the shift vector becomes ill-defined, breaking the ability to smoothly evolve h and momentum forward.

Additionally, the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints assume the metric components and their conjugates can be independently specified on an initial slice. This fails when choosing data that violates causality or when back-reaction and matter-field gradients are so steep that a single spatial slice cannot capture the dynamics accurately.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without this split, general relativity is a labyrinth. The full Einstein field equations are ten coupled nonlinear partial differential equations in four spacetime dimensions, and they give no hint about which parts represent real physics and which parts represent your arbitrary coordinate choice. The canonical formulation cuts through this fog: it defines a space of all possible spatial geometries and momenta, marks out which subset (defined by satisfying the constraints) is actually physical, and makes evolution transparent and unique. Without this structure, numerical simulations of gravity collapse into confusion, searches for gravitational waves drown in noise from coordinate artifacts, and attempts to quantize gravity lose all guidance. With it, the theory becomes a tool you can actually use: you can separate signal from noise, you can predict what will happen, and you can build physical intuition about how spacetime actually evolves.

Where It Sits In The Course

The canonical formulation is the hinge where the course turns from describing spacetime as a static geometric object (topics like smooth spaces, the metric, and curvature) toward describing it as a system that changes over time (how slices evolve, how waves ripple through it, how causality flows). It connects backward to the metric and curvature (which determine what h looks like and how it behaves) and forward to gravitational waves (which are undulations in h that propagate according to the evolution equations), to cosmology (where α and β encode the expansion of the universe and the foliation of spacetime into slices), and to black holes (where the constraints hold even at the event horizon, but α drops to zero—a sign that the horizon is null and light itself cannot escape).

Connective Thread

The canonical formulation is the bridge from viewing spacetime as a static scaffold (described by concepts like smooth spaces, straightest paths, and the metric) to viewing it as a living, evolving system. It answers the question: if spacetime is not a fixed stage but an entity that changes, what rules govern its evolution? It answers by reorganizing Einstein's equations into initial-value form—a form where you specify initial conditions and let evolution equations calculate the future, all while respecting the coordinate freedom that general relativity permits. Every topic that follows—gravitational waves, the dynamics of black holes, the expansion of the universe—depends on this initial-value picture. They all describe how particular geometries evolve and change, not how static configurations sit unchanging in time.
