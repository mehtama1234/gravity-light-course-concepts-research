Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

You see a distant galaxy, but its light has passed near a massive galaxy between you and it. The massive galaxy's gravity warps spacetime, bending the light path. Now the distant galaxy appears in the wrong place in the sky, magnified, or even split into multiple images. We need to understand how gravity bends light rays without pretending it pulls on light like a breeze pushes dust.

Naive Picture

You might imagine gravity pulls on light rays sideways, like wind bends a thrown ball: the stronger the pull, the sharper the bend. Each ray gets deflected by some angle that depends on how close it passes to the mass.

Why It Fails

That picture treats light rays as if they follow one path at a time, each responding independently to gravity. But light does not respond to a separate gravitational force. Light rays are shaped by the geometry of spacetime itself. When spacetime is curved, straight lines do not exist anymore—what we call a light ray is actually a geodesic (the path of zero-acceleration) in that curved geometry. A ray near a mass and a ray far away follow different geodesics, so they diverge or converge together. You cannot explain convergence or divergence by pointing at individual rays.

Mathematical Object

A bundle of light rays close together is called a congruence of null geodesics (null means the paths are light paths; congruence means a family filling a region). At each moment in spacetime, you have a small patch of rays pointing in nearly the same direction. The congruence is the rule that tells you, at every nearby point in spacetime, which direction a light ray points. Spacetime curvature acts on this whole bundle: it can squeeze it, stretch it, tilt it, or twist it.

Operation

Take a congruence of light rays and move a small distance perpendicular to them. Measure how the cross-sectional area changes, how the rays' directions shift relative to each other, and whether the rays are rotating as a group. You are not measuring single rays; you are measuring how the bundle transforms. This is called the expansion, shear, and twist of the congruence.

Why It Matters

This operation is how gravity reveals itself through light. When light passes a massive object, spacetime curves, and the light congruence's cross-section shrinks (focusing) or grows (diverging). If you measure whether images appear magnified, split, or displaced, you are really measuring how the light congruence was reshaped by spacetime curvature. This is the window gravity opens to observers: we cannot see curvature directly, but we can see how it focuses or scatters light.

What Breaks Without It

If you only track single rays and their angles, magnification becomes a separate puzzle from image position, and time delays become another separate calculation. You lose the reason they are connected. The physics is that a converging bundle of rays means more light energy arrives at a point (magnification), which must come from deflected rays, which creates multiple images. Without the congruence language, these are three disconnected tricks instead of one unified effect.

Mini Example

Imagine light from a star traveling toward Earth, but it passes within 1000 kilometers of a black hole. Near the black hole, spacetime curves so sharply that light rays that started out nearly parallel are forced to converge. Far from the black hole, those same rays were heading toward different parts of Earth's telescope. The convergence means more light arrives, so the star appears brighter—the magnification. The rays that now converge came from different parts of the light cone, so the light we receive appears to come from a slightly shifted direction. The bundle told us the brightening and the shift are one effect, not two separate facts.

Common Confusion

People often think lensing means light is attracted to mass like a charged particle is attracted to a magnet. But the gravitational lensing of light has nothing to do with light having mass or responding to a force field. Light travels in straight lines—in the curved spacetime's geometry. Those straight lines happen to converge near a mass because spacetime itself is curved. The convergence is a property of geometry, not of light. A different kind of particle would follow a different geodesic (because it has mass and travels slower), and it would be deflected by a different amount. Light is special because it travels along null geodesics, which are the fastest possible paths—and the shape of those paths reveals the deepest structure of spacetime.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Picture light traveling across space as a river of rays, not just one ray. Far from any mass, these rays travel straight, like a uniform stream. But when they pass near a massive object, spacetime curves. A curved space is not like a surface drawn inside flat space; it is the geometry that all paths must follow. Light rays must follow geodesics—paths of zero acceleration—in whatever geometry they inhabit. When spacetime is curved, nearby geodesics are not parallel anymore. Some converge, some diverge, some twist around each other. This is not because light has mass or is being pulled by a force. It is because the stage itself is curved, and straight lines do not exist on a curved stage. A congruence is the family of all those light rays together. Measuring how the bundle's cross-section changes tells us the strength and shape of spacetime curvature that the light experienced. A shrinking cross-section means gravity focused the light—more light energy at a point, so we see magnification. A growing cross-section means gravity scattered it. By watching how light bundles converge or diverge, we are reading the curvature of spacetime without measuring it directly. This is why light is the clearest messenger: it tells us about geometry through focusing and imaging.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Suppose you have a bundle of light rays parameterized by their position (three coordinates) and their direction (three more). The rays evolve forward in time according to the geodesic equation: d²xᵃ/dλ² + Γᵃᵦᵧ (dxᵝ/dλ)(dxᵞ/dλ) = 0, where λ is a parameter along the ray, Γ are the Christoffel symbols (they encode how spacetime curves; you can compute them from the metric gᵃᵦ, which measures distances in curved space), and the notation dxᵃ/dλ is the ray's direction. Now take two nearby rays in the bundle. The difference between them grows or shrinks. The rate of change of that separation is governed by the Riemann curvature tensor Rᵃᵦᵧᵨ—it measures the intrinsic twisting and bending of spacetime. The expansion θ (how fast the bundle's area changes) obeys dθ/dλ = −Rᵃᵦ kᵃ kᵇ/2, where kᵃ is the direction of the rays and Rᵃᵦ = Rᶜᵃᶜᵦ is the Ricci curvature. The Ricci tensor encodes which directions in spacetime are curved; for light in vacuum, Rᵃᵦ = 0 (following Einstein's equation with zero matter), but the full Riemann tensor is nonzero, so light still focuses.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

General relativity is built on a single claim: the geometry of spacetime encodes gravity, not a separate force. Without understanding how families of light paths behave in curved geometry, you cannot see how the theory works at all. Light gives us the only way to test whether spacetime is truly curved, or whether it just looks different from one coordinate system to another. If you reduce lensing to individual ray deflections, you have lost the core insight that gravity is geometry.

Where It Sits In The Course

Lensing connects the machinery of geodesics (how objects move in curved space) to the Einstein equation (how matter curves space) through a concrete observation: light congruences. The nearby concepts—the Riemann and Ricci tensors, the metric, null geodesics—all converge here. Lensing is the bridge where geometry becomes visible: you measure light's focus and compute the curvature that caused it.

Connective Thread

Earlier topics establish that light follows null geodesics and that the Einstein equation governs how matter curves spacetime. This page shows why that machinery matters: it predicts which light rays converge, creating images and magnification. Later topics use this understanding to measure redshift (light is stretched by geometry), to understand horizons (even light congruences cannot escape), and to detect gravitational waves (they are propagating waves of spacetime curvature that focus and defocus light bundles). Without lensing, those topics would be isolated calculations; with it, they form one story of how geometry and observation connect.
