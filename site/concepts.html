Concepts

Foundations

Topology and Continuity

You want to talk about curves being smooth, functions being continuous, and nearby points staying nearby under a transformation. But before you measure *how close* points are—before rulers and meters—you need a language for *which* points are near each other at all.

 Foundations

Manifolds and Local Flatness

You want to describe a curved world using local measurements. A surveyor on Earth uses a flat map of their neighborhood, not a globe. Physics works the same way: observers carry clocks and rulers and measure what's nearby. But where do those local measurements fit into a bigger picture that might be curved or strange?

 Foundations

Multilinear Algebra

Imagine measuring the force on a stretched rubber sheet when you push in two directions at once. The force depends on both your push directions together, not one at a time. Physics constantly needs to describe how something responds to multiple inputs — directions, slopes, flows — and keep track of which inputs matter and how they combine.

 Foundations

Tangent Spaces and Fields

You need to describe motion at each event in spacetime—what direction a particle could move next. You also need to assign local measurements like temperature or field strength to every point. Without a language for "possible next steps" and "point-by-point quantities," physics has no way to express local physics.

 Geometry of Gravity

Connections and Parallel Transport

Walk north on a globe, holding a compass pointing east. At the equator, your compass still points east. At the north pole, it has rotated ninety degrees. How do you measure whether the direction actually changed, or whether the surface itself twisted it?

 Geometry of Gravity

Curvature

Two astronauts in free fall near each other will drift together or apart. Not because they push or pull, but because spacetime itself is shaped. You need a local way to measure that drifting—to say 'here, nearby motion diverges from what flat space would predict.'

 Geometry of Gravity

Metric Manifolds

Imagine laying a path on a curved hillside. You can describe which spots are near each other, but you cannot yet say how far apart they are, how fast something travels between them, or which paths a light ray can take. You need a rule that assigns a measurement—a notion of distance and time interval—to every tiny step on the surface.

 Dynamics and Matter

Symmetry and Conservation

When you observe a physical system, you need a way to pick out the quantities that don't change—the conserved ones. In a sphere, symmetry tells you that rotation leaves the shape untouched. In spacetime, if the geometry looks the same in every direction, what remains unchanged when you act out that sameness?

 Dynamics and Matter

Integration on Manifolds

You want to add up quantities spread across a curved surface—rainfall over a country, the charge in a region of space, the mass in a galaxy—without your answer depending on which coordinate system you picked. The earth is curved, but you need one reliable total.

 Dynamics and Matter

Matter and Stress-Energy

Gravity must account for what makes matter curve spacetime: not just mass, but energy, momentum, pressure, and internal stresses all push back on geometry. Newton thought mass was enough, but relativity reveals energy and momentum are faces of one unified object.

 Dynamics and Matter

Einstein Equation

We can see that gravity bends the paths of planets and light, and we suspect that this bending is really the shape of space and time. But seeing is not a law. We need a precise rule that says exactly how much the presence of matter and energy warps the geometry around it, and how that warping in turn steers everything moving through it.

 Light and Observation

Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics

You watch starlight bend around the Sun, or light hover at a black hole's edge. The light isn't being pulled by a force—it's following a path the curved spacetime itself prescribes. We need a language for 'paths light naturally takes through warped geometry.'

 Dynamics and Matter

Canonical Formulation and Constraints

Imagine filming a swelling soap bubble. Each frame shows the bubble's shape, but the film itself doesn't tell you what happens next: you need to know both how it looks now and how fast it's changing. General relativity faces the same problem—spacetime has no preferred "flow of time", so we must start from a frozen snapshot of space and ask what rules govern its evolution to the next snapshot.

 Applications

Cosmology and Expansion

When you try to apply gravity's equation to the whole universe, you face an impossible task: track every galaxy, every star, every atom. Physicists need a way to capture the universe's large-scale behavior without that hopeless detail work.

 Applications

Black Holes and Horizons

Imagine you throw a rocket upward from Earth. Make it fast enough, and it escapes to infinity. Near a black hole, something stranger happens: even light itself—the fastest thing that exists—cannot climb back out once it crosses a certain boundary. The puzzle is this: what exactly is that boundary?

 Light and Observation

Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves

Spacetime around a binary star is nearly flat except for a tiny ripple carrying energy away. We need a way to study that small ripple separately from the flat background, measure what it carries, and predict how instruments on Earth will see it.

 Foundations

Open Sets As Neighborhood Tests

When you step into a room, you can move a few inches in any direction without walking out the door. Before you have a ruler or a distance formula, you know you have room to move. In the same way, when studying geometry and relativity, the very first thing you need is a way to say which regions give you that kind of breathing room around their points — before you've defined what "distance" even means.

 Foundations

Continuous Maps Preserve Nearness

Two thermometers measure the same room but use different scales. When real temperatures are close together, do the two thermometers always read nearby numbers? How do you know that switching from Celsius to Fahrenheit doesn't secretly create jumps between nearby temperatures?

 Foundations

Charts, Atlases, and Overlap Rules

You're studying a sphere or a black hole or spacetime itself. One coordinate system works fine in some regions but breaks elsewhere—either mathematically or physically. You need a way to tile the whole space with overlapping local maps and stitch them together so the physics doesn't depend on which map you pick.

 Foundations

Smoothness As Allowed Change

You're describing a curved surface—say a sphere—by cutting it into flat patches and labeling each patch with coordinates. But coordinates are human choice: you could relabel the same patch in infinitely many ways. When you take a derivative in one labeling system and then switch to another, does the derivative give you the same physical fact, or does it depend on your choice? The course needs that question settled before it can trust derivatives at all.

 Foundations

Dual Vectors Measure Directions

In physics, some quantities are arrows (velocity, force, direction of motion) and some are measuring tools (pressure, temperature gradient, intensity of light). The course needs to distinguish them—they transform differently under coordinate changes, and mixing them breaks the math.

 Foundations

Tensor Components Versus The Object

When you measure the same physical thing in different coordinate systems (different rulers, different starting points, different rotation angles), the numbers you write down change. But the thing itself—the rule for how to measure it—must stay the same. How do you keep track of both the changeable numbers and the unchangeable rule?

 Foundations

Tangent Vectors As Derivatives

You stand on a hillside and want to measure how temperature changes as you step in one direction versus another. But what exactly is 'a direction at a point'? Spacetime isn't sitting inside a flat container where you can draw arrows from the outside. You need a way to define direction that lives *within* the space itself.

 Foundations

Vector Fields As Local Instructions

Imagine a landscape where at every point—a mountain summit, a valley floor, anywhere—there is a direction you can move or a velocity you inherit. You need language to assign one direction per location and track how directions change as you move. That is the problem: how do you capture motion or instruction that varies smoothly from place to place?

 Geometry of Gravity

Covariant Derivative Corrects Comparison

Imagine measuring a vector field (like an arrow field) along a curved surface. When you compare two nearby arrows—one at your current point, one slightly ahead—how do you know if a difference comes from the field actually changing or just from the surface's shape bending the comparison frame?

 Geometry of Gravity

Geodesics As Free Motion

Imagine a stone floating in space, far from all gravity and obstacles. It travels in a straight line. But what does "straight" mean when space itself is curved? In flat space the answer is obvious, but near a black hole or inside the warped spacetime around a star, there is no global flat background to measure against. The course needs a way to say "this particle is moving freely" using only the shape of spacetime itself.

 Geometry of Gravity

Parallel Transport Loop Test

Imagine you draw an arrow on a stretched rubber sheet, then move your finger around a closed curve while keeping the arrow pointing in the same direction—at least, in the way your finger itself defines 'same direction.' When your finger returns to the start, does the arrow point where it began? On a flat sheet it does. On a curved sheet like a sphere, it may not. You need a test that catches this without stepping outside the surface to peek at how it bends.

 Geometry of Gravity

Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change

Picture two friends standing close together on Earth's surface, both facing north. If they each walk 100 meters north, then 100 meters east, they end up at slightly different spots — the Earth's curvature got in the way. A mathematician needs a precise object that says: 'Taking steps in different orders leaves a mismatch.' That object is Riemann curvature.

 Dynamics and Matter

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Imagine a sphere: if you send nearby particles all in straight paths outward from the center, do they draw together or spread apart? The same question applies to spacetime itself. You need to measure whether paths that feel straight (geodesics—roads objects naturally follow when nothing pushes them) bunch closer or fan wider. That bundling or spreading is what Ricci curvature tracks.

 Light and Observation

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

 Geometry of Gravity

Proper Time As Clock Reading

Two people set off on a journey through space and time, each wearing a wristwatch. They meet again years later. Their watches show different elapsed times. How do we describe what each clock actually counted?

 Dynamics and Matter

Killing Fields As Symmetry Directions

Imagine a spinning merry-go-round where every seat looks identical as you turn it. Or a straight-line train track that looks the same no matter where you walk along it. You want to say: 'This geometry has a symmetry.' But how do you capture that as a direction you can point to, so the math can use it?

 Dynamics and Matter

Stokes' Theorem As Boundary-Bulk Accounting

You have a region of space—say, a ball of gas, or a patch of spacetime. Something is flowing through it: energy, particles, momentum. You want to know the total amount in the region. One way: look inside, measure everything. Another way: stand at the surface and count what crosses the boundary. These two must agree if the accounting is honest.

 Dynamics and Matter

Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry

Imagine a rubber sheet with weights on it; the weights push down and the rubber curves. If you write an equation balancing the two sides—weights on the right, curvature on the left—both must follow the same accounting rules. Matter doesn't vanish or appear; it's conserved locally. Geometry must obey the same bookkeeping, or the equation is nonsense.

 Applications

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Imagine weighing all the matter in the universe—galaxies, gas, everything we can see. When physicists do this and ask whether the universe should be expanding faster or slower, the numbers don't match what telescopes show. Something else is pushing the expansion. That something is invisible and doesn't clump into galaxies like ordinary matter does.

 Light and Observation

Redshift As Stretched Light

You measure starlight in a telescope and count the wave peaks in time. When the star is far away in an expanding universe, the waves arrive stretched. The wavelength is longer, the frequency lower. The light is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. You need to know: is this redshift because the star is moving away, or because space itself is expanding between you and the star, or because the star is in strong gravity? These look identical in the data.

 Light and Observation

Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

You see a distant galaxy, but its light has passed near a massive galaxy between you and it. The massive galaxy's gravity warps spacetime, bending the light path. Now the distant galaxy appears in the wrong place in the sky, magnified, or even split into multiple images. We need to understand how gravity bends light rays without pretending it pulls on light like a breeze pushes dust.

 Dynamics and Matter

Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints

You want to predict the future of spacetime itself. But not every snapshot of space you imagine can actually occur at the start of a real spacetime. Something has to check whether a snapshot is lawful before you evolve it forward.

 Applications

Scale Factor As Expansion History

A universe with millions of galaxies scattered throughout space doesn't stay still—distances between them change over time. You need one number, varying with time, that captures this change in a unified way for the whole spacetime.

 Applications

Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and see that galaxies are getting farther apart over time. But just knowing the distance grows is not enough—you need a way to ask: *how fast* is space expanding right now? Is it doubling every billion years or every trillion years? That difference is everything for predicting the future of the universe.

 Applications

Event Horizon As Causal Boundary

Picture a river with a waterfall. A swimmer beyond a certain line cannot send a message upstream to observers before the waterfall—not because there's a physical wall, but because the water's flow forbids it. Black holes work the same way: from certain regions, no signal (not even light) can travel outward to reach distant observers. We need a precise definition of that boundary based on what signals are allowed to do.

 Applications

Penrose Diagrams Compress Infinity

When you draw a black hole or the far future of the universe on paper, ordinary distances make infinity go to the edge of the page, and the most important regions vanish. But the question we care about—can a light ray escape to infinity, or does it fall in?—depends on directions and slopes, not distances. You need a way to see the whole spacetime story on one finite page.

 Light and Observation

Gauge Change Versus Physical Change

A LIGO detector measures tiny ripples in spacetime, but the measurement apparatus itself was built on Earth using a coordinate system—a choice of which direction we call up, which way is north. When you write down small changes in the metric, you don't know if the numbers changed because spacetime actually rippled or because you switched to a different coordinate grid. How do you tell the difference?

 Light and Observation

Linearized Equations For Small Disturbances

You have a complicated equation—say, describing how a wave travels through spacetime—but solving it exactly is hopeless. You know the answer is close to something simple, with just a small wobble added. How do you isolate that tiny wobble and calculate it without solving the whole monster?

 Light and Observation

Quadrupole Sources Need Changing Shape

When two stars orbit each other, their combined mass stays put but its shape keeps changing. We need to know: does this motion radiate gravitational waves? If so, how much?

 Light and Observation

Strain As Relative Length Change

Imagine two long, straight metal tubes held in space very carefully, perpendicular to each other, with mirrors at the ends. When a gravitational wave passes through, the distances between the mirrors shift — one gets slightly longer, one slightly shorter. How do you measure something so tiny that the change is far smaller than an atom?

 Light and Observation

Interferometer Light As A Ruler

A gravitational wave squeezes and stretches space itself. We need a way to measure how much—to turn the invisible wiggle in the metric into a number an experiment can record. A mechanical ruler won't work: the stretching is too small and happens to the space the ruler sits in, not the ruler separately.
