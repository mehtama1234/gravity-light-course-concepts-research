Foundations

Topology and Continuity

You need to describe curves that don't jump, and functions that keep nearby points nearby. But before you can say what 'nearby' means using measurements, you need to say which points count as neighbors to each other at all.

 Foundations

Manifolds and Local Flatness

You want to describe a curved world using only local measurements. A surveyor on Earth uses a flat map of their neighborhood, not a globe. Physics works the same way: observers carry clocks and rulers and measure only what is nearby. But how do those local measurements fit into a bigger picture that curves or has strange properties?

 Foundations

Multilinear Algebra

Push a rubber sheet in two directions at once. The sheet pushes back differently than if you pushed it in just one direction. Physics constantly describes how something reacts when you apply multiple directions, slopes, or flows at the same time — and it matters which direction goes with which response.

 Foundations

Tangent Spaces and Fields

At any moment and place in spacetime, a particle or light ray can move in many different directions. At the same moment, you need to measure things that vary from point to point—temperature, pressure, electric field strength. Without a way to say "these are the directions available here" and "this is how quantities change as you move," physics cannot describe what actually happens at each specific event.

 Geometry of Gravity

Connections and Parallel Transport

Walk north on a globe, holding a compass pointing east. At the equator, your compass still points east. At the north pole, it has rotated ninety degrees. How do you measure whether the direction actually changed, or whether the surface itself twisted it?

 Geometry of Gravity

Curvature

Two astronauts floating freely next to each other will gradually move closer together or farther apart. Not because they push or pull, but because the space around them is bent. We need a way to measure this drift and compare it to what would happen in flat, unbent space.

 Geometry of Gravity

Metric Manifolds

Imagine laying a path on a curved hillside. You can point to which spots are next to each other, but you cannot yet answer: how far apart? How fast does something move between them? Which paths can light take? To answer these, you need a rule—a measurement device that works at every tiny piece of the surface and tells you the distance and time interval for any small step.

 Dynamics and Matter

Symmetry and Conservation

When you study any physical system, you search for quantities that never change—conserved ones. A sphere stays the same shape when you rotate it. In spacetime near a black hole, if the geometry looks the same in all directions, what stays unchanged when you apply that sameness as a transformation?

 Dynamics and Matter

Integration on Manifolds

You want to add up quantities spread across a curved surface—rainfall over a country, the charge in a region of space, the mass in a galaxy—without your answer depending on which coordinate system you picked. The Earth is curved, but you need one reliable total.

 Dynamics and Matter

Matter and Stress-Energy

Gravity bends space and time. But what causes the bending? Newton said: mass. Einstein's answer is richer. Mass does bend spacetime—but so does energy, momentum (motion), pressure (crowding), and internal stress. Relativity treats all of these as one unified object, because energy and momentum are really the same thing seen from different angles. Gravity must respond to all of them together.

 Dynamics and Matter

Einstein Equation

We can see that gravity bends the paths of planets and light, and we suspect that this bending is really the shape of space and time. But seeing is not a law. We need a precise rule that says exactly how much the presence of matter and energy warps the geometry around it, and how that warping in turn steers everything moving through it.

 Light and Observation

Optical Geometry and Null Geodesics

Watch starlight bend as it passes the Sun, or see light frozen just outside a black hole. That bending is not gravity yanking the light sideways like a magnet pulls iron. Instead, the light is following a path that the curved shape of space and time itself creates. We need words to describe 'which paths does light actually travel through a warped world?'

 Dynamics and Matter

Canonical Formulation and Constraints

Imagine filming a soap bubble as it swells. Each frame captures the bubble's shape at one moment, but the film alone does not tell you what the bubble will do next. You need two pieces of information: what it looks like right now, and how fast it is changing. General relativity runs into the same puzzle with spacetime. Spacetime has no universal clock ticking everywhere at the same rate, so we cannot simply say "here is the geometry now, here is how fast it is changing—go compute the future." We must build a language that does not assume a natural time-flow.

 Applications

Cosmology and Expansion

When you write down Einstein's equation for gravity, it describes how curved space-and-time changes in response to matter and energy. To use it for the whole universe, you would need to know the exact arrangement of every galaxy, star, and atom — an infinite amount of detail. Physicists needed a shortcut: a simpler rule that captures the universe's large-scale behavior without tracking everything.

 Applications

Black Holes and Horizons

Throw a ball upward from Earth. Make it go fast enough—11 kilometers per second—and it never comes back. Now imagine a much heavier object: a collapsed star so dense and tightly packed that the escape speed becomes the speed of light itself. Nothing can go faster than light. So nothing can escape. The question is simple: where exactly does this prison boundary lie?

 Light and Observation

Perturbation Theory and Gravitational Waves

Two stars orbit each other and gradually spiral inward. As they do, they send out ripples in spacetime itself—disturbances in the shape and curvature of space and time—and these ripples carry away energy. We need a way to study those ripples on their own, measure what energy they carry, and predict how instruments on Earth (like laser interferometers that measure tiny length changes) will detect them.

 Foundations

Open Sets As Neighborhood Tests

When you step into a room, you can move a few inches in any direction without walking out the door. Before you have a ruler or a distance formula, you know you have room to move. In the same way, when studying geometry and relativity, the very first thing you need is a way to say which regions give you that kind of breathing room around their points — before you've defined what "distance" even means.

 Foundations

Continuous Maps Preserve Nearness

You measure the temperature of a room two ways: once with a Celsius thermometer, once with a Fahrenheit one. When the real air temperature changes by a tiny bit, do both thermometers show tiny changes, or can one jump wildly while the other creeps? How do you know that converting between temperature scales doesn't secretly hide jumps?

 Foundations

Charts, Atlases, and Overlap Rules

You are studying a sphere, a black hole, or the whole of spacetime. One coordinate system (a grid of numbers labeling points) works fine in some regions but fails in others—either the math breaks down or the physics doesn't make sense there. You need a practical way to cover the entire space with overlapping local grids, each working perfectly in its own zone, and then stitch them together so that any physical fact stays true no matter which grid you're using.

 Foundations

Smoothness As Allowed Change

You're drawing a bumpy surface—like Earth—using flat maps. Each map covers a region with coordinates (longitude, latitude). But any overlap region could be labeled infinitely many ways. When you calculate how fast something changes using one map, then switch to another map covering the same spot, does that rate of change stay the same, or does it flip based on which labels you picked? Before the course can trust any calculation involving rates of change, this has to be answered.

 Foundations

Dual Vectors Measure Directions

Physics tracks two types of quantities: arrows (velocity, force) show direction and speed of motion, while measuring rules (pressure gradient, light intensity) ask 'how much flows in this direction?' Treating them identically breaks the math because they transform opposite ways when you shift coordinates.

 Foundations

Tensor Components Versus The Object

You measure something with a ruler, and you write down numbers. Your friend measures the same thing with a different ruler, or from a different starting point, or with the ruler rotated. They write down different numbers. Yet you're both measuring the same physical thing. The rule you use to measure stays real and stays the same. How do you write down one rule that produces true results no matter which ruler or starting point you pick?

 Foundations

Tangent Vectors As Derivatives

You stand on a hillside and want to measure how temperature changes as you step in one direction versus another. But what exactly is 'a direction at a point'? In Einstein's picture, spacetime—the thing that makes up everything—isn't sitting inside some larger flat container. There is no external reference frame to draw arrows from. So you need a way to define what 'a direction' even means using only what is built into spacetime itself.

 Foundations

Vector Fields As Local Instructions

Imagine a landscape where at every point—a mountain summit, a valley floor, anywhere—there is a direction you can move or a velocity you inherit. You need language to assign one direction per location and track how directions change as you move. That is the problem: how do you capture motion or instruction that varies smoothly from place to place?

 Geometry of Gravity

Covariant Derivative Corrects Comparison

You are standing on Earth's curved surface with a compass pointing north. Walk a few steps forward, and measure the compass direction again. The direction changed—did the Earth's magnetic field actually shift, or did your position on the curve bend your frame of reference? This is the core puzzle: how do you compare arrows (vectors) at two different locations on a curved surface when each location has its own local sense of direction?

 Geometry of Gravity

Geodesics As Free Motion

A stone drifts through empty space, far from stars, gravity, and obstacles. It moves in a straight line forever. But what is straight? On a flat piece of paper the answer is trivial—draw a ruler. Yet near a black hole or inside the curved spacetime around a massive star, there is no background flat space to measure against. You cannot use a ruler because spacetime itself is the only thing that exists. The course needs a way to say: "this particle is moving with no forces pushing it" using only the shape of spacetime, nothing outside it.

 Geometry of Gravity

Parallel Transport Loop Test

Picture a rubber sheet with an arrow drawn on it. You put your finger on the sheet and slide it around a closed loop—a path that starts and ends at the same spot. As your finger moves, you try to keep the arrow pointing in the same direction: 'same' means according to how the sheet itself feels locally, not according to some outside room. When your finger gets back to the start, does the arrow point the same way it did at the beginning? On a flat sheet, yes. On a curved sheet—like the surface of a globe—the answer might be no. You need a test that reveals this twist without ever stepping off the surface to measure it from outside.

 Geometry of Gravity

Riemann Curvature As Noncommuting Change

Imagine two friends standing very close together on Earth's curved surface, both facing north. They each walk 100 meters forward (north), then 100 meters to the right (east). If Earth were flat, they'd meet up at the same spot. But Earth is curved, so they land slightly apart — neither of them moved wrong, the space itself changed their directions as they walked. A mathematician needs a precise way to capture this: a statement that says 'the order in which you take steps matters on a curved surface.' That is what Riemann curvature measures.

 Dynamics and Matter

Ricci Curvature As Volume Change

Picture a sphere. Stand at the north pole and send a bunch of particles outward in straight lines, like longitude lines on a globe. Watch what happens: as they travel, they get closer together—they converge. Now imagine a saddle (the curved surface of a horse saddle). Do the same thing—send particles in straight lines outward from a point. This time they spread apart. This bundling or spreading is the question: when objects follow their natural straight paths through curved space, do those paths squeeze together or fan wider? Ricci curvature measures exactly that.

 Light and Observation

Metric Signature And Light Cones

Imagine a road where some journeys are forbidden. A traveler can walk to a neighbor's house, but no signal can arrive faster than light. In spacetime, we need a rule for which paths are even possible: Can you reach this event from that one? Can light connect them? The metric, a measurement device for distances, must answer these questions.

 Geometry of Gravity

Proper Time As Clock Reading

Two people set off on journeys through space and time, each with a wristwatch. They meet years later. Their watches show different amounts of elapsed time. The question: how do we describe and measure what each clock actually counted?

 Dynamics and Matter

Killing Fields As Symmetry Directions

Imagine a spinning merry-go-round where every seat looks identical as you turn it. Or a straight train track that looks the same no matter where you stand on it. You want to say: 'This geometry has a symmetry.' But how do you write that down as a direction in space, so the math can grab it and use it?

 Dynamics and Matter

Stokes' Theorem As Boundary-Bulk Accounting

You have a region of space—say, a cloud of gas or a chunk of spacetime. Stuff is flowing through it: energy, particles, momentum. You want to know how much is in the region right now. Method one: zoom in everywhere inside and measure. Method two: stand at the edge and watch what enters and leaves. These two answers must match, or your accounting is broken.

 Dynamics and Matter

Einstein Tensor As Conserved Geometry

Picture a rubber sheet. Place a weight on it and it sags. Now write an equation: weight on one side, how much the sheet curves on the other. Both sides have to follow the same rule—nothing appears or disappears without reason. The weight doesn't vanish, and the sheet's curvature doesn't either. If your equation doesn't respect this, it's broken.

 Applications

Cosmological Constant As Vacuum Term

Astronomers measure how fast galaxies move apart—the universe is expanding. If you add up all the matter they can see (gas, dust, stars, and the invisible dark matter holding galaxies together), gravity's pull should slow that expansion down. But the actual measurements show something strange: the expansion is speeding up, not slowing. Something invisible is pushing outward, and it doesn't gather into clumps like matter does.

 Light and Observation

Redshift As Stretched Light

When you look at starlight through a telescope, you measure the distance between repeating peaks in the waves—the wavelength. For distant stars in an expanding universe, something strange happens: the peaks arrive farther apart than they left the star. The waves are stretched. They shift toward the red end of the light spectrum, where the wavelengths are longer and the jiggles slower. The question is: why? Did the star move away from you (like a siren's wail stretching as an ambulance recedes)? Did the space between you and the star get bigger (stretching the peaks apart just from the distance growing)? Or is the star buried in strong gravity where time itself runs slowly? All three look identical when you only look at the light.

 Light and Observation

Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

Look at a distant galaxy through a telescope. Between you and it sits another galaxy so massive that the space and time around it are bent. When light from the far galaxy travels toward you, it passes through this warped region. The bent space steers the light off its straight path. You see the distant galaxy in the wrong spot in the sky. Sometimes it looks magnified—brighter and larger. Sometimes it splits into two or three images of the same galaxy. We need a way to understand this bending without pretending gravity is a force pulling on light.

 Dynamics and Matter

Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints

You want to predict how spacetime will change going forward. But here is the catch: not every picture of space you can draw right now is one that could actually exist at the start of a real spacetime. Before you let evolution equations work on a snapshot, something has to check whether that snapshot is allowed by Einstein's rules.

 Applications

Scale Factor As Expansion History

Imagine you scatter thousands of dots across a piece of rubber, each dot a galaxy. As the rubber stretches, every dot moves away from every other dot. You want one number—call it a—that changes with time and tells you how much that stretching has happened at any moment. That single number should capture the whole story of how distances in the universe change.

 Applications

Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and notice that galaxies are pulling apart from each other—getting farther away over time. But knowing the distance grows is only half the picture. You need to know *how fast* this is happening right now, at this moment. Is space doubling in size every billion years, or every trillion years? That difference changes everything about what happens to the universe in the future.

 Applications

Event Horizon As Causal Boundary

Picture a river flowing over a waterfall. At some point downstream, no swimmer can send a message upstream to someone sitting before the falls—not because a wall blocks them, but because the water's motion forbids it. A light beam behaves the same way near a black hole: past a certain boundary, light cannot travel outward to reach observers who are far away. We need a clear way to define that boundary using what signals are and are not allowed to do.

 Applications

Penrose Diagrams Compress Infinity

When you draw a black hole or the far future of the universe on paper, ordinary distances make infinity go to the edge of the page, and the most important regions vanish. But the question we care about—can a light ray escape to infinity, or does it fall in?—depends on directions and slopes, not distances. You need a way to see the whole spacetime story on one finite page.

 Light and Observation

Gauge Change Versus Physical Change

LIGO is a detector that watches for tiny ripples in spacetime itself. But LIGO was built on Earth, and someone had to choose a coordinate system—decide which direction is up, which way is north. When numbers in the metric change, you face a puzzle: did spacetime actually ripple, or did the numbers shift just because you switched to a different coordinate grid? How do you tell the difference?

 Light and Observation

Linearized Equations For Small Disturbances

You have a complicated equation—say, describing how a wave travels through spacetime—but solving it exactly is hopeless. You know the answer is close to something simple, with just a small wobble added. How do you isolate that tiny wobble and calculate it without solving the whole monster?

 Light and Observation

Quadrupole Sources Need Changing Shape

Two stars orbit each other. Their total mass doesn't move, but the way that mass is arranged—its shape—keeps shifting. The question is: as they orbit, do they send ripples through spacetime? If so, how strong are those ripples?

 Light and Observation

Strain As Relative Length Change

Two long metal tubes sit in space, standing perpendicular to each other, with mirrors at their ends. A gravitational wave ripples through — spacetime itself flexes. The mirrors move closer together in one direction and farther apart in the other. You want to measure this motion, but the distances shift by amounts smaller than an atom. How do you notice something so impossibly small?

 Light and Observation

Interferometer Light As A Ruler

A gravitational wave squeezes and stretches space itself—the actual shape of the space-and-time mesh. You need a way to measure how much that squeezing and stretching happens. A normal ruler won't work because the ruler is made of space too. When space stretches, the ruler stretches along with it. You end up trying to measure change using a tool that changes in exactly the same way.
