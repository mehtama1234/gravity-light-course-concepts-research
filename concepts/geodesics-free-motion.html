Geodesics As Free Motion

Imagine a stone floating in space, far from all gravity and obstacles. It travels in a straight line. But what does "straight" mean when space itself is curved? In flat space the answer is obvious, but near a black hole or inside the warped spacetime around a star, there is no global flat background to measure against. The course needs a way to say "this particle is moving freely" using only the shape of spacetime itself.

Naive Picture

A first guess: a free object should travel along the path that looks straightest when you draw the spacetime as a surface or rubber sheet. Draw the curved surface, mark the shortest curve between two points, and that's the trajectory.

Why It Fails

That picture smuggles in an invisible flat space outside. When you draw a curved rubber sheet on paper, you are already using the flat paper underneath. The spacetime itself has no outside to sit in. A definition of "free motion" must live entirely within spacetime and depend only on the geometry accessible from inside.

Mathematical Object

A geodesic is a curve (a path traced out in spacetime) with a special property: at every point along the curve, the tangent vector to the curve does not change direction according to the local measuring rule called the connection. The connection is the instruction for how to compare directions at nearby points without leaving the surface. A geodesic is a curve where that comparison says "the tangent stays the same."

Operation

To check whether a curve is a geodesic: take the tangent vector at each point on the curve, transport it to the next point using the connection rule, and ask if it changed. If it did not change (its covariant derivative is zero), the curve is a geodesic. This operation turns an intuitive idea—"the direction doesn't bend"—into a checkable mathematical condition.

Why It Matters

In general relativity, a free particle follows a geodesic of spacetime, and light follows a geodesic of a degenerate kind (one that moves at light speed). The metric (the measurement rule that defines angles and distances) determines which geodesics are possible for particles and which for light. This is how gravity works: the curved spacetime tells particles and light rays which paths to take.

What Breaks Without It

Remove the geodesic concept and you have no way to state what "free motion" or "unforced motion" means in curved spacetime. Calculations might still produce numbers, but those numbers would depend on your choice of coordinates, your arbitrary definitions, and would not represent a real feature of the physics. The entire bridge from curved geometry to real particle paths collapses.

Mini Example

Imagine an airplane flying from New York to London. On a flat map, the shortest-looking path curves upward (toward the north pole). That curved line on the map is actually the straightest path on the real curved Earth—a great circle. An airplane following this path is a geodesic traveler: at no point does it try to turn left or right. A pilot aiming to fly straight does not consult a flat map; instead, the pilot keeps the plane level and let the curvature of Earth naturally curve the path. The plane is following a geodesic: its tangent (the direction it is headed) does not bend relative to the surface itself.

Common Confusion

"Geodesics are always the shortest paths." Not always. A geodesic is locally straight according to the connection, meaning it has zero curvature in the sense defined by parallel transport. Globally, a geodesic might be the longest path (like the back side of a great circle on a sphere), or there might be multiple geodesics between two points. What matters is that the geodesic is the path a free, unforced object actually takes—and that is determined by the connection, not by minimizing distance.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Start with the real problem: in spacetime curved by gravity, what does it mean for a particle to move freely? It cannot mean "move in a straight line" because spacetime has no global straight. It cannot mean "follow a path that looks straight on a drawing" because that requires imagining a flat space outside, which spacetime does not have. So we must define freedom using only the geometry inside spacetime. The key tool is the connection—a local rule for comparing directions at nearby points. A freely moving particle is one whose direction (its tangent vector) does not change as we move it from point to point along the particle's own path, according to that local rule. This gives a precise, intrinsic definition: a geodesic is a curve such that its tangent vector, when transported along itself via the connection, does not change. No reference to an outside, no smuggled-in flatness. The operation is concrete: at each point on the curve, compute whether the connection rule says the tangent has changed. If it has not changed at any point, the particle is in free motion.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Let the curve be parameterized by a parameter λ (often time or arc length). At each point on the curve, the tangent vector is d/dλ (the direction in which the curve moves). The covariant derivative ∇_d/dλ (d/dλ) measures how the tangent vector changes when transported along the curve using the connection ∇. For a geodesic, this covariant derivative is zero: ∇_d/dλ (d/dλ) = 0. In coordinates, this becomes d²x^μ/dλ² + Γ^μ_ρσ (dx^ρ/dλ)(dx^σ/dλ) = 0, where Γ^μ_ρσ are the connection coefficients (numbers that encode the local comparison rule). The equation says: the second derivative of position (acceleration) plus a correction term built from the connection coefficients (which describe how the coordinates themselves twist) sum to zero. This is the geodesic equation.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Every major topic in the course—curvature, parallel transport, the Einstein equation, matter distribution, black holes, cosmological solutions—relies on geodesics as the language for "free motion." Without it, these topics become calculations with no physical interpretation. Students would manipulate symbols without understanding what those symbols represent in the real universe. The geodesic concept transforms geometry from an abstract symbol game into a description of how things actually move.

Where It Sits In The Course

Geodesics connect the language of vector fields and parallel transport (how you move vectors) to the geometric ideas that follow: curvature, which describes how geodesics in different directions tilt relative to each other. Geodesics are the "straight" paths against which we measure all deviation. When you study curvature on the next page, you will see it defined as the failure of two nearby geodesics to stay parallel under transport.

Connective Thread

This concept depends on mastering vector fields, connections, parallel transport, and the covariant derivative from earlier pages. The geodesic idea reuses all those tools in a single coherent way: it defines a curve by saying its tangent vector does not change under covariant derivative. Everything that follows—curvature, the Riemann tensor, Einstein's equation, the motion of matter—hangs on this definition of free motion.
