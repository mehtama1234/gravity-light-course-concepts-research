Hubble Rate As Change Of Scale

You measure the distance to a galaxy far away and notice that galaxies are pulling apart from each other—getting farther away over time. But knowing the distance grows is only half the picture. You need to know *how fast* this is happening right now, at this moment. Is space doubling in size every billion years, or every trillion years? That difference changes everything about what happens to the universe in the future.

Naive Picture

The obvious first thought: galaxies are speeding away through empty space, like cars driving apart on a highway. The farther away a galaxy sits, the faster it zooms backward. Meanwhile, space itself stays flat and quiet—only the galaxies are moving.

Why It Fails

This picture misses the real story. Galaxies are not moving through space—space itself is stretching, like a rubber sheet being pulled from all sides, dragging the galaxies along with it. When astronomers measure redshifts (how much light stretches as it travels), they are not measuring one galaxy's speed through a fixed background. They are measuring how the *scale of the whole universe* is changing. It is the difference between asking 'how fast is this car going?' and asking 'how fast is the container growing?'

Mathematical Object

The Hubble rate is a single number that tells you the fractional expansion speed. Imagine the universe has a size that we can label with a scale factor—call it $a(t)$—which says how much bigger the universe is now than at some earlier time. If the scale factor goes from $a_0$ to $a_1$ over time $\Delta t$, the Hubble rate answers: per unit time, what *fraction* of the current size is being added? Written as a formula: $H = (1/a) \, da/dt$. This is the fractional rate of change.

Operation

Here is how astronomers find the Hubble rate: observe a galaxy and measure how much its light is redshifted—stretched to longer wavelengths. Use the Doppler formula to convert that into a recession speed. Measure the galaxy's distance using brightness or other methods. Then divide the speed by the distance. The result is a number per unit time—say, 0.1 per billion years. That is the Hubble rate: how much the scale factor grows as a fraction of itself per unit time, right now.

Why It Matters

In Einstein's theory, gravity is not a force pushing objects around—it is the shape of spacetime itself. The Hubble rate is the number that tells you whether and how fast that shape is stretching on cosmic scales. It is the observable consequence of Einstein's equation acting on a universe filled with matter and energy. Measure the Hubble rate, and you can write down the metric—the rule that describes distances and times—and thus see exactly what gravity is doing.

What Breaks Without It

Remove the Hubble rate and you cannot connect what you observe—redshifts and distances—to what Einstein's equation predicts about spacetime shape. You cannot predict whether expansion will accelerate or slow. You cannot test the theory on the largest scales. The whole chain from measurement to prediction snaps.

Mini Example

Suppose at time $t=0$, the scale factor $a = 1$ and the Hubble rate is $H = 0.1$ per billion years. After 1 billion years, the scale factor is approximately $a \approx 1.1$. The fractional growth is $(1.1 - 1)/1 = 10\%$, which matches $H \times \Delta t = 0.1 \times 1 = 0.1$. A galaxy 1 billion light-years away is now roughly 1.1 billion light-years away. But notice: the Hubble rate stays the same ($H \approx 0.1$) because we measure *fractional* change, not absolute change. If the universe were twice as large (say $a=2$), the same $H=0.1$ would mean the scale factor grows by $0.2$, a 10% fractional increase—still expansion at the same rate. The number $H$ captures the growth relative to the universe's current size.

Common Confusion

Many people confuse the Hubble rate with the speed at which a galaxy is receding. They are different things. The Hubble rate is a ratio—recession speed divided by distance—reported as a fraction per unit time. Two galaxies at different distances recede at different speeds, but both speeds reflect the same Hubble rate at that moment. The Hubble rate is not a speed; it describes how the metric itself evolves with time.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Here is the core idea: when you watch galaxies move apart, you are not seeing objects flying through empty space—you are watching space itself expand. The metric—the rule for measuring distance and time—is literally changing. There is no static background to measure velocity through. What changes is the scale of the universe, encoded in a single function $a(t)$ that tells you how much bigger distances have become. The Hubble rate is how fast this scale grows *relative to how big it already is*—the fractional growth rate. If the scale increases by a billion light-years, that means something completely different in a 10-billion-light-year universe versus a 1000-billion-light-year universe. The Hubble rate corrects for this by reporting growth as a percentage, not an absolute number. Observationally: measure recession speeds, measure distances, divide them. That ratio is the Hubble rate—the fractional expansion happening right now. This single number, changing only with cosmic time, is the thread connecting observations to the spacetime geometry that Einstein's theory describes.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The Hubble rate is defined as $H(t) = (1/a(t)) \, (da/dt)$. Here is how to read it: $da/dt$ is the absolute rate of change of the scale factor, measured in length units per time. Divide by $a(t)$ and you get a dimensionless fraction per unit time. Example: if $a = 10$ billion light-years and $da/dt = 1$ billion light-years per billion years, then $H = (1/10) \times 1 = 0.1$ per billion years. The scale factor is growing at 10% per billion years. The Hubble rate is the same at all locations in the universe at a given moment (because the universe is uniform and looks the same everywhere), but it does change with time as the universe evolves. In a universe dominated by matter, $H$ shrinks slowly over time; in a universe dominated by dark energy, $H$ stays nearly constant. These different behaviors come straight from Einstein's equation and the stress-energy of matter and radiation, making the Hubble rate the direct observable window into gravitational dynamics.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

The Hubble rate is the anchor that ties observations back to real physics. Without it, you cannot tell whether changes you measure are genuine changes in spacetime or just artifacts of choosing different coordinates—a pure choice of mathematical notation with no physical meaning. Einstein's theory says real physics must be the same for all observers. Lose the Hubble rate and you lose that anchor; expansion becomes indistinguishable from an illusion created by picking the wrong measurement rule.

Where It Sits In The Course

The Hubble rate appears throughout cosmology: in the models that solve Einstein's equation for universes full of matter and energy, in the definition of a cosmic horizon beyond which causal signals cannot propagate, in the redshift-distance relationships that astronomers use to map the universe. It is the one parameter that encodes how spacetime geometry evolves in a universe that looks the same everywhere and in all directions.

Connective Thread

This concept builds on the scale factor—how the universe's size is encoded as a function of time—which earlier pages introduce. It then becomes the foundation for cosmic evolution: given the Hubble rate and what the universe is made of, you can solve for the universe's entire past and future. Later ideas like cosmic horizons and Penrose diagrams rest on the Hubble rate to describe the boundaries of causality and the large-scale shape of spacetime.
