Tangent Vectors As Derivatives

You stand on a hillside and want to measure how temperature changes as you step in one direction versus another. But what exactly is 'a direction at a point'? Spacetime isn't sitting inside a flat container where you can draw arrows from the outside. You need a way to define direction that lives *within* the space itself.

Naive Picture

The intuitive first image: a direction is just a tiny arrow, like an arrowhead touching the surface. You learned this in high school—draw the arrow, measure the slope. Simple and comforting.

Why It Fails

That works when the space sits inside a flat plane or flat 3D room. But spacetime might curve everywhere, and there is no larger flat room to hold it. An arrow drawn 'outside' wouldn't tell you anything about the space itself. You'd be importing a structure that isn't there.

Mathematical Object

A tangent vector is a rule: a machine that eats any smooth function on the manifold and spits out a number—the rate at which that function changes along a specific direction at that point. It's intrinsic to the space, not an external arrow. The machine encodes both the point and the direction purely through how it interrogates functions.

Operation

To use a tangent vector, you hand it a smooth function (say, temperature T on the manifold). The tangent vector tells you dT along its direction. You can test the tangent vector by checking how it acts on many different functions—always at the same point, always respecting the linearity rule: if you feed it a sum, you get the sum of the answers.

Why It Matters

Particle velocity is a tangent vector—it says how your position changes with time. Light rays travel along tangent vectors called null directions. Field strengths vary along tangent vectors. Unless you have an intrinsic definition of direction that lives inside spacetime, you cannot write down what it means for light or matter to move without sneaking in a flat background that isn't actually there.

What Breaks Without It

If tangent vectors are just external arrows, you are forced to assume spacetime sits in a flat external space. Then you cannot talk about intrinsic geometry—curvature becomes meaningless, and Einstein's equation has nowhere to live. You lose the whole point: gravity as the shape of spacetime, not a force in a pre-existing arena.

Mini Example

Imagine a smooth temperature field T on the surface of a sphere. Pick a point (say, the North Pole) and a direction (say, toward the equator along a meridian). The tangent vector in that direction is a rule that takes T and returns the rate at which T climbs as you move that way. Call it v(T). Now pick another function—air pressure P. The same tangent vector (same point, same direction) returns v(P), a different number. Both outputs come from the same underlying direction, tested against different functions. This is the tangent vector: the point-and-direction, encoded as a derivative operator.

Common Confusion

People often think a tangent vector is a vector in the usual high-school sense: 'an arrow with magnitude and direction.' It is not. The magnitude and direction emerge only when you choose a metric—a way to measure lengths. The tangent vector *itself* is purely the derivative operator: what it does to functions. Two tangent vectors are the same if they give the same derivative value for every function, regardless of any choice of coordinates or measurement rule.

First-Principles Walkthrough

You stand on a hillside and notice temperature changes with direction—hotter walking downslope than sideways. Intuitively, a direction is an arrow. But where does that arrow live? If your space is a curved surface with no room around it, there is nowhere external to draw from. So the course takes a different path: a direction at a point is not an arrow, but a rule that tells you how any function changes along that direction. You test the direction by feeding it a temperature function and asking, 'How fast does T change here along this way?' The direction—the tangent vector—is identified entirely by what it does to functions. Hand it height, it gives a slope. Hand it pressure, it gives another number. Same direction, different function, different output. But always in the same way: the operation respects addition and scaling, just like derivatives do. This is an *intrinsic* definition of direction: it lives inside the space itself, needs no external reference, and captures exactly what direction means for a curved manifold. The full machinery of calculus can now operate inside spacetime without importing a flat background.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A tangent vector v at a point p acts on smooth functions like a derivative. If f is any smooth function on the manifold, then v(f) is a real number, calculated as follows: v respects linearity—v(af + bg) = a·v(f) + b·v(g) for constants a, b and functions f, g—and it obeys the product rule—v(fg) = v(f)·g(p) + f(p)·v(g). These rules are *exactly* the axioms for a derivation at a point. In coordinates, if the function f has components f(x¹, x², ...) and the tangent vector has components v = v^i ∂/∂x^i, then v(f) = v^i ∂f/∂x^i, the directional derivative. But the key insight: v itself is coordinate-independent; the rule v acts the same way no matter which coordinate system you use to describe the manifold.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Differential geometry is built on the ability to talk about change at a point without assuming a flat external space. If you define tangent vectors any other way—as arrows from outside, or as components without the rule—then curvature, parallel transport, and the whole structure of general relativity collapse into coordinate tricks. This definition is the foundation: it makes tangent vectors *local* operators that belong to the manifold, not imported Euclidean objects.

Connective Thread

Earlier pages introduced smooth manifolds and functions on them. This page shows how to define direction *within* those manifolds using only functions and derivatives. Every later concept—velocity, parallel transport, curvature, the metric, the Einstein equation—depends on having this intrinsic notion of tangent vector. Without it, you cannot distinguish real physical effects from coordinate accidents. With it, the entire theory of gravity becomes a coherent story about geometry.

Tutorial 5: Tangent Spaces

Pressure test: Use a tangent vector by letting it act on functions. The tutorial pressure is to stop relying on a drawn arrow outside the manifold and instead ask what directional rate the vector assigns to each local function.

Why it changes this concept: This makes tangent space internal. Motion, light direction, and observer velocity can now be explained without borrowing a surrounding flat space, because the direction is known by what it does at the point. That also prepares the later field picture: a field is built from these local actions point by point.
