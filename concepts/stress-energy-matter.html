Matter and Stress-Energy

Gravity bends space and time. But what causes the bending? Newton said: mass. Einstein's answer is richer. Mass does bend spacetime—but so does energy, momentum (motion), pressure (crowding), and internal stress. Relativity treats all of these as one unified object, because energy and momentum are really the same thing seen from different angles. Gravity must respond to all of them together.

Naive Picture

A ball sitting still has mass. Heat it up—molecules move faster inside, pressure builds—but the ball's weight barely changes. Naive picture: pressure is just how crowded things are inside. It should not change how much gravity the ball makes. Mass alone is all that matters for gravity.

Why It Fails

Relativity teaches that energy is mass (E=mc²), so heat energy is gravitational mass. Pressure, too, is real motion of particles, carrying momentum. Momentum itself carries energy. A single number (mass) cannot capture this web. You need a ledger—one organized object—that tracks energy, momentum, and pressure together, because rotating your frame of reference mixes them all up. One observer sees mostly mass; another observer moving fast sees energy and momentum too. The ledger must hold steady across all frames.

Mathematical Object

A stress-energy tensor is a 4×4 bookkeeping table (like a spreadsheet with 4 rows and 4 columns). Each cell holds one number that describes a 'flow': a kind of stuff moving in a direction. Top-left corner: energy density (how much energy per unit volume, measured by someone at rest). Top-right block (three entries): momentum flowing—the push and tug through space. Bottom-right 3×3 block: pressure and internal stress—how much matter inside one region pushes on neighboring regions. The row tells you 'what stuff are we counting' (energy or momentum component). The column tells you 'in which direction is it flowing.' Multiply the numbers by a direction vector (an arrow showing which way you are looking) and you get a measurable quantity in that direction.

Operation

Take the tensor (the 4×4 table). Point an arrow in some direction—say, the direction an observer is moving, or the outward normal to a surface. Multiply each row by components of that arrow and sum. The result: the energy density that observer measures, or the momentum flux passing through that surface, or stress between regions. Different arrows give different answers, but all observers agree on the physical truth underneath.

Why It Matters

Einstein's equation is a rule: spacetime curvature = geometry constant × stress-energy tensor. Left side talks about bending. Right side talks about matter and energy. Without the stress-energy tensor, there is no language to write what the right side is. Light carries energy; it should curve spacetime. Electromagnetic fields have momentum and pressure; they should curve spacetime. Internal pressure in a star should curve spacetime. The tensor is the only object that holds all these pieces together in a frame-independent way—the only way to tell the geometry what to do.

What Breaks Without It

Suppose you try to write Einstein's equation using only mass (a single number) instead of the stress-energy tensor. The equation becomes incomplete. Light carries energy but no rest-mass; the equation would say light does not curve spacetime. A pressurized gas has no more rest-mass than the same gas at low pressure; the equation would say pressure has no effect. The universe in its first moments was radiation-dominated (mostly light and heat, not atoms); the equation would mispredict how fast space itself expands. Physics and geometry fall apart.

Mini Example

A sphere of hot gas at rest. It has rest-mass energy (the top-left entry of the tensor). Inside, molecules collide, creating pressure—internal stress pushing outward (the diagonal entries in the bottom-right 3×3 block). In a weakly relativistic system (speeds small compared to light speed), this pressure contributes roughly (v²/c²) times the mass effect to spacetime curvature—tiny for ordinary air but noticeable in neutron stars, where particles crowd so hard that pressure rivals the mass-energy. In the early universe, radiation pressure dominated, and it drove expansion more than rest-mass did.

Common Confusion

Students often treat stress-energy as a collection of separate pieces: mass here, momentum there, pressure elsewhere. They memorize that 'pressure contributes' but do not feel why. The point is that stress-energy is one object. Rotate your reference frame and all entries mix—energy becomes momentum, momentum becomes energy. Pressure is not a small correction; it is woven into the tensor's fabric. For a gas at high density or high temperature, pressure genuinely bends spacetime as much as mass does. A neutron star's pressure rivals its mass-energy in gravitational effect. The early universe was dominated by radiation pressure, not mass.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine coffee in a cup. You feel its weight—that is mass pulling down. The coffee is also hot; molecules inside jiggle and collide, pushing outward with pressure. Newton's gravity depends on mass alone. Heat does not change mass, so he said pressure does not matter for gravity. Einstein disagreed. He said energy is mass (E=mc²), so heat energy is gravitational too. More than that: momentum (the push of moving particles) and pressure (the push from collisions) are just energy and momentum in different guises. The problem is listing them separately—'mass here, heat there, momentum elsewhere'—misses the whole point. These are not separate things; they are one unified ledger, a 4×4 table called the stress-energy tensor. Each entry records a kind of 'stuff' flowing in a direction. Energy flowing forward in time. Momentum flowing sideways through space. Pressure and stress pushing between directions. When you ask 'what energy does this observer measure,' you multiply the table entries by the observer's velocity vector and read the answer. Different observers multiply by different velocity vectors, so they get different numbers—but the physical truth is invariant, encoded in how the tensor transforms. Einstein's equation then simply says: spacetime curvature is proportional to this tensor. Geometry bends in response to every entry—energy, momentum, pressure, stress. All of it.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The stress-energy tensor is denoted T_μν where μ and ν are indices running from 0 (time) to 3 (three spatial directions). T_00 is energy density. T_0i (top-right block) is momentum density component i; T_i0 (left-side middle block) is momentum flux in direction i. T_ij (bottom-right 3×3 block) is stress or pressure between spatial directions i and j. For a perfect fluid at rest in a given frame: T_00 = ρ (density times c²), T_ii = pressure p (diagonal), T_ij = 0 (off-diagonal). The conservation law is ∇_μ T^μν = 0, the covariant divergence of T equals zero. In words: energy density changes locally at the same rate as energy flux diverges (spreads out); momentum density changes at the same rate as stress diverges. This equation holds in all frames—it is truly coordinate-free and frame-independent, the language relativity requires.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Stress-energy is the only vocabulary Einstein's equation has to describe how matter and energy curve spacetime. Without it, the left side of Einstein's equation (curvature, describing the bent geometry) has no right-side partner. Curvature becomes a free-floating abstraction—you can calculate it, but nothing anchors it to reality. Mass, pressure, radiation, momentum—all the physics—disconnect from geometry. You get pure mathematics with no link to why spacetime actually bends. Relativity stops being a theory of how gravity works and becomes a hollow geometric game. The whole structure collapses: curvature and stress-energy must speak the same language (both tensors), obey the same transformation rules (both frame-independent), and relate through one equation (Einstein's), or the theory tells you nothing about how the real universe behaves.

Where It Sits In The Course

Stress-energy belongs to a family of tensors. Its partner is the Einstein tensor (describing curvature). Both are rank-2 tensors—both are 4×4 tables that transform the same way under coordinate changes. Einstein's equation equates them (up to a constant and the metric). A sibling is the Ricci tensor, which measures local bending and feeds into the Einstein tensor. All obey the same transformation law, so they describe frame-independent truth. The conservation law ∇·T = 0 (stress-energy is conserved) mirrors the property ∇·G = 0 (the Einstein tensor is divergence-free). This matching creates a self-consistent system: if energy is conserved and geometry obeys Einstein's equation, everything locks together.

Connective Thread

Stress-energy follows coordinate-free calculus and Stokes' theorem—tools that track how quantities flow and spread across surfaces and volumes. These tools let you write conservation laws (like ∇·T = 0) in a frame-independent way. Stress-energy then feeds directly into Einstein's equation and the cosmological constant (which is vacuum stress-energy—the energy of empty space itself), the point where geometry and matter meet. You cannot understand Einstein's equation without understanding what stress-energy is, why it must be conserved (by Noether's theorem), and how it couples to curvature. Each step builds on the last.
