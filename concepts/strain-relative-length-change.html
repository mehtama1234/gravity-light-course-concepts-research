Strain As Relative Length Change

Two long metal tubes sit in space, standing perpendicular to each other, with mirrors at their ends. A gravitational wave ripples through — spacetime itself flexes. The mirrors move closer together in one direction and farther apart in the other. You want to measure this motion, but the distances shift by amounts smaller than an atom. How do you notice something so impossibly small?

Naive Picture

You might think: just measure each tube's length before the wave arrives, then measure it again after. Write down the difference. If one was 10 meters and is now 10.000001 meters, the change is 0.000001 meters — simple.

Why It Fails

Here's the trap: absolute length means nothing when spacetime itself is warping. If the wave stretches space, then both the tube AND the ruler you're using to measure it stretch together. Your measurement might show no change at all, even though space actually flexed. You can only tell something changed by comparing the tube to something nearby — another tube, another marker, anything else in the local neighborhood.

Mathematical Object

Strain is a pure ratio — no units, just a number. It's the change in distance between two nearby points, divided by their starting distance. Example: two mirrors separated by 1000 meters. A wave shifts them to 1001 meters apart. The change is 1 meter. Divide by the original 1000 meters and get 0.001. That's the strain. It tells you the fractional change — how much the separation grew or shrank as a percentage of what it was.

Operation

Pick two reference points — say, the mirrors at each end of a tube. Measure how far apart they are right now. Call this L_old. Wait a moment. Measure the distance again, call it L_new. Calculate (L_new minus L_old) divided by L_old. That result is strain. It's the fractional shift in the separation.

Why It Matters

Light travels back and forth between the mirrors. The path length depends on how far apart the mirrors sit. When a gravitational wave changes the distance, the light's round-trip journey gets slightly longer or shorter. This changes how many light waves fit in that path — it changes the interference pattern (the way light from two paths adds or cancels). Strain is the connection: warped spacetime changes distances, distances change the light paths, and light reveals what strain is doing.

What Breaks Without It

Without strain, you can only note absolute distances — 10 meters becomes 10.001 meters. But in a curved spacetime where the wave is warping everything, 'absolute distance' has no real meaning. You're measuring with a ruler that's also being warped. You can't tell if the detector moved, if it bent, or if the wave actually passed through. Strain is the only tool that cuts through this confusion by comparing nearby points to each other.

Mini Example

Two mirrors start 1 km apart. A wave squeezes space — they move to 1 km minus 1 meter. The raw change is −1 meter. The strain is −1 divided by 1000, or −0.001. Now take another pair of mirrors, starting 4 km apart. The same wave compresses them by 4 meters. Strain is −4 divided by 4000, also −0.001. Two different absolute movements, but the same strain. This sameness is crucial: it tells you both mirror pairs felt the same squeezing, which is exactly the detector's geometry.

Common Confusion

Many people confuse strain with the stretching force you feel when you pull taffy. Strain is not a force. It's not a stress (the internal tension that resists being pulled). Strain is simply a number: the fractional change in distance. A material can have strain even if no stress is pulling on it — you're just describing the geometry, the shape, not whether something is being yanked or compressed.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine two perpendicular metal tubes with mirrors at the ends. Light bounces between the mirrors. A gravitational wave passes through and does something strange: it jiggles the shape of spacetime. The tubes themselves don't actually stretch — the space they sit in does. So when you measure one tube and write '10.1 meters instead of 10 meters,' you haven't said anything real. Compared to what standard? Your measuring stick might have grown too. Here's the insight: pay attention not to one tube's length but to how the two tubes compare. When the wave hits, one direction gets compressed while the perpendicular direction expands — a squeezing effect. The real question isn't 'how much did tube A grow' but 'how much did tube A change relative to tube B?' Picture this: tube A shrinks by 1 millimeter, tube B shrinks by 0.5 millimeters. The difference matters more than either absolute number. Strain captures that. Calculate the fractional change for each arm: change divided by original length. Tube A changed by 1 mm out of 1000 mm = 0.001. Tube B changed by 0.5 mm out of 500 mm = also 0.001. The same fractional change, even though the absolute changes differed. Now the light detector works: when light bounces between mirrors separated by different strains, the interference pattern shifts. The pattern tells you the wave's true signature, independent of where you started your measurements.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Strain for a single arm equals (new length minus old length) divided by old length. Write it as: strain = (L_new − L_old) / L_old. The result has no units — it's a pure number, a ratio. This ratio is independent of which units you choose. Measure in meters or centimeters, the strain stays the same. It's also independent of your reference frame — a slowly moving observer and a fast-moving observer will agree on the strain in their local neighborhood because strain compares two points close to each other, not distant ones. For a detector with two perpendicular arms, you compute strain separately for each arm. Often you then find the difference between them — tube A's strain minus tube B's strain, called differential strain. This difference is what the light's interference pattern actually encodes. The interferometer reads the arms' relative strain, not either absolute strain alone.

Where This Idea Itself Breaks Down

Strain h = ΔL/L is a small-angle approximation: it assumes h ≪ 1, so that the fractional change in length is tiny. When h ~ 1 or larger (a 100% compression or expansion), the formula becomes non-linear and the superposition principle breaks down; you cannot simply add up strains from multiple waves because (1 + h₁)(1 + h₂) ≠ 1 + (h₁ + h₂) when the products h₁·h₂ matter. Physically, a detector cannot measure strains larger than the initial arm length without the arms crossing or becoming physically damaged.

Strain also assumes the mirrors themselves are point masses or rigid bodies whose intrinsic size does not deform with the wave. For very strong gravitational waves (those generated by black-hole mergers in the final plunge), the spacetime curvature is so large that the mirrors would be torn apart by tidal forces—the assumption of rigid, non-deforming test masses fails, and the strain measurement becomes meaningless because the 'measuring instrument' itself is being destroyed.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

General relativity tells us gravity isn't a force tugging objects downward — it's the bending of spacetime itself. Once the geometry of space and time bends, measuring a distance stops being like pulling a tape measure. You need to know: distance relative to what? Strain answers that by comparing nearby points. It isolates the geometric change from the noise and the choices about where to anchor your measurements. Without strain, you're trying to detect a wave by reading an absolute ruler — like standing on the ocean and trying to see a wave by measuring the height of one flagpole. The pole itself bobs up and down with the wave. Strain is what makes the wave visible.

Where It Sits In The Course

Strain fits into a web of ideas: perturbation theory (treating spacetime as a flat background plus small wiggles), gravitational waves (those wiggles propagating through space), and laser interferometers (light bouncing in perpendicular arms, whose separation changes with the wave).

Connective Thread

Earlier in the course, you learned what gravitational waves are — wiggles in spacetime itself. You learned that a source must have a changing quadrupole moment (an imbalance in how its mass is arranged, that jiggles back and forth). This page bridges that to what a detector actually reads. 'The quadrupole moment is changing' becomes 'the strain history in the detector arms.' Later lessons use that strain history: the laser's interference pattern encodes the strain, revealing the wave's strength and which direction it's squeezing.
