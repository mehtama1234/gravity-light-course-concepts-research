Redshift As Stretched Light

When you look at starlight through a telescope, you measure the distance between repeating peaks in the waves—the wavelength. For distant stars in an expanding universe, something strange happens: the peaks arrive farther apart than they left the star. The waves are stretched. They shift toward the red end of the light spectrum, where the wavelengths are longer and the jiggles slower. The question is: why? Did the star move away from you (like a siren's wail stretching as an ambulance recedes)? Did the space between you and the star get bigger (stretching the peaks apart just from the distance growing)? Or is the star buried in strong gravity where time itself runs slowly? All three look identical when you only look at the light.

Naive Picture

The simplest guess is motion. When a siren moves away, sound waves bunch up behind it and spread out in front. The peaks get farther apart—the wave stretches. Call this the Doppler effect. Redshifted light looks like the same thing: the star is running away, so the light waves stretch. If you write down the math for moving sources and moving receivers, it works. Wavelength gets longer, frequency drops, you see red instead of blue. Mystery solved—the star is receding.

Why It Fails

This picture works in a small region where space is flat, but falls apart when you step back and look at the whole universe. Billions of galaxies are not actually zooming away at impossible speeds. Instead, the metric—the measuring stick itself that defines distance—is expanding. Every distance is growing. Imagine a rubber sheet with dots marked on it. As the sheet stretches, the dots move apart even though they are not crawling across the rubber. The peaks of the light wave are not moving; the space between them is growing. The wave stretches because the ruler is getting bigger. Meanwhile, near a black hole, an entirely different effect takes over. A clock sitting close to the horizon ticks much slower than one far away—time itself flows at different rates. Light born from the slow clock looks redshifted when it reaches the distant observer, even though nothing moved at all. Only the flow of time changed.

Mathematical Object

Redshift is a single pure number you extract from light by comparing it at two moments: when it left its source and when it hit your detector. You take the wavelength (or the frequency—the wiggle speed—of the light) at each moment and form a ratio. More formally, light travels as a wave in spacetime, carrying a property called the wave vector (a direction and frequency all bundled together). If you call this k at the source and k at the observer, the redshift is 1+z = k_observer / k_emitter, where each k is measured using the local time of that location. Redshift is not a thing that sits in one place; it is a relationship stretching across spacetime from source to detector.

Operation

To compute redshift, you follow the light path backward from your detector to its source, tracking it through the spacetime geometry. At the moment and location of emission, you write down the wave vector—the light's direction and frequency—using the emitter's local concept of time. At the moment and location of detection, you do the same using your local concept of time. Then you divide: the frequency you detected divided by the frequency that left the source. That ratio (1+z) is pure numbers; it encodes everything the spacetime geometry did to the light between those two events. You are not changing anything, not moving the light, not rewriting spacetime—you are just reading what the metric says the frequencies were at each end.

Why It Matters

This concept is the bridge from abstract geometry to real telescope data. The metric is the rule that tells you how time flows and distance is measured at each point in spacetime. Where time runs fast, clocks tick rapidly. Where time runs slow, clocks crawl. Light carries a frequency (how fast it jiggles). If the emitter's time runs fast and the observer's time runs slow, the light looks redshifted—it left with a high-frequency jiggle stamped into it, but arrives at a location where time is sluggish, so the same jiggle looks slower. By computing redshift using the metric and the light path, you decode what the metric was actually doing from what your telescope measures. This is how you test whether space is really expanding in cosmology. This is how you confirm that gravity slows time near massive objects. Redshift is the meter that lets you see the invisible metric.

What Breaks Without It

If you do not define redshift carefully using the metric and light path, you lose the ability to read data. You cannot tell whether distant galaxies are moving away from you through a static space, or whether space itself is expanding and carrying them along. You cannot distinguish redshift caused by moving sources from redshift caused by time running at different rates in different places. Cosmological observations become uninterpretable: a distant galaxy might look like it is racing away at light speed when actually the metric is just stretching slowly and steadily. The whole project of testing general relativity against telescope observations collapses, because you have no precise rule connecting what the theory predicts about geometry to what you actually measure with an instrument. Theory and experiment disconnect.

Mini Example

Imagine a toy universe: a uniform grid of space that stretches uniformly, like rubber being pulled equally in all directions. A light source at position x=0 emits light with wavelength λ₀=1 centimeter at time t=0. The light bounces outward. By time t=10 seconds, the grid has doubled—every distance is twice as large as before. The light has traveled a distance that was 10 centimeters in the old grid, but now measures 20 centimeters in the new grid because the grid itself expanded. When the observer detects the light, the wavelength has also doubled to λ=2 centimeters. The observer computes redshift as z=(λ-λ₀)/λ₀=(2-1)/1=1. Nothing moved. No source receded. The space expanded, pulling the wave peaks apart like dots on a stretching balloon.

Common Confusion

A common misconception is that redshift has a single cause—either motion, or expansion, or gravity—and you identify which one is happening by the amount of redshift. This is wrong. In any real situation, all three causes are usually present simultaneously, and the redshift measures their combined effect as one number. A galaxy that is moving away from you due to cosmic expansion, sitting in its own local gravitational well (so its clock ticks a bit slower than yours), and also moving with some peculiar velocity (its own extra motion relative to the expansion flow) will show redshift from all three effects mixed together. The single number z tells you the total; you cannot separate the parts without additional measurements (such as the galaxy's distance from other methods). Redshift is not a cause—motion is, or expansion is, or gravity is. Redshift is a meter. It reads what the metric actually does to light. Mistaking it for a cause leads to misinterpreting data.

First-Principles Walkthrough

You aim a telescope at a galaxy so distant that the light reaching you today began its journey billions of years ago. You split the light into its spectrum—the collection of all the colors present in it—and measure the wavelengths. You notice something unexpected: the wavelengths are longer than you expected based on what you know about that type of galaxy. The light is shifted toward red, which in the spectrum means longer wavelengths and slower jiggles. Your first instinct is to invoke motion: a siren wails higher as it approaches and lower as it recedes, and light should work the same way. But this instinct assumes a hidden choice—that space is fixed and unchanging. In reality, the metric (the measuring stick that defines distance and time in the universe) is not fixed; it is expanding. When the metric expands, every distance grows. The distance between you and the galaxy increases, yes, but also the distance between successive peaks of the light wave increases. The wave stretches not because the source moved, but because the ruler stretching it is getting bigger. Imagine a tape measure being pulled steadily; the numbers on it get farther and farther apart even though the tape itself is not moving through space. To measure this precisely and consistently, you need a definition that works in any geometry, not just in textbook examples. The metric is your tool. At the moment the distant galaxy's light was born, the galaxy's local clock ticked at a certain rate, encoding the galaxy's experience of time. At the moment your detector catches the light, your local clock ticks at its own rate. The light wave was stamped with the frequency matching the galaxy's time-flow. It arrives at a location where your clock ticks differently, so the frequency has changed relative to your time. That change is encoded completely in the metric between the galaxy and you. To find the redshift, you compare those two frequencies using the metric and the path the light took. This single number—1+z—tells you everything the geometry did to the light along the way: whether space expanded (if so, how much), whether the source was in a stronger gravitational field than you (which slows its clock), whether the source was moving, or some mixture of all three. Without this precise definition anchored to the metric, you cannot extract geometry from observations, and the whole experimental foundation of general relativity crumbles.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

The wave vector k of light is a mathematical object that packages both the direction light travels and how fast it jiggles. Written out, k = (ω/c, k⃗), where ω is the angular frequency (how fast the wave oscillates, measured in cycles per unit time) and k⃗ is the spatial part (which direction the light travels). The key property is that k is a tangent vector—a direction and magnitude defined at a single point of spacetime. The metric g is the rule that lets you measure lengths of tangent vectors and compare vectors at different points. The redshift formula writes as 1+z = (g(u_obs, k)) / (g(u_emit, k)), where u is the four-velocity (the direction and speed through spacetime) of the observer and emitter. The notation g(u,k) means you use the metric to compute how much the observer's notion of time aligns with the light wave. This formula looks abstract, but it encodes a concrete meaning: you are comparing two ratios. At emission, you measure how much the emitter's local time direction aligns with the light's oscillation. At detection, you measure how much the observer's local time direction aligns with the same light oscillation. You divide the second ratio by the first. If both observer and emitter are at rest (not moving) and sit in identical metric conditions, the ratio is exactly 1 and z=0 (no redshift). If the observer is moving away, the frequencies do not align as well at detection, so z becomes positive (redshift toward red). If the observer sits in a region where time runs slower (stronger gravity), the observer's clock ticks slower, making the light's frequency look slower relative to the observer's time, so again z is positive. The formula does not separate these three causes; it measures their sum.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Redshift is the foundation of experimental relativity. General relativity makes predictions: it says that matter and energy curve spacetime, changing the metric. But you cannot see the metric with any instrument; you only see light. To test whether the theory is right, you must translate predictions about the metric into predictions about redshift (numbers you actually measure), then compare to telescope observations. If redshift is not defined clearly and is not anchored to the metric, the theory becomes an untestable game, a collection of equations with no connection to reality. With a rigorous definition, every observation becomes a test of the theory. Is the universe expanding as predicted? Does gravity really slow time, making light redshift near massive objects? Are the proportions correct? Redshift is the only meter you have to read the metric. Without it, you cannot answer whether general relativity is actually true.

Where It Sits In The Course

Redshift is the junction point of the course so far. Geodesics describe the paths that light and matter trace through the curved spacetime you have been building. The metric defines those paths and also determines how frequencies transform between observers at different locations. The Einstein equation relates the metric to matter and energy, telling you what shape the spacetime takes. Now, redshift combines these pieces: it is the observable that reports what the metric is doing. Observations of redshift in real galaxies and stars become the test of whether the metric predicted by the Einstein equation is the one we actually inhabit. Redshift translates the abstract language of geometry into the practical language of data.

Connective Thread

The concepts so far have laid down the pieces: geodesics describe how light paths bend through curved spacetime; the metric defines those geodesics and also governs how observers moving differently will measure different frequencies; the Einstein equation predicts what metric grows from a distribution of matter. Redshift packages all three into a single observable number. In the concepts ahead—cosmology, gravitational waves, black holes—redshift will keep appearing as the way to extract information about the metric from what you measure. Without a unified definition of redshift, each topic would need its own measurement rule, and you would lose the thread connecting everything back to the metric as the master quantity that governs the universe. Redshift is that thread.
