Gravitational Lensing And Focusing

Look at a distant galaxy through a telescope. Between you and it sits another galaxy so massive that the space and time around it are bent. When light from the far galaxy travels toward you, it passes through this warped region. The bent space steers the light off its straight path. You see the distant galaxy in the wrong spot in the sky. Sometimes it looks magnified—brighter and larger. Sometimes it splits into two or three images of the same galaxy. We need a way to understand this bending without pretending gravity is a force pulling on light.

Naive Picture

A simple guess: gravity acts like an invisible hand pushing sideways on light rays. A stronger push means a sharper bend. Every ray bends a little, depending on how close it passes to the heavy galaxy. Each ray gets its own deflection angle and that is the whole story.

Why It Fails

This picture treats light rays as independent travelers, each obeying the same rule in isolation. But light does not respond to a sideways push. Light paths are shaped by the shape of space and time around them. When space is bent, there is no such thing as a perfectly straight line anymore. What we call a light ray is the straightest possible path available in that bent space—the zero-acceleration path. Two rays nearby follow different straightest paths because they start in different spots in the bent space. Some of these paths pull together (converge), others spread apart (diverge), others corkscrew around each other. You cannot explain why rays converge by looking at each ray alone. You have to look at how they move relative to each other.

Mathematical Object

A bundle of light rays traveling close together and nearly parallel is a single object called a congruence of null geodesics. In everyday language: a bundle (meaning many rays packed tight), of null (meaning light-speed paths), geodesics (meaning the straightest paths available). At any moment in space and time, you take a small patch of rays all pointing nearly the same way. That patch is one congruence. The congruence is the full rule: it tells you at every nearby spot in space-time, which direction a light ray points. Curved space and time act on the entire bundle at once. The bundle can get squeezed, stretched, tilted, or twisted by the curvature.

Operation

Take your bundle of light rays and imagine moving perpendicular to them by a tiny distance. Watch three things: Does the cross-sectional area shrink or grow? Do the rays' directions shift relative to each other? Are the rays spinning as a group? You are not watching single rays. You are watching how the whole bundle transforms. These three changes have names: expansion (area change), shear (directional shifts), and twist (group rotation).

Why It Matters

This bundle measurement is how gravity shows itself to us through light. When light passes a heavy object, curved space and time squeeze or stretch the bundle's cross-section. If more light energy arrives at your telescope, the bundle has been squeezed. If you see the image in a shifted direction, the bundle has been tilted and squeezed together. If you see one galaxy as two images, one bundle has been split by the curvature. By measuring how the bundle changes, you measure how much and in which direction the space and time are bent. We cannot see curvature directly—it is invisible. But we can see how it focuses or spreads light.

What Breaks Without It

If you track only single rays and their bend angles, you have three separate puzzles: Why does a magnified image appear brighter? Why do images shift position? Why do some galaxies split into multiples? Each looks like a different trick. You calculate them separately. But they are connected. A squeezed bundle means more rays arrive at one spot—that is magnification. The same squeeze tilts the rays, creating a direction shift. And if the squeeze is strong enough, it can split the bundle into parts heading toward different places on your detector. Without the bundle language, you lose the reason these three effects hang together. With the bundle language, they are one unified effect: curvature shapes the bundle.

Mini Example

Imagine starlight from a distant star falling toward Earth, but it grazes within 1,000 kilometers of a black hole. Near the black hole, space and time curve so sharply that light rays that started out nearly side-by-side are forced to converge—squeezed by the curvature. Far from the black hole, those same rays were heading toward different corners of your telescope. When they converge, more light arrives at your detector. The star appears brighter—you call this magnification. The rays that now converge scattered from different directions when they were far from the black hole, so you perceive the light coming from a shifted position in the sky. The bundle shows you that the brightness change and the position shift are the same event viewed two ways: curvature has squeezed the bundle.

Common Confusion

Many people imagine that lensing means gravity pulls on light the way a magnet attracts an iron nail. They think light has mass or responds to an invisible attracting field. Wrong. Light travels in straight lines—in the bent space-time's own geometry. Those straight lines happen to converge near a heavy mass because space-time itself is bent there. The convergence is a shape property, not a force effect. A different particle with mass would follow a different straight path (it moves slower and experiences space-time differently) and would bend by a different amount. Light is special: it races along the fastest possible straight paths. The shape of those fastest paths reveals the deepest truth of space-time.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Imagine light as a river of rays, not a single thread. Far from any mass, the rays flow straight like a uniform stream in still water. But when the river passes near a massive object, space and time bend. Bent space is not a flat surface warped in a higher dimension—it is the real landscape that all paths must navigate. Light must follow the straightest available path in whatever space it inhabits. In flat space, straight lines are parallel and never meet. In bent space, the straightest lines curve. Nearby rays in bent space follow nearby straightest lines. Some converge toward each other, some spread apart, some spiral around each other. This is not because light has weight or feels a force pulling it. The stage itself is bent, and straight lines curve on a bent stage. A congruence is the whole river of rays together. Watch how the river's cross-section shrinks or swells. That tells you the strength and shape of the bending that the light experienced. If the river narrows, gravity focused it—more water (more light energy) flows into a smaller channel. If it widens, gravity scattered it. By watching light rivers converge or spread, you read the bend of space and time without directly measuring it. This is why light is nature's messenger: it shows us geometry through focus and imaging.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Start with a bundle of light rays, each labeled by its position (three numbers) and direction (three more). The rays move through space-time following the geodesic equation: d²xᵃ/dλ² + Γᵃᵦᵧ (dxᵝ/dλ)(dxᵞ/dλ) = 0. Here λ marks progress along the ray; Γ symbols (called Christoffel symbols) encode the shape of space-time curvature—you calculate them from the metric gᵃᵦ, which is the rule that measures distances in bent space; dxᵃ/dλ points in the ray's direction. Now take two nearby rays in your bundle. The gap between them changes over time. How fast the gap grows or shrinks is ruled by the Riemann curvature tensor Rᵃᵦᵧᵨ—it measures how deeply space-time twists and bends. The expansion θ (how fast the bundle's area shrinks) changes according to dθ/dλ = −Rᵃᵦ kᵃ kᵇ/2, where kᵃ is the ray direction and Rᵃᵦ = Rᶜᵃᶜᵦ is the Ricci curvature. The Ricci tensor tells you which directions in space-time are bent most sharply. In vacuum far from matter, Rᵃᵦ = 0 (Einstein's equation with no mass present), but the full Riemann tensor remains nonzero, so light bundles still converge and diverge—curvature shapes them.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

General relativity rests on one claim: the shape of space and time is gravity. There is no separate gravity force. To see how the theory actually works, you must understand how families of light paths behave in bent geometry. Light is your only tool to test whether space-time is truly curved, or whether it just looks different from one coordinate system to another. If you reduce lensing to single-ray deflections, you have missed the core truth: gravity is not a force, it is geometry. The bundle picture proves this. Light congruences show you that geometry and observation are the same thing.

Where It Sits In The Course

Lensing connects three core pieces of general relativity: geodesics (how all objects move in bent space), the Einstein equation (how matter bends space), and concrete observation (what we see). Light bundles tie them together. Nearby ideas—the Riemann tensor and Ricci tensor that measure bending, the metric that measures distance in bent space, null geodesics that are light paths—all come together at lensing. Lensing is where abstract geometry becomes something you can touch: you measure light's focus and work backward to find the bending that caused it.

Connective Thread

Earlier sections show that light follows the straightest paths in bent space-time (null geodesics) and that the Einstein equation governs how matter bends space. This section shows why that matters: the machinery predicts which light paths converge and diverge, creating images and magnification that match what we observe. Sections that follow build on this: redshift (light stretches because space-time is bent), horizons (light paths are trapped and cannot escape), and gravitational waves (ripples of bending space-time that focus and unfocus light bundles in a detectable pattern). Without lensing, those topics would be isolated equations and isolated observations. With it, they form one connected story: the shape of space-time and what we observe are one thing.
