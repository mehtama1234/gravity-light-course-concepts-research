Topology and Continuity

You want to talk about curves being smooth, functions being continuous, and nearby points staying nearby under a transformation. But before you measure *how close* points are—before rulers and meters—you need a language for *which* points are near each other at all.

Naive Picture

Maybe points are near each other if they fit inside an imaginary ball of some fixed size, the way neighborhoods are bounded by streets. But that requires distance to be defined first.

Why It Fails

In relativity, there is no fixed distance rule. Light cones matter more than spheres. The theory must speak about continuity before it knows what distance means—or it has already snuck in an assumption about what 'close' means.

Mathematical Object

A topology is a specific collection of sets—the 'open' sets—chosen so that you can talk about neighborhoods without numbers. A set is open if, whenever a point is in it, you can draw an open neighborhood around that point that stays inside. The collection must be closed under unions and finite intersections, plus include the whole space and the empty set. That is the exact structure needed to talk about limits and continuity.

Operation

To test if a function is continuous, you ask: for every open set in the target space, is its pre-image (the set of starting points that map into it) also open in the source space? If yes, the function is continuous. This is coordinate-free—it works no matter how you label or measure points.

Why It Matters

Fields like the gravitational potential and the metric tensor must be continuous before we can differentiate them to find field equations. Light rays must be continuous curves before we can ask whether they accelerate or bend. Topology is the foundation that lets all these continuity claims make sense without assuming anything about distance.

What Breaks Without It

A formula written in coordinates can look smooth and well-behaved while hiding a jump when you change to different coordinates. You lose the ability to distinguish a genuine continuity break from an artifact of your choice of numbers. Field equations and conservation laws become ambiguous—you cannot tell whether a discontinuity is physical or just an illusion of poor labeling.

Mini Example

Imagine a weather map. Before the forecast gives you temperatures or wind speeds, you can already ask: does the cold region connect to the warm region, or is there a sharp boundary? You can trace a path from one region to another and check if it ever has to jump. You can define one region as 'open' (interior and boundary together are stable) and another as 'closed' (isolated from what is outside). None of that needs a thermometer—you only need to know which points are neighbors. That intuition is exactly what a topology captures.

Common Confusion

Many people think topology is vague or handwavy—'geometry without numbers.' It is the opposite. Topology is the exact *minimum* structure you need to make continuity and limits rigorous, and it deliberately refuses to commit to any particular distance rule. That refusal is its power. It lets the theory work on any manifold, whether curved or flat, without embedding it in a bigger space or assuming a metric from the start.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Here is the problem: you want to speak about curves that wiggle without jumping, and functions that keep nearby points nearby, but you haven't yet decided how to measure distance. It feels like you need rulers first. But that is backwards. In general relativity, the gravitational field *creates* the rulers. Before asking how fast anything changes, the theory must have a way to ask whether it changes at all—continuously or not. That means you need a prior structure that lets you say which points count as neighbors, without relying on any distance rule. That structure is a topology: a carefully chosen collection of open sets. Open sets let you define neighborhoods (a neighborhood of a point is any open set containing it) and limits (a sequence reaches a limit if all sufficiently-far-out terms fall into any open neighborhood of that limit). Once you have that, you can define continuity: a map is continuous if whenever you pick an open set in your destination, the set of starting points that land in it is also open in your source. This definition uses only the open sets—no distance, no coordinates. And that is why it matters for gravity. Fields and paths must be continuous before we can differentiate them. Light rays must be continuous curves. Observers must vary continuously. But none of that makes sense until the theory has a *topology*—the underlying structure that lets it even ask whether something is continuous.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

A topology on a set X is a collection T of subsets such that: the whole space X and the empty set are in T; any union of sets in T is also in T; the intersection of any *finite* number of sets in T is also in T. Given this T, a function f : X → Y (where Y has its own topology) is continuous if and only if for every open set U in Y, the pre-image f⁻¹(U) = {x ∈ X : f(x) ∈ U} is open in X. The inverse-image test works because it translates 'nearness in the target' to 'nearness in the source' using only open sets. Why finite intersections and arbitrary unions? Because a point is 'near' a subset if it lies in every open neighborhood of points in that subset—an intersection. But you don't need a point to be in *infinitely many* open sets; the finite case captures what matters. The condition builds just enough structure to talk about limits and continuity without smuggling in any distance metric.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

Without topology, you cannot separate a genuine physical discontinuity from a mere accident of how you drew your coordinate grid. A field equation derived in one coordinate system might look continuous; in another, it might appear to jump. The theory would be ambiguous about what is real and what is just a choice of labels. The entire edifice—derivatives, field equations, conservation laws, and the meaning of 'observer'—requires continuity to even be defined. Topology is the bedrock.

Where It Sits In The Course

Continuous maps (the pre-image test for open sets) and open sets (neighborhoods) are the two pieces of topology you use directly. Charts and atlases (maps from pieces of curved spaces to flat coordinate patches) are topologically defined structures. Manifolds rest on topology for their definition of nearness and continuity. Dual vectors (one-forms) are defined as continuous linear maps—you need topology to make 'continuous' rigorous.

Connective Thread

You begin here after learning that continuous maps must preserve the structure of open sets and that open sets are the right way to define neighborhoods. This page builds the formal concept. Later, charts and atlases will be smooth overlaps of topologically-open patches. Manifolds will be topological spaces with charts. And every derivative, metric, and field equation will assume continuity in this topological sense. The thread is: topology lets you talk about continuity; continuous charts build manifolds; manifolds carry metrics and curvature; curvature and metrics generate Einstein's equation. Without the first link, the entire chain has an unjustified gap.
