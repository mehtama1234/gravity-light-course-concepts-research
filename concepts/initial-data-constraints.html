Initial Data Must Satisfy Constraints

You want to predict how spacetime will change going forward. But here is the catch: not every picture of space you can draw right now is one that could actually exist at the start of a real spacetime. Before you let evolution equations work on a snapshot, something has to check whether that snapshot is allowed by Einstein's rules.

Naive Picture

In everyday mechanics, you pick where things are and how fast they are moving at one instant, then the equations tell you where everything goes next. A physicist is free to invent a starting snapshot and then let the machine run. Why not do the exact same thing for gravity?

Why It Fails

In Einstein's theory, the shape of space and the speed at which that shape is changing are stuck together—they are not independent choices. Einstein's ten equations actually hide four hidden rules that say: these two things must match each other at every moment, right now. If you invent a shape and a rate of change without making them match, you have created a picture that no real spacetime could ever show.

Mathematical Object

Initial data is two pieces stuck together: first, the shape of one thin slice of space at one moment (a spatial metric—the measuring rule for distances on that slice), and second, the squeeze-and-stretch of that space as it begins to change (extrinsic curvature—how much space is bending as time moves forward). These two pieces must pass a two-part check: the Hamiltonian constraint (linking space's bend to the energy sitting in it) and the momentum constraint (linking space's bend to the momentum sitting in it).

Operation

Before you feed a snapshot into the evolution equations, you run it through two tests. Does the shape of space and its rate of change satisfy the Hamiltonian constraint everywhere on that slice? Does it satisfy the momentum constraint everywhere? If both pass, the data is lawful and you may evolve. If either fails anywhere, that snapshot cannot be the start of any real spacetime.

Why It Matters

Einstein's theory says gravity is not a force—it is the shape of spacetime itself. That shape changes according to Einstein's equation. But Einstein's equation has a secret: four of its ten parts are not instructions for what happens next; they are rules that say what must be true right now, at this moment. Checking those rules before evolution makes sure your starting snapshot does not contradict Einstein's theory at its foundation.

What Breaks Without It

If you skip the constraint check and evolve a snapshot forward using only the six true evolution equations, you will compute an answer. But your answer obeys only six of Einstein's ten equations. The four constraint equations will be violated along your entire solution. You will have solved half the theory, not the full thing. Your answer describes fake physics.

Mini Example

Picture a tiny rubber sphere suspended in spacetime at one moment. You measure it: it bulges slightly. You measure its deformation rate: it is expanding. Before you claim this snapshot is real, you must ask: do this shape and this expansion rate pass the constraint equations? If the bulge is too big for the expansion, or if the expansion is too fast for the curvature, the constraint fails—the snapshot is forbidden, and no real spacetime could start this way. If both constraints hold, you may evolve it forward, and Einstein's six evolution equations will automatically obey all four constraints at every moment that follows.

Common Confusion

Many people think constraints are extra equations you use only in advanced numerical work, or something mathematically refined but not essential. That is backwards. Constraints are why Einstein's ten equations form a self-consistent theory. Four of those ten pieces are not about the future—they are about the present moment. They are already threaded through the theory. You cannot throw them away without wrecking the foundations.

First-Principles Walkthrough

Here is the puzzle that sits at the heart of spacetime: in everyday mechanics, you can freely choose a position and a velocity at one instant, and the laws of motion carry you forward from there. Relativity works differently. The shape of space at one moment and the speed at which that shape is changing are not free choices—they are locked together by Einstein's own equations, specifically by four out of the ten equations that look like they should tell you what happens next, but actually tell you what must be true right now. What you need to start any spacetime prediction is initial data: a spatial shape (the curved form of one thin slice at one moment) plus a spatial momentum density (how that shape is starting to squeeze and stretch). But you cannot just invent any pair you want. You must check two constraint equations: the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint. These are the locks built into the theory. They ask: given this spatial shape and this rate of change, do four of Einstein's equations actually hold? If yes, you may hand the data to the evolution equations. If no, the snapshot cannot exist in any real spacetime. Why does this matter? Because Einstein's equation is not just a simple list of ten equations. It has hidden structure. Four of those ten pieces are not evolution rules—they are consistency rules. They are already baked into the theory. If you ignore them, you get contradiction. Checking them before evolution is not a choice or a refinement—it is a requirement written into the physics itself.

Mathematical Detail In Plain Language

Einstein's equation consists of ten component equations, built from tensors (bookkeeping objects that hold related numbers and keep their meaning even when you change coordinate systems). When you split spacetime into slices of space stacked in time—a technique called the 3+1 split—something remarkable appears: four of those ten equations contain only space derivatives, never time derivatives. These four are the Hamiltonian constraint and the momentum constraint. They connect the curvature of space to the energy and momentum present. The other six equations are true evolution equations: they tell you how the spatial shape changes as time moves forward, given initial data that already satisfies the four constraints. In mathematical shorthand, the constraints say roughly that the divergence of the extrinsic-curvature tensor equals the momentum density, and the scalar curvature of space equals the energy density, up to factors of the gravitational constant G and the speed of light c. Without these four constraints holding on your starting slice, the evolution equations produce solutions to only six of Einstein's ten equations—not the complete theory.

Why This Cannot Be Skipped

This course walks you toward black holes, gravitational waves, and an expanding universe. Every one of those predictions comes from Einstein's full, complete equation. If you do not check constraints before evolution, your initial data might carry you into computing a solution that secretly breaks Einstein's theory. In numerical relativity—the computer simulations that predict black-hole collisions and gravitational-wave signals—bad initial data without constraint checking creates ghost solutions that appear reasonable but do not actually satisfy Einstein's equation. The constraints are not optional polish or advanced numerics. They are the hidden skeleton that makes Einstein's equation work as a single, self-consistent whole.

Where It Sits In The Course

This concept sits at a crossroads in the theory of gravity. It connects directly to the canonical formulation of general relativity, where the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints are the fundamental pillars. It also anchors numerical relativity: you cannot get physical solutions without enforcing constraints at each time step. And it is the key to cosmology: the constraint equations reduce to the Friedmann equations that describe an expanding universe, connecting the speed of expansion to the density of matter and radiation at each moment.

Connective Thread

You also sidestep a trap: later learning that spacetime can be any smooth shape you draw. It cannot. Spacetime must satisfy Einstein's equation, and that equation depends critically on constraints being true at every moment in time.
